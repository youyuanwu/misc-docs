Philosophy of Language

Philosophy of language asks how marks, sounds, and gestures come to mean anything; how words refer to things; how sentence meanings depend on their parts; how context affects what is said; and how speakers use language to communicate and act. These questions meet whenever someone understands a sentence they have never heard, refers to an absent object, says something false but meaningful, implies more than their words encode, or uses familiar words in a new setting.

No single relation called "meaning" answers all of them. A name may contribute an object to what a sentence says while presenting that object under a cognitively significant mode. A sentence may have truth-conditions while its utterance also carries an implication, presupposition, tone, or force. A speaker may use a sentence literally, ironically, deceptively, or fictionally without changing the language overnight. Philosophy of language therefore studies a connected set of relations among expressions, speakers, audiences, practices, and the world.

The section treats formal semantics as an integral part of the subject. Model-theoretic, intensional, typed, and dynamic methods make claims about composition, scope, reference, context, and truth precise. They do not replace the philosophical question what makes one interpretation rather than another the meaning of a public language. Formal semantics supplies models of linguistic content; metasemantics asks what fixes those models as correct for a community of speakers.

Status. Complete as planned. The four-page spine and all thematic folders are written, published, cross-linked, and validated.

Boundaries

Several neighbouring sections own parts of the terrain:

  • Logic develops formal languages, proof systems, interpretations, model theory, and metalogical results. This section uses that machinery to analyse natural-language meaning and explains its philosophical limits.
  • Intentionality and Aboutness asks how mental states represent. Here the focus is public language and communication. The two subjects constrain one another without being assumed to have the same source.
  • Propositions and Other Abstracta, Possible Worlds, and Fictional and Intentional Objects ask what candidate contents and referents are. This section asks what semantic work they perform.
  • Religious Language is a regional application involving analogy, verification, metaphor, and realism. The general theories belong here.
  • Linguistics, psychology, and communication science provide indispensable evidence, but the section is not a substitute for a grammar, a history of a language, or an experimental methods course.

Contents

The spine

  • What Is Philosophy of Language? - five central questions, basic distinctions, recurring test cases, and the relation among language, thought, and the world.
  • Semantics and Pragmatics - expression, sentence, utterance, and speaker meaning; context dependence, implicature, presupposition, and the disputed boundary between what is encoded and what is communicated.
  • Theories of Meaning: An Overview - Fregean sense and reference, truth-conditional semantics, use, inferential role, verification, causal-historical reference, externalism, and the role of formal semantics.
  • Method and Evidence in Philosophy of Language - judgments about cases, ordinary and attested use, corpora, experiments, cross-linguistic evidence, formal virtues, interpretation, and reflective equilibrium.

Meaning

  • What Is Meaning? - semantic value, composition, communication, and metasemantics; understanding, correctness, publicity, productivity, misrepresentation, and cognitive significance.
  • Sense and Reference - Frege's identity puzzle, modes of presentation, sentence reference, opacity, empty names, direct reference, and fine-grained content.
  • Truth-Conditional Semantics - truth-conditions, Tarski and Davidson, finite axiomatization, radical interpretation, context sensitivity, hyperintensionality, and non-declaratives.
  • Meaning as Use and Language-Games - language-games, forms of life, family resemblance, rule-following, private language, public standards, composition, and reference.
  • Intention and Convention - Gricean speaker meaning, reflexive intentions, Lewisian coordination, signaling systems, conventional meaning, circularity, and normativity.
  • Inferentialism - Sellars and the space of reasons, Brandomian commitments and entitlements, proof-theoretic meaning, material inference, holism, composition, reference, and semantic normativity.
  • Verificationism and Manifestation - the verification principle, operationalism, Dummett's manifestation argument, semantic anti-realism, proof-conditional meaning, and intuitionistic logic.
  • Meaning Scepticism and Rule-Following - Kripke's quus case, dispositions, inner instructions, primitive facts, the sceptical solution, community standards, and the distinction between Wittgenstein and "Kripkenstein."

  • Proper Names - Millianism, Fregean and cluster descriptivism, Kripke's arguments, rigidity, baptism, causal transmission, belief reports, and reference change.
  • Empty Names and Reference Failure - negative existentials, free logic, gappy propositions, Meinongianism, pretense, fictional artifacts, and metalinguistic analyses.
  • Natural-Kind Terms and Semantic Externalism - Twin Earth, samples, division of linguistic labour, social externalism, rigidity, necessary a posteriori identities, and scientific change.
  • Indexicals and Demonstratives - character, content, contexts, direct reference, demonstrative intention, the essential indexical, de se attitudes, and indexical shift.
  • Reference Indeterminacy - radical translation, inscrutability, proxy functions, model-theoretic permutation, causal and naturalness constraints, charity, and structuralism.

Recurring distinctions

The section will use the following terms consistently:

TermQuestion
SemanticsWhat stable content does an expression or construction contribute?
PragmaticsHow do context and communicative reasoning shape an utterance's interpretation?
MetasemanticsIn virtue of what do expressions have their semantic values?
ReferenceHow does an expression pick out an object, kind, property, or other value?
Truth-conditionWhat would the world have to be like for a sentence or proposition to be true?
Speaker meaningWhat does a speaker intend an audience to recognize by an utterance?
ForceWhat act is performed: assertion, question, command, promise, and so on?
EffectWhat does the utterance cause in an audience, whether intended or not?

These are distinctions among explanatory roles, not assumptions that language divides into perfectly sealed components. Much of the subject concerns where the boundaries should be drawn.
