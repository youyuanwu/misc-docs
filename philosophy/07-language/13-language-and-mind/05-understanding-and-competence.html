Understanding and Linguistic Competence

A competent speaker understands novel sentences, detects some errors, resolves context, and uses language across changing purposes. They normally cannot state the rules governing these abilities. Understanding therefore appears to be practical and tacit rather than explicit possession of a semantic theory.

The concept must also allow partial competence, misunderstanding, deference, and improvement. Treating competence as perfect mastery idealizes away ordinary speakers; treating it as any disposition erases error.

Tacit knowledge

A semantic theory may represent what a speaker tacitly knows:

  • lexical values;
  • composition rules;
  • contextual dependencies;
  • inferential patterns.

Tacit knowledge is not unconscious recitation of axioms. It is a structured capacity whose manifestations the theory explains.

The attribution is substantive only if the theory predicts systematic performance and learning.

Know-how

Understanding resembles knowing how to:

  • apply expressions;
  • interpret novel combinations;
  • paraphrase;
  • correct;
  • respond to reasons;
  • coordinate with others.

Know-how avoids intellectualist regress. A speaker need not interpret a rule before every use.

It must still explain representational content and why abilities share one meaning.

Dispositions

Competence is manifested dispositionally, but actual dispositions include mistakes, fatigue, distraction, and finite limits.

Idealization distinguishes competence from performance. The danger is circularity: "what the speaker would do if semantically competent" cannot define competence.

Independent constraints come from patterns across tasks, correction, generalization, and community practice.

Error

A competent speaker can:

  • misspeak;
  • hold false beliefs;
  • misremember a word;
  • make invalid inferences;
  • misunderstand a technical term.

Error is possible because competence supplies standards not identical with current performance.

Persistent central error can become partial competence or idiolect rather than a momentary mistake. Boundaries are graded.

Partial competence

Speakers use "elm," "arthritis," or "quark" while lacking expert criteria. They possess:

  • grammatical knowledge;
  • stereotype;
  • deferential intention;
  • enough inferential role for conversation.

Social externalism explains how public content can exceed individual mastery.

Interpretation

Understanding another speaker involves integrating:

  • conventional meaning;
  • context;
  • intention;
  • shared knowledge;
  • rationality;
  • world interaction.

Successful prediction alone is not necessarily understanding if achieved through brittle lookup. Counterfactual flexibility and error correction strengthen the attribution.

Translation and explanation

Ability to translate or explain is evidence but not necessary in every case. Children and fluent speakers often understand expressions they cannot define.

Definitions themselves rely on further understood language. Practical application and contrast can reveal more than verbal explanation.

Competence versus processing

A grammar or semantics describes structural knowledge; psycholinguistics studies how it is used under time and memory constraints.

The distinction should not isolate theory from evidence. Processing, acquisition, and breakdown can reveal which structures are psychologically real.

Assessment

Understanding is a family of integrated capacities constrained by public norms and world-directed success. It is neither:

  • explicit theoretical knowledge;
  • flawless performance;
  • bare behavioural disposition;
  • private association;
  • one benchmark score.

A strong attribution cites composition, generalization, reference, inference, context, repair, and participation in correction.
