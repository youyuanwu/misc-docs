Private Language and the Private-Language Argument

Someone can suffer silently, keep a secret diary, or invent a name for a feeling that is difficult to describe. None of these familiar possibilities immediately establishes the possibility of a private language in Wittgenstein's philosophical sense: a language whose meanings are supposedly fixed by what only its speaker can identify, independently of any shareable standards of use.

The private-language argument examines whether that proposal makes sense. Its central question is not whether experiences occur inside us, or whether other people can inspect them. It is how attending to an experience could establish a rule for a sign, rather than merely produce an association or an impression of understanding. What would distinguish using the sign correctly from its merely seeming correct?

Wittgenstein develops this question through connected discussions of naming, sensation, memory, pain, first-person authority, and rule-following. There is no universally accepted reconstruction of one conclusive argument. Some readers find a demonstration that necessarily private meanings are impossible; others find a diagnosis of a philosophical picture that never succeeds in specifying a genuine possibility.

The most defensible starting distinction is:

Privacy of experience does not by itself establish privacy of meaning.

This distinction does not eliminate experience. Nor does it establish that meaning is whatever a majority approves, that every meaningful statement must be verifiable, or that every language-user must presently have companions.

Reading guide

  • The target separates philosophical privacy from secrecy, solitude, and first-person access.
  • The diary and the memory issue develop the central challenge to private definition.
  • Pain language and the beetle explain the positive and negative lessons.
  • Solitary speakers, Kripke, and competing readings mark the interpretive disputes.
  • Objections and limits distinguish the argument's force from conclusions it does not establish.

What "private" means

Several different claims are easily conflated:

Kind of privacyExampleRelation to the argument
SecrecyA diary whose cipher has not been disclosedOthers could in principle learn the code.
Solitary useCalculating or speaking while aloneAn actual audience is unnecessary for ordinary language.
Personal vocabularyA nickname or abbreviation used by only one personIts use may still have intelligible standards.
First-person accessFeeling a pain without observing one's behaviourAn asymmetry of access does not settle how "pain" means anything.
Difficulty of expressionA feeling for which available words seem inadequateLimited vocabulary does not establish necessary incommunicability.
Unshared experienceNo one else has undergone this particular episodeSharing a concept need not involve undergoing the same episode.
Philosophically private meaningA sign defined solely through an intrinsically private act of acquaintanceThis is the disputed proposal.

A secret language is contingently inaccessible: a key, explanation, or history could make it intelligible. The proposed private language is necessarily inaccessible to others in the relevant respect. It is supposed to be understandable only through the speaker's direct access to private referents, not merely difficult to decode.

Its signs need not be invisible. A written "S" can be publicly observable while its alleged meaning is private. Conversely, silently thinking an ordinary English sentence does not make its meaning private. The location of a sign and the conditions of its meaning are different questions.

Likewise, the issue is not necessarily a complete language with a private grammar. A single purportedly private sensation-word can raise it. Embedding that word in public syntax does not automatically explain its allegedly private contribution.

Even the claim that sensations are private needs care. Other people can sometimes know that someone is in pain; they need not merely conjecture. That they do not ordinarily undergo the sufferer's episode is a different point. Wittgenstein questions philosophical inferences drawn from these differences, not the ordinary distinction between hurting and observing someone who hurts.

Why the idea is attractive

Meaning seems to begin with acquaintance

A tempting picture runs as follows:

  1. I encounter an experience directly.
  2. Its nature is immediately present to me.
  3. I attach a sign to it.
  4. The sign thereafter means that experience or experiences of that kind.
  5. Public communication translates between such independently meaningful inner vocabularies.

This picture promises an especially secure foundation for meaning. Public signs can be misheard and objects misidentified, but the present experience seems immediately available. Why should its name require anything beyond that acquaintance?

The difficulty concerns the transition from having something before the mind to having a standard for using a sign. Even complete certainty that something is occurring would not, without further explanation, establish what a word means.

Historical background

The proposal connects with several traditions without being identical to any one:

  • Cartesian foundationalism gives first-person awareness a privileged role in justification. Private-language considerations ask whether the concepts used in such awareness could themselves be established independently of public practices.
  • Lockean theories of ideas encourage the picture of words standing immediately for ideas in an individual's mind. The question is how those ideas acquire the identities and semantic roles that communication supposedly preserves.
  • Russell's acquaintance-based account of logically proper names supplies a particularly direct comparison: acquaintance with a particular is supposed to make possible an especially immediate form of naming.
  • Sense-datum theories can suggest that publicly meaningful descriptions ultimately rest on privately identifiable objects of experience.

These are motivations and points of pressure, not interchangeable doctrines. The argument does not refute everything Descartes, Locke, or Russell wrote, nor every theory involving mental representation. Its target is the idea that an inner object and an act of attending to it can, by themselves, supply meaning.

The textual setting

Here PI refers to the numbered remarks in Part I of Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations, first published in 1953. References use remark numbers rather than edition-dependent page numbers.

The familiar label "the private-language argument" can therefore mislead. The diary is not the entire discussion, and the discussion is not a detachable proof with premises announced in textbook order. Wittgenstein repeatedly voices a temptation, tries to give it substance, and examines what happens. A sentence spoken by the imagined interlocutor need not express Wittgenstein's own settled position.

The relation to the earlier rule-following remarks is particularly important. Readers disagree about whether the diary supplies a relatively local argument about sensation language or an application of a more general account of meaning and rules.

Ostensive definition and its background

An ostensive definition explains an expression by presenting a sample or pointing to something. Pointing to a red tile while introducing "red" can teach a colour word. But the same gesture could introduce the tile's name, its shape, its material, or the number of tiles.

The gesture succeeds when the learner has enough background to understand which role the expression is to occupy. This need not mean that the learner can already state a definition. Practical abilities to compare, sort, respond, and continue a task can provide the background.

Wittgenstein's point is not that pointing never works. It is that pointing is not a self-sufficient foundation for every other aspect of language.

Moving the pointing inward

Private ostension substitutes inward attention for an outward gesture:

I attend to this sensation and resolve to call it "S".

The substitution does not automatically answer the original questions. Is "S" a name for this particular episode, a quality, an intensity, a bodily location, or a recurring kind? What counts as another instance? What does using the word accomplish?

These questions do not demand an exhaustive verbal analysis before anyone can learn a word. They ask whether an intelligible use has been supplied at all. Attention may help someone acquire a term within an existing practice without independently creating that practice.

Wittgenstein calls the role of an expression in such a practice its grammar. Here grammar concerns conditions of meaningful use, not merely word order or schoolroom rules. Calling something a name already places it within ways of identifying, reusing, explaining, and distinguishing expressions.

The sensation diary

The proposed experiment

In PI 258, a diarist proposes to record the recurrence of a sensation by writing "S". They cannot supply an ordinary explanation of the sensation-word. Instead, they focus attention on the sensation while associating the sign with it, intending to remember the connection.

The experiment is not simply an ordinary person recording headaches in shorthand. That would already supply a concept of headache, a purpose for the record, and ways of interpreting its entries. The philosophical proposal asks private attention to do the foundational work without relying on those resources.

For purposes of discussion, imagine the following sequence:

  1. On Monday, the diarist experiences something and writes "S".
  2. On Thursday, something seems familiar, and they write "S" again.
  3. Asked what makes Thursday's use correct, they appeal to remembering Monday's sensation and recognising its recurrence.
  4. Asked what counts as remembering the meaning correctly, they can offer only the present impression that the connection is right.

The challenge is not that Thursday's entry has been proved false. It is whether the description has established a rule under which either truth or error would be possible.

A reconstruction of the challenge

One influential, non-sceptical reconstruction is:

  1. The proposed diary is meant to record instances of a determinate sensation-kind, not merely to record the diarist's current willingness to write "S".
  2. Merely experiencing something while making a mark does not, by itself, specify a technique for using the mark.
  3. If each later impression both supplies the standard and counts as satisfying it, nothing distinguishes applying an established rule from replacing it with the current impression.
  4. The proposed private act has therefore not explained how "S" acquires the claimed rule-governed use.
  5. Adding an intelligible technique or role may give the sign a meaning, but then one must examine whether its meaning still rests on private ostension alone.

The conclusion is deliberately limited: the proposed explanation of meaning has failed. It is not a demonstration that the diarist feels nothing.

The disputed steps are substantive. A defender of private language may claim that acquaintance or a recognitional capacity supplies precisely the standard denied in steps 2 and 3. Simply repeating that correctness requires a criterion does not settle whether such a capacity qualifies.

Establishing meaning, remembering meaning, and applying it

Three questions must remain separate:

  • Establishment: What made "S" mean anything in the first place?
  • Retention: What would count as remembering that meaning?
  • Application: Given an established meaning, does this occasion satisfy it?

A criticism of the first should not be presented as merely a difficulty with the third. Nor should a response assume an established private meaning while claiming to explain how that meaning arose.

There is also a difference between understanding a word and making a true statement with it. Someone can understand "headache" and make a false diary entry through deception or mistake. The argument does not identify meaningfulness with the truth of every use.

Why this is not simply memory scepticism

A common summary says that the diarist cannot be sure they remember the original sensation correctly, so their word is meaningless. Taken literally, that argument is too quick. Public speakers also rely on memory, and knowledge does not ordinarily require an infallible guarantee.

The stronger challenge is constitutive rather than merely epistemic:

  • An epistemic question asks how I know whether I have met an existing standard.
  • A constitutive question asks what makes this the standard I am trying to meet.

Uncertainty about an answer presupposes a meaningful question. Wittgenstein's challenge concerns whether private ostension has supplied the question's terms.

Remembering a sample is not automatically a standard

Suppose I remember the shade of a painted door. The remembered image can help me choose paint because colour comparison already has an established role. The memory can be accurate or inaccurate within that practice.

In the private-language case, invoking a remembered sensation does not by itself establish that a determinate naming rule was instituted earlier. A memory cannot give an earlier event a semantic status that the account has not yet explained.

PI 265 considers an imagined table used to justify another recollection. Its relevance is the difference between an independent check and a restatement of the very impression being checked. Repeating the same report does not provide a second source of support. This is not a general ban on using memory to check memory.

Independence is not infallibility

An independent standard need not be:

  • available to a second observer at every moment;
  • immune to every possible doubt;
  • a physical object rather than a learned technique;
  • capable of resolving every borderline case;
  • actually consulted before every correct use.

For example, an established measuring procedure can distinguish a correct measurement from an error even when the evidence for one past measurement has been lost. The possibility of meaningful but presently uncheckable claims is not in question.

Nor does recruiting another person magically remove dependence on memory. A community whose only procedure were to endorse whatever presently felt right would inherit the problem. The issue concerns the role of standards, not the number of people reporting confidence.

From a private mark to a useful sign

PI 270 introduces a revealing variation. Suppose the sensation associated with "S" regularly accompanies a rise in blood pressure, and readings from an instrument confirm the connection. The diarist can now use their sensation to predict a publicly measurable condition.

This gives the sign a practical role: it enters expectations, comparisons, and assessments of success. But what has been secured?

  • The person may have acquired a useful way of recognising a bodily condition.
  • The use can be explained and investigated without another person undergoing the same sensation.
  • The success does not establish that an independently private qualitative identity was what originally fixed the sign's meaning.

For the newly specified predictive role, an undetectable change in the supposedly private quality would not automatically change the standard of successful use. The work is being done by the technique and its connections, not by a hidden sample whose identity alone legislates correct application.

This does not make a blood-pressure reading the definition of pain or of every sensation. It tests one proposed way of giving "S" a use. A scientific correlation is not automatically a semantic definition.

Ordinary sensation diaries are therefore not counterexamples merely because they are personal. Someone can record unfamiliar discomfort, compare intensity over time, learn new descriptions, and revise earlier classifications. Their reports need not be externally verifiable in every detail to belong to an intelligible practice.

Pain language, criteria, and avowals

The discussion is not only negative. Wittgenstein offers an alternative to the picture in which a child first privately identifies an inner object and then discovers which public sound names it.

Learning an expression

PI 244 connects pain language with natural expressions such as crying. Adults respond to a child's distress, teach words, ask questions, offer comfort, and distinguish different circumstances. Verbal expression can take over roles previously occupied by non-verbal expression.

This is not a universal developmental timetable or a claim that everyone learns in exactly the same way. It illustrates how a sensation-word can acquire a place in life without requiring an antecedent private definition.

It also does not imply that "pain" means "crying". Crying may express pain; the word concerns the pain, not simply the sound. Mature language supports distinctions between hurting, pretending, concealing, remembering, fearing, and describing pain.

Criteria and symptoms

In this context, a criterion belongs to the ways a concept is applied and understood. A symptom is evidence connected with a condition through an empirical correlation. The distinction concerns roles, not two permanently separate lists of observable events.

For example, distress, circumstances, and avowals help give pain-talk its familiar place. A newly discovered physiological marker might then supply additional evidence for pain. Discovering that correlation presupposes enough independent understanding of pain to identify what the marker is evidence for.

Criteria are not a mechanical checklist guaranteeing truth. People can feign pain, hide it, misunderstand a question, or express it atypically. How precisely to explain the defeasibility of criteria is itself an interpretive dispute. What matters here is that criteria are neither merely arbitrary associations nor an infallible window into someone's experience.

Public criteria also need not mean publicly measurable bodily movements alone. Testimony, context, learned distinctions, and patterns of interaction matter. Another person's avowal is part of the evidence; it is not automatically inferior to an observer's preferred behavioural indicator.

First-person authority

An avowal is a first-person expression such as "I am in pain". In its ordinary present-tense use, it need not result from inspecting an inner object or inferring pain from one's own behaviour. It can express the pain while also informing someone.

This helps explain an asymmetry:

  • The sufferer ordinarily does not need evidence that they are hurting.
  • Someone else may recognise their pain through expression, testimony, and context.
  • Both can use the same concept without acquiring it through the same epistemic route.

In PI 246, Wittgenstein questions the picture that only the sufferer knows while everyone else merely guesses. He also questions treating the sufferer's ordinary avowal as if it were the conclusion of an exceptionally certain observation.

These remarks should not be flattened into either "introspection is infallible" or "first-person reports are just guesses". Authority concerning current pain, memory of past pain, classification of a complex emotion, and knowledge of a state's cause are different matters. A mistaken diagnosis does not by itself discredit an avowal of suffering.

The broader account belongs in Self-Knowledge and Introspection.

The beetle in the box

PI 293 asks us to imagine people who each possess a box containing what they call a "beetle". No one can inspect anyone else's box. Each person claims to understand the word by examining their own contents.

Now suppose the contents differ between people, change over time, or are absent from some boxes. If these differences have no bearing on the established public use of "beetle", the private contents cannot perform the role assigned to them in an explanation of that use.

The comparison exposes a problem with the model:

Each person privately identifies an object; the public word means that object; shared understanding consists in independently repeating this private identification.

If what makes the object the relevant kind is itself intelligible only inside each box, the model has not explained how the word has a shared rule of application. If a shared practice already explains the word, the allegedly self-interpreting private object is no longer doing the foundational work.

What drops out, and what does not

The lesson concerns the private object's assigned semantic role, not the existence or importance of experience. It does not follow that:

  • there are no pains;
  • people are indifferent to suffering;
  • all experiences are qualitatively alike;
  • pain is identical with pain behaviour;
  • a person who fails to express pain has none.

PI 304 resists precisely the forced choice between treating sensation as an object of the imagined kind and treating it as nothing at all.

Nor is the beetle comparison a general proof that unobserved objects cannot be referents. Many ordinary and scientific expressions refer to things their users have never inspected. The question is whether the stipulated private item explains the use of the expression, not whether invisibility by itself prevents reference.

The diary and beetle cases thus address related but distinguishable questions. The diary challenges the establishment of a private sign's rule; the beetle challenges a picture of how private objects supposedly underwrite shared sensation language.

Rule-following and normativity

A word can apply correctly or incorrectly. This makes its use normative in a minimal sense: a description of what a speaker happens to do is not yet a complete account of what counts as using the word according to its meaning.

An appeal to an inner instruction does not automatically close the gap. If every instruction guides only through a further interpretation, that interpretation will need another, and so on. The regress relocates understanding instead of explaining it.

PI 198-202 connects rule-following with practice. Grasping a rule can be manifested in applying it, correcting mistakes, explaining examples, and continuing a technique. It need not consist in consulting a final, self-interpreting mental formula.

The private-language case concentrates this general problem. Saying "I mean the same sensation as before" does not help unless "same", "sensation", and the sign's proposed role already have a determinate application. An inner act is not exempt from the question simply because it occurs privately.

Two cautions are essential:

  1. Regularity is not yet rule-following. A device can behave regularly without understanding or following a linguistic rule. A practice-based account must explain the distinction, not merely rename repeated behaviour.
  2. Semantic correctness is not a universal moral obligation. Someone may understand a word while lying, joking, or deliberately departing from a convention. The issue is what the expression means, not whether every utterance is sincere or advisable.

For the wider issues, see Rule-Following and Private Language and The Normativity of Meaning.

Solitary language and the community question

Does rejecting private language establish that language must be social? That depends on what "social" requires.

Three different solitary speakers

CaseWhat needs explaining
A previously socialised person now living aloneHow an already acquired language continues in solitary use.
A person isolated from birth who develops stable techniquesWhether an individual could originate linguistic standards without social learning.
A supposed private linguist whose present impression is the only standardWhether a rule has been specified at all.

The first is not normally controversial. A castaway can calculate, keep records, recognise mistakes, and use words meaningfully without a current audience. Actual Robinson Crusoe had a social history.

The second, often called the lifelong Crusoe case, is harder. Imagine a person who sorts objects, marks seasons, preserves records, and revises predictions. Are these activities enough to constitute rule-governed language, or do we understand them as linguistic only by importing a background of social instruction and correction?

The third is the central philosophical target. It must not be identified with the first two merely because all involve one person.

Stronger and weaker social requirements

On a community view, shared practices of instruction, correction, and agreement are constitutive of linguistic standards. An individual considered wholly apart from such practices cannot provide the required contrast between seeming and being right. Norman Malcolm and, in a distinctive way, Kripke defend influential social readings.

Other interpreters, including Baker and Hacker, deny that actual participation in a community is a necessary condition of every possible case of rule-following. A solitary person's relations to an enduring environment and their own organised techniques may supply standards not identical with present inclination.

Some accounts also distinguish a socially constituted paradigm from exceptional cases intelligible by comparison with it. The choice need not be between ignoring social life and imposing a literal minimum number of speakers.

The phrase public in principle is useful but not a solution by itself. It should not mean that every stranger must be able to decode every utterance, or that every judgment must be independently testable. It expresses the thought that the standard has an intelligible role beyond a private act of endorsement. Whether that role requires an actual community remains disputed.

Consequently, a careful account should not present the impossibility of all solitary languages as an uncontested result of PI 258.

Kripke's sceptical interpretation

Saul Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language (1982) made the discussion central to a broader debate about meaning. His reconstruction is often called Kripkenstein to distinguish its philosophical argument from an uncontested account of Wittgenstein's own position.

From sensations to every rule

Suppose all my past additions involved relatively small numbers. What fact makes it true that I meant ordinary addition rather than a deviant function that agrees with every previous answer but diverges on a new case?

Kripke's quus example makes this vivid: an alternative operation can agree with the relevant past calculations while yielding 5 rather than 125 for 68 plus 57. Past performances alone do not select the intended rule.

The sceptic then challenges proposed additions to the account: dispositions can include mistakes; mental instructions require interpretation; an inner feeling of understanding does not specify indefinitely many correct future applications. The challenge is not just that outsiders lack evidence. It asks what fact constitutes the meaning at all.

A sceptical solution

A straight solution would identify a fact that answers the sceptic on the sceptic's terms. Kripke's sceptical solution instead examines the circumstances in which a community is entitled to attribute understanding: training, agreement, correction, and integration into shared practices.

On this reading, meaning-attributions are vindicated through their assertibility conditions and role in communal life, rather than through the kind of individual meaning-fact demanded by the sceptic. The private-language conclusion becomes a consequence of the more general account.

This is not simply the claim that a majority vote makes arithmetic true. Nevertheless, it faces questions about collective error, dissent, innovation, and whether communal dispositions can supply what individual dispositions allegedly lack.

Why the interpretation is disputed

Many readers argue that Wittgenstein diagnoses a misunderstanding in PI 201 rather than endorsing global scepticism about meaning. On such readings, ordinary rules and meaningful statements do not need replacement by an alternative semantics; the mistaken demand for an interpretation that supplies its own application needs to be abandoned.

The diary discussion can also be read as challenging a particular foundational picture without first accepting that there are no facts about meaning.

Evaluating Kripke's argument and interpreting Wittgenstein are therefore distinct projects. The detailed sceptical argument belongs in Meaning Scepticism and Rule-Following.

Competing readings of the argument

The main interpretive tendencies can be distinguished without treating them as wholly exclusive schools:

ReadingCentral questionMain point of contention
Memory and checkingHow could the diarist check later recognition against the original sample?If it demands certainty, why does it not undermine public language too?
Grammatical or normativeWhat establishes a rule, rather than an impression of following one?Can private acquaintance or recognition supply a genuine standard?
Community-basedHow do shared practices constitute correctness?Is an actual community necessary, and how is collective error preserved?

A grammatical reading may conclude that the proposal violates conditions of meaningful use. A therapeutic reading may resist presenting those conditions as a philosophical theory. It asks the proponent to explain the imagined activity and argues that the description either returns to familiar language or fails to specify anything.

This affects what "impossible" means. It need not describe a coherent invention that humans happen to lack the power to construct. It may mark a failure to give the proposed invention a determinate sense.

The categories also cut across one another. Rejecting a memory-sceptical argument does not settle the community question. Accepting a role for public criteria does not commit one to Kripke's global sceptical reconstruction.

Objections and replies

1. Why cannot direct acquaintance fix the meaning?

Objection: The sensation has a determinate nature whether or not anyone else can recognise it. I can name that nature directly and later recognise it. Requiring a public criterion seems to confuse having a meaning with explaining it to others.

Reply: The challenge is not simply that the explanation cannot be communicated. It is that encountering a determinate item does not by itself determine which feature, classification, or future use a sign is to have. The account must explain the move from acquaintance to a rule.

Remaining issue: A realist may regard acquaintance plus a recognitional capacity, or an irreducible semantic relation, as enough. The argument cannot defeat that position just by stipulating that all genuine criteria must already be public. Its force depends on the criticism of the proposed act's semantic role.

2. Could reliable memory or a reliable mechanism suffice?

Objection: The diarist might reliably detect one sensation-kind. Why demand additional checks? A thermometer can track a condition without consulting a community.

Reply: Reliability presupposes a condition relative to which performance is successful. A causal association between sensation and mark is not yet an account of why this condition, rather than another correlated feature, is what the mark means. Actual dispositions also include errors.

Remaining issue: Causal, functional, or teleological theories of content attempt to provide that additional account. Private-language considerations create an explanatory demand for such theories; they do not refute every possible naturalistic account of meaning. Nor does private access alone rule out reliable recognition.

3. Can something be meaningful without being verifiable?

Objection: A past event can be determinate even if all evidence has vanished. Likewise, a private sensation-word might have a meaning that nobody else can check.

Reply: The stronger reconstruction accepts the distinction between truth and our ability to establish it. It questions the institution of the supposed private meaning, not the legitimacy of every currently unverifiable claim.

Remaining issue: If a version of the argument simply infers meaninglessness from uncheckability, it requires a controversial verificationist premise. That premise should be defended explicitly, not smuggled into the word "criterion".

4. Does public language face exactly the same regress?

Objection: Public definitions use other words; public corrections can be mistaken; communities also rely on memory. Appealing to them appears to move the problem rather than solve it.

Reply: A practice-based account does not propose an endless sequence of explicit definitions. It points to capacities, activities, and standards within which explanations and corrections operate. Not every act of understanding is another act of interpretation.

Remaining issue: The defender must show how these practices preserve normativity rather than merely redescribe agreement. A community can miscalculate by its own procedures, overlook evidence, or misapply a distinction. Its current consensus cannot be made infallible without recreating the original problem collectively.

5. What about an entirely new or unrepeatable sensation?

Objection: I can experience something for which no existing word is adequate. I might introduce a name before anyone else understands it, or name an episode that will never recur.

Reply: Novel words and singular reference are not excluded. One can locate an episode in time, contrast its features, associate it with circumstances, or develop ways of recognising similar experiences. The relevant repeatability concerns the technique of using the sign, not a requirement that every referent recur.

Remaining issue: The distinction between an emerging personal vocabulary and a necessarily private definition must be argued case by case. An inability to describe a feeling fluently is not evidence that the speaker has failed to mean anything.

6. What about "this feeling" or a private phenomenal concept?

Objection: While having an experience, I can think "this feeling". Perhaps the demonstrative or recognitional concept refers directly to its phenomenal character even though others cannot acquire it in the same way.

Reply: An ordinary demonstrative already belongs to practices of reference, attention, contrast, and reuse. Its referent may be accessible to its user in a distinctive way without its entire semantic role being privately constituted.

Remaining issue: Public grammatical form alone does not settle whether a concept has a private component. Contemporary theories of phenomenal concepts propose different relations among acquaintance, recognition, reference, and public description. Whether particular theories reproduce the targeted private-object picture requires further argument, not an automatic appeal to Wittgenstein.

7. Does the argument deny pain to infants, animals, or silent people?

Objection: If sensation concepts require linguistic practices, it seems that beings without language cannot have sensations, and people without observable expressions cannot suffer.

Reply: Having an experience is different from possessing a concept of it, and both are different from explaining a word's meaning. An infant can be in pain without being able to define or use "pain". A person can suppress expression or be unable to communicate.

Remaining issue: Accounts of criteria must accommodate atypical expression and limits on observers' knowledge. The argument supplies no licence to treat missing behavioural evidence as proof of absent experience.

Qualia, inverted spectra, and ineffability

Shared words and unshared qualities

An inverted-spectrum scenario imagines two people whose colour discriminations and linguistic uses agree while their experiences differ systematically. Both call ripe tomatoes red, but what seeing red is like for one allegedly resembles what seeing green is like for the other.

The possibility of a complete inversion is disputed. It requires specifying qualitative identity while preserving the relevant similarities, contrasts, memories, and dispositions. One cannot settle that dispute merely by asserting that experience is private.

If a coherent inversion were possible, it would not immediately establish a private language. Shared colour words could retain their use despite the qualitative difference. Conversely, rejecting private ostensive definitions does not by itself prove that complete functional or linguistic agreement guarantees identical experience.

PI 272 and the surrounding remarks examine the temptation to assign private experience a role that makes no difference to the use of sensation-words. How far this undermines modern qualia theories is disputed; it should not be treated as a settled theorem about every conceivable inversion.

Ineffability is not one claim

Several limitations can be called ineffability:

  • I lack the vocabulary to describe a feeling.
  • A description does not enable someone to imagine it accurately.
  • Someone must undergo an experience to acquire a particular recognitional ability.
  • No possible linguistic practice could give the alleged private quality any shared role.

The first three do not automatically entail the fourth. Describing an experience is not the same activity as producing it in someone else. A meaningful description may remain incomplete, and acquiring an experience may transform understanding without revealing a wholly private language.

Similarly, the claim that there is something it is like to suffer does not by itself commit one to intrinsically self-identifying mental objects. A theory can affirm phenomenal character while rejecting that account of its semantic role.

For the broader metaphysical debates, see Qualia, Subjectivity, and What-It-Is-Like.

Inner speech, mental representation, and scientific inquiry

Inner speech normally employs resources acquired in ordinary language. Its current inaccessibility to an audience does not make it private in the philosophical sense. One can silently correct an inference or search for the right word.

A language of thought is also not automatically the targeted private language. In cognitive science, an internal representational system may have combinatorial structure and world-directed content explained through functional, causal, or computational relations. It need not consist of conscious sensations privately named by their owner.

Private-language considerations do challenge an explanation that treats the intrinsic appearance of an inner symbol as sufficient to determine its meaning. They do not, without further argument, disprove internal representations, non-linguistic thought, or computational models. Those distinctions are developed in Thought and Language.

The same caution applies to scientific investigation of experience:

  • Introspective reports can be informative without being infallible.
  • Comparing reports can refine distinctions without making the experiences identical.
  • Physiological measurements can supplement testimony without becoming the definition of what a person feels.
  • A failure to decode an experience does not establish that it is necessarily inaccessible in the philosophical sense.
  • Successful prediction of a report does not mean that the observer undergoes the subject's experience.

The argument concerns the intelligibility and grounding of descriptions. It is not a substitute for empirical work on perception, memory, pain, or language acquisition.

What follows, and what does not

Proposed lessonAssessment
Having an experience is not the same as establishing a word's meaningCentral to the challenge.
Mere inward attention cannot be assumed to provide its own rule of applicationA principal conclusion of the grammatical reconstruction.
First-person and third-person uses can share a concept despite different routes of accessIllustrated by ordinary sensation language.
Every meaningful statement must be independently verifiableNot established; this adds a verificationist premise.
A language must always have at least two actual speakersDisputed, and not a consequence of the diary alone.
Whatever a community agrees on is correctNot established; collective error remains possible.
Pain is just behaviour, or experiences do not existNot established and contrary to the intended distinction between experience and its philosophical misdescription.
Qualia, dualism, or all mental representations have been refutedMuch stronger claims requiring additional arguments.
Other minds and the external world have been proved to existNot established by a criticism of private semantic foundations.

The discussion can nevertheless affect larger philosophical projects.

For foundational epistemology, it challenges the assumption that a self-contained private vocabulary is already available before questions about the world and other people arise. For the problem of other minds, it questions the picture in which one first gives mental terms meaning exclusively from one's own case and then struggles to extend them to anyone else.

For theories of meaning, it makes the difference between reference, association, recognition, and norm-governed use unavoidable. For philosophy of mind, it separates the reality of experience from a particular account of how experience determines concepts.

None of these consequences amounts to a complete theory of mind or language. The argument is most powerful when directed at a specified explanatory model, not used as a slogan against every appeal to subjectivity.

Assessment

A balanced treatment preserves five distinctions:

  1. Private experience versus private meaning: undergoing an episode alone does not make the standards of describing it necessarily private.
  2. A standard versus certainty about meeting it: fallible application is not the same as having no rule.
  3. Association versus definition: a mental connection needs an intelligible role before it explains linguistic meaning.
  4. Solitude versus normative isolation: being alone does not entail that present inclination is one's only standard.
  5. Wittgenstein's discussion versus its reconstructions: the diary, the community view, therapeutic readings, and Kripke's scepticism should not be collapsed.

The strongest general lesson is not that inner life is unreal or that public agreement is infallible. It is that a private episode cannot simply be assigned the power to establish its own linguistic significance. An explanation must show what makes the sign a word with a use, rather than assume that the presence of experience has already done that work.

Whether this defeats every sophisticated proposal for private concepts remains controversial. It decisively identifies the question those proposals must answer.

Questions for study

  1. How does an ordinary symptom diary differ from the diary proposed in PI 258?
  2. What would count as an independent standard without demanding an infallible check?
  3. Does recognising a sensation explain a word's meaning, or presuppose enough of that meaning to make recognition relevant?
  4. Can a lifelong solitary speaker establish rules through interaction with an enduring environment? What would a community add?
  5. Why does the beetle comparison not entail that pain is unreal?
  6. Would an undetectable qualitative inversion change a public colour word's meaning? Which additional assumptions would an answer require?
  7. Can Kripke's sceptical argument be philosophically important even if it is not the best interpretation of Wittgenstein?

Sources and further reading

Primary texts

  • Ludwig Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations (1953). Read PI 243-271 alongside PI 26-38 and 198-202, then continue through PI 315. PI 258, 265, 270, 293, and 304 are landmarks, not substitutes for the surrounding discussion.
  • Saul A. Kripke, Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language (1982). The central source for the sceptical reconstruction and its communal solution. It should be evaluated as a philosophical argument as well as an interpretation.

Scholarly surveys

  • Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Private Language. Detailed treatment of the diary, the problems with memory-sceptical readings, the community debate, and Kripke. Its reconstruction is itself an argued interpretation.
  • Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Ludwig Wittgenstein, section 6: Rules and Private Language. An accessible account connecting the issue with rules, forms of life, solitary speakers, and the rejection of behaviourist readings. Compare its assessment of the community question with the Stanford survey rather than assuming a single consensus.

Further interpretation

  • P. M. S. Hacker, Wittgenstein: Meaning and Mind. An analytical commentary on the relevant part of the Investigations, useful for detailed questions about sensation, criteria, and the grammar of psychological concepts.
  • Marie McGinn, The Routledge Guidebook to Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations. A guide to the structure and philosophical method of the larger work, helping place private language within the account of meaning and understanding.
  • Stephen Mulhall, Wittgenstein's Private Language: Grammar, Nonsense, and Imagination in Philosophical Investigations, Sections 243-315 (2007). A sustained reading of the private-language discussion that foregrounds the question whether the proposed philosophical language has been given a coherent sense.
