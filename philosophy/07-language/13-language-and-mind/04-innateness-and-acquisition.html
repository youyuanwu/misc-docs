Innateness and Acquisition

Children acquire complex linguistic competence rapidly from finite, noisy, and socially embedded input. What must the learner bring to the task? Nativist theories posit language-specific innate structure; usage-based theories emphasize statistical learning, construction formation, cognition, and interaction.

Acquisition constrains theories of meaning because semantic competence must be learnable by actual finite agents.

Poverty of stimulus

A poverty-of-stimulus argument has this form:

  1. children acquire knowledge K;
  2. available input does not contain enough evidence for K under general learning;
  3. therefore learners possess relevant innate constraints.

The argument requires precise specification of:

  • acquired knowledge;
  • actual input;
  • plausible learning mechanisms;
  • alternative cues.

General claims that input is "poor" are insufficient.

Universal grammar

Universal Grammar posits innate constraints on possible human grammars. Versions range from rich language-specific principles to minimal computational operations plus general constraints.

UG explains convergence and restricted error. Its burden is biological plausibility, cross-linguistic diversity, and demonstration that domain-general learning cannot recover the patterns.

Statistical learning

Learners track:

  • transitional probabilities;
  • distributional classes;
  • co-occurrence;
  • prosody;
  • prediction errors.

Statistical learning explains segmentation and pattern discovery without explicit rule instruction.

Statistics require representational biases and structured input; they are not a theory-free alternative to innateness.

Construction grammar

Usage-based approaches learn pairings of form and function at multiple scales:

  • words;
  • idioms;
  • argument structures;
  • partially schematic constructions.

Abstraction emerges across exemplars. Productivity is graded rather than all-or-nothing.

The challenge is explaining rapid generalization and avoidance of unattested overgeneralizations.

Social-pragmatic learning

Joint attention, intention reading, imitation, correction, and conversational routines help identify reference and force.

Children do not learn from strings alone. They observe speakers acting in shared environments.

Social cues are themselves interpreted capacities, raising questions about their development and relation to general social cognition.

Meaning acquisition

Word learning must solve underdetermination: an ostensive episode supports many possible referents.

Constraints include:

  • mutual exclusivity;
  • whole-object bias;
  • syntactic bootstrapping;
  • pragmatic inference;
  • causal history;
  • category learning.

No single bias explains every lexical class.

Negative evidence

Children receive limited explicit correction of grammar, but absence, reformulation, prediction, and failed communication provide indirect negative evidence.

How much is available is empirical. The logical learnability of a class should not be inferred from an idealized corpus alone.

What acquisition establishes

Learning data can test:

  • whether semantic values are compositional;
  • which contextual cues children use;
  • how conventions become public;
  • whether inferential roles are learned together;
  • which generalizations are productive.

Acquisition does not by itself decide the metaphysics of meaning. An innate capacity can target socially fixed content, and learned representations can be externally individuated.

Assessment

A plural picture is likely:

biological biases+statistical learning+social interaction+general cognition+cultural transmission.

The controversy concerns the contribution and specificity of each component, not whether children possess either innate structure or learning.
