Thought and Language

Does language express thoughts that already exist, or does possessing a public language make distinctively human thought possible? The relation can run in either direction or be reciprocal.

A thought-first view explains communication as encoding prior content. A language-dependent view emphasizes public concepts, inference, self-reflection, and social learning. Evidence from infants, animals, imagery, and aphasia shows that some cognition is non-linguistic, while language plausibly transforms its structure and scope.

Thought before language

Infants and non-human animals can:

  • discriminate objects;
  • track number approximately;
  • navigate;
  • remember locations;
  • pursue goals;
  • predict agents.

These capacities support representational thought without public sentence use. Perceptual and motor cognition also operate faster than verbal articulation.

The conclusion is limited: some thought is non-linguistic, not that language never constitutes any thought.

Language of thought

Fodor's language-of-thought hypothesis proposes an internal combinatorial system, Mentalese. Public language expresses structures already available internally.

It explains:

  • productivity;
  • systematicity;
  • propositional attitudes;
  • compositional reasoning.

Mentalese need not resemble English or be conscious. Its content still requires grounding.

The cognitive architecture is developed in The Computational Theory of Mind.

Language-dependent thought

Public language can enable:

  • explicit logical reasoning;
  • exact large-number concepts;
  • recursive planning;
  • narrative self-description;
  • institutional categories;
  • reflection on reasons;
  • transmission of abstract knowledge.

Some concepts depend on linguistic practices and institutions. One cannot privately invent the full concept of a legal contract without normative surroundings.

Inner speech

Reasoning often uses internalized speech. Inner speech can aid:

  • working memory;
  • self-control;
  • planning;
  • metacognition;
  • rehearsal.

It is one cognitive tool, not the universal medium of thought. Visual, spatial, affective, and motor cognition may use different formats.

Concept possession

Strong linguistic views tie concept possession to mastery of inferential and public rules. This explains correction and rational commitment.

Weak views allow non-linguistic concepts while holding that words stabilize, differentiate, and make them available for reflection.

The dispute turns on criteria for concept possession: discrimination, flexible use, inference, or public justification.

Communication

If thought precedes language, communication maps internal contents to public signs. If thought is socially constituted, communication is partly the medium through which content exists.

A reciprocal model is plausible:

Assessment

The evidence rejects two extremes:

  • no thought without public words;
  • language is merely an inert label on fully formed cognition.

Non-linguistic representation precedes language, while public linguistic practice supports kinds of abstraction, norm-governed inference, and self-interpretation not explained by pre-linguistic capacities alone.
