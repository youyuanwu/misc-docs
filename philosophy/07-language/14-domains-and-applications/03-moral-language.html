Moral Language

"Cruelty is wrong" appears to state a truth, guide action, express disapproval, and invite disagreement. The semantics of moral language asks which of these functions is fundamental and how the others arise.

This is a gateway to metaethical questions, not a substitute for a future ethics or metaethics section.

Cognitivism

Cognitivism holds that moral sentences express truth-apt propositions. Realist versions make some objectively true; error theory says they are uniformly false because the required properties do not exist.

Cognitivism explains embedding and genuine disagreement naturally. It owes an account of moral properties, knowledge, and motivation.

Non-cognitivism

Non-cognitivism treats moral utterances primarily as expressions of attitudes, prescriptions, or plans rather than descriptions.

"Cruelty is wrong" may express disapproval and guide action.

The challenge is compositional embedding:

If cruelty is wrong, encouraging cruelty is wrong.

The embedded antecedent is not itself an act of disapproval, yet reasoning remains valid.

Expressivism and quasi-realism

Modern expressivism models attitudes with enough structure for consistency, conditionality, and disagreement. Quasi-realism explains realist-seeming truth and objectivity talk from within expressive practice.

Critics ask whether the theory reconstructs cognitivism rather than remains distinct.

Disagreement

Moral disagreement can involve:

  • conflicting beliefs;
  • conflicting attitudes;
  • different standards;
  • factual disagreement;
  • conceptual negotiation.

Persistence after factual agreement does not by itself show that no proposition is at issue.

Motivation

Moral judgment often motivates action more directly than ordinary factual belief. Expressivists treat this as evidence that attitudes are built into content.

Cognitivists can explain motivation through desires, practical identity, or rational norms. The psychological generalization has exceptions.

Thick terms

"Cruel," "courageous," and "generous" combine description and evaluation. Separating a neutral descriptive core from attitude can distort their use.

Multidimensional semantics, presupposition, and pragmatic stance are possible analyses.

Assessment

Moral language tests:

  • truth-aptness;
  • expressive content;
  • embedding;
  • disagreement;
  • action guidance;
  • concept revision.

The semantic evidence constrains metaethics without deciding realism independently of ontology and epistemology.
