Method and Evidence in Philosophy of Language

Philosophy of language draws on unusually varied evidence. A philosopher judges whether two sentences could differ in meaning, a linguist tests which readings a construction permits, a corpus records how expressions are used, an experiment measures interpretation under controlled conditions, a historian reconstructs a reference chain, and a formal semanticist compares models for compositional adequacy. Each source constrains theory. None by itself reveals what meaning is.

Two methodological errors sit at opposite extremes. Armchair isolation treats a small set of intuitions about invented English sentences as decisive and ignores variation among speakers, contexts, and languages. Uninterpreted empiricism treats counts or response times as though they arrived already classified as evidence about semantic content, pragmatic inference, processing difficulty, or social convention. A better method identifies the target claim, the observation, and the assumptions connecting them.

This page explains the principal sources of evidence and gives a common framework for using them. It presupposes the distinctions introduced in Semantics and Pragmatics.

Evidence is theory-mediated

Suppose speakers tend to interpret "Some students passed" as suggesting that not all passed. The observation is compatible with several explanations:

  1. "some" semantically means some but not all;
  2. "some" means at least one, while a conversational inference excludes all;
  3. the enriched reading is a learned default that can be overridden;
  4. the response reflects task design rather than ordinary interpretation.

The data do not label themselves. A disciplined inference has the form:

observation+bridge assumptions⟹claim about language.

The bridge may concern what a task measures, which alternatives speakers considered, whether context was sufficiently specified, or how syntax constrains composition. Making it explicit turns disagreement over "what the evidence says" into disagreement over premises that can be compared.

Judgments about cases

Philosophers and linguists routinely ask whether an expression is meaningful, whether a sentence can be true in a described case, what a pronoun can refer to, whether an inference is cancellable, or whether two utterances say the same thing. Such judgments are valuable because competent speakers are themselves sensitive to linguistic structure.

They are not infallible reports of semantic facts. A judgment can be influenced by:

  • ambiguity left unresolved by the prompt;
  • implausible or underspecified context;
  • processing difficulty;
  • theoretical training;
  • prescriptive norms;
  • dialect and idiolect;
  • demand characteristics; or
  • confusion between what is said and what is merely suggested.

The response should not be to discard judgments, since experiments also rely on participants' linguistic responses. It is to improve them: vary context, compare minimal pairs, separate different questions, test multiple speakers, and look for a stable pattern rather than a verdict from one sentence.

Ordinary use and attested examples

Appeals to "what we say" are stronger when they concern attested practice rather than a philosopher's memory of it. Dictionaries, recordings, transcripts, historical texts, and naturally occurring exchanges can reveal:

  • which uses actually occur;
  • how interpretations vary by setting;
  • when a construction emerged or changed;
  • whether an allegedly anomalous use is systematic; and
  • how speakers repair misunderstanding.

Attestation does not equal semantic correctness or theoretical importance. A frequent use may be an error, a quotation, a joke, or a performance tailored to a platform. Rare constructions may still reveal grammatical competence. Corpus evidence also inherits sampling biases: published writing, searchable websites, and recorded speech do not represent every community equally.

Ordinary-language evidence is most effective when paired with an explicit claim. If a theory predicts that a purported implication is cancellable, naturally occurring cancellations matter. If it predicts a reading is impossible, robust attestation of that reading is more damaging than mere frequency differences.

Corpora and quantitative patterns

A corpus permits reproducible searches across many tokens. It can show collocation, distribution across genres, changes over time, and associations between linguistic forms and contexts. Quantitative evidence helps correct selective examples and can generate cases a theory did not anticipate.

But corpus counts underdetermine interpretation. Strings do not arrive with their scope, referent, force, or irony marked. Annotation introduces theoretical judgment, and automated classification introduces a model with its own errors. Absence from a finite corpus is weak evidence of impossibility.

Useful practice includes:

  • publishing search and exclusion criteria;
  • inspecting concordance lines rather than relying on totals;
  • separating author, genre, period, and dialect;
  • checking annotation agreement;
  • distinguishing tokens from independent speakers or documents; and
  • reporting uncertainty instead of treating a count as a semantic law.

Experiments

Experimental methods can vary context while holding wording fixed, compare populations, measure reading time, track eye movements, elicit paraphrase, or ask participants to judge truth in a scenario. They are especially useful when a theory predicts graded, context-sensitive, or processing-dependent effects.

Cross-linguistic evidence

A theory of human language should not mistake properties of familiar European languages for universals. Languages vary in how they mark tense, evidential source, honorific status, definiteness, number, gender, topic, switch reference, and many other categories. Some meanings expressed lexically in one language are grammaticalized in another or left to context in a third.

Cross-linguistic comparison can reveal whether a proposed category is:

  • universal in function but variable in form;
  • one strategy among several;
  • an artifact of a translation;
  • specific to a language family; or
  • a theoretical distinction with no required surface marker.

Translation must not erase the phenomenon. Asking whether another language has "the word for" an English item is different from asking how its speakers perform the same referential, inferential, or communicative work. Reliable comparison requires descriptive grammars, consultation with speakers, and awareness of variation within a language.

Formal modelling

A formal model earns its place by making commitments explicit. It may specify semantic types, denotations, composition rules, possible circumstances, information states, or update operations. From those assumptions it derives predictions about truth, entailment, ambiguity, scope, anaphora, or discourse.

Three virtues are especially important:

  • coverage: the model handles the relevant constructions and readings;
  • constraint: it does not generate arbitrary unattested readings;
  • explanation: related phenomena follow from shared principles rather than separate stipulations.

Mathematical elegance is evidence of organization, not automatically evidence of linguistic truth. Two empirically equivalent formalisms may encode different explanations, and a powerful formalism can model many interpretations without showing which one speakers use. Model Theory explains satisfaction once an interpretation is given; metasemantics asks why that interpretation is the correct one.

Formalization is most useful when it exposes a choice that prose conceals: whether a description denotes an object or introduces quantificational structure, whether a presupposition is a definedness condition or a context requirement, or whether a contextual value occupies a syntactic position.

Interpretation and charity

Evidence about an unfamiliar speaker is behavioural and contextual: assent, dissent, action, correction, inference, and interaction with a shared environment. Radical interpretation asks how meanings and beliefs can be assigned together without first possessing a translation manual.

Principles of charity favour interpretations that make speakers broadly coherent and responsive to shared circumstances. Charity is not the assumption that speakers agree with the interpreter about everything. An assignment that made every utterance wildly false would undermine the evidence that the utterances have been translated correctly at all. Some shared pattern is a condition of locating disagreement.

The method can be overused. Too much charity risks defining deep conceptual disagreement out of existence, imposing the interpreter's logic, or ignoring historical and practical differences. Interpretation should balance coherence, causal contact, speakers' own corrections, successful coordination, and the possibility of error.

History and reference chains

Some semantic claims depend on diachronic facts. Causal-historical theories of names and natural-kind terms appeal to introductions, transmission, deference, and later uses. Evidence may include naming practices, scientific reclassification, borrowing between communities, and cases where associated descriptions changed while reference appeared stable.

Historical evidence can discriminate between a theory on which reference is fixed by current descriptions and one on which it is inherited through a practice. It also complicates simple chains: names split, merge, shift referents, are reintroduced independently, or persist through widespread error. A usable causal account must say which intentions and social relations preserve a chain.

Thought experiments

Twin Earth, radical translation, the private diarist, and other thought experiments isolate dependencies that ordinary cases entangle. Their conclusion is conditional: if the scenario is coherent and the judgment stable, then a proposed analysis faces a specified pressure.

Three questions should be asked:

  1. Coherence: does the scenario describe a genuine possibility, or conceal an inconsistent combination?
  2. Diagnosis: is the response semantic, pragmatic, metaphysical, or merely verbal?
  3. Robustness: does the judgment survive changes to irrelevant details and presentation?

Thought experiments are strongest when they organize subsequent evidence rather than replace it. Twin Earth, for example, directs attention to environmental dependence and division of linguistic labour; actual scientific and linguistic practices then show how such dependence works.

Reflective equilibrium

No single method has automatic priority. A good theory seeks reflective equilibrium among:

  • clear judgments about well-specified cases;
  • attested and cross-linguistic use;
  • experimental patterns;
  • compositional and explanatory virtues;
  • compatibility with plausible psychology;
  • historical facts about linguistic practices; and
  • fit with neighbouring theories of mind, logic, and ontology.

When these conflict, revision is local and explicit. A theorist may reject a judgment as a processing artifact, revise a formal rule, distinguish dialects, or conclude that an ordinary category combines several phenomena. The cost must be stated rather than hidden by redescribing the evidence.

A checklist for evaluating a claim

For any substantive claim about language, ask:

  1. What is the target: expression meaning, utterance content, implication, force, processing, or effect?
  2. What is the observation, and can it be reproduced or independently checked?
  3. Which bridge assumptions connect the observation to the target?
  4. What rival semantic, pragmatic, or psychological explanations remain?
  5. Does the account generalize beyond one construction, context, speaker group, and language?
  6. What does the formalization predict, forbid, or leave unspecified?
  7. Which result would count against the account?

This discipline does not eliminate philosophical disagreement. It makes clear whether the disagreement concerns data, interpretation, formal architecture, or the deeper question of what meaning consists in.
