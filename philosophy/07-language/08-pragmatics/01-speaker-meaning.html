Speaker Meaning

A speaker can communicate more than their sentence encodes. "The door is open" can be a request to close it, a warning that someone may enter, or an explanation of a draft. The conventional sentence meaning remains stable while what the speaker means varies with intention and context.

Grice explains speaker meaning through an overt communicative intention: a speaker intends an audience to recognize an intention, and intends that recognition to play a role in the audience's response. Communication is therefore neither mere coding nor mere causal influence.

The account illuminates hints, gestures, indirect speech, and implicature. It also faces hard cases involving open audiences, habitual communication, non-cooperation, failed uptake, and communication without fully articulated higher-order intentions.

Meaning and intentional influence

Not every intended effect is communication. Secretly placing an obstacle so someone turns left is intentional influence, but it does not communicate a request to turn. The agent succeeds only if the intention remains hidden.

Communication is characteristically overt. A pointing gesture works because the audience is meant to recognize the attempt to direct attention. A request works because the audience recognizes it as a reason-producing act.

A first approximation is:

S means that p by utterance u if S intends an audience A to respond to p partly by recognizing that very intention.

The reflexive structure distinguishes showing from covertly causing.

Informative and directive intentions

Different utterances aim at different responses.

  • An assertion may aim at belief, acceptance, or recognition of commitment.
  • A request may aim at action or formation of an intention.
  • A warning may aim at attention and precaution.
  • A question may aim at provision of information.

Reducing all speaker meaning to belief production is too narrow. A witness can assert for the record without expecting belief; a teacher can remind an audience of what it already knows.

The intended response is often uptake: recognition of the act and content, whether or not the audience complies or believes.

Reflexive intention

The audience must not merely infer that the speaker has an intention. Recognition is intended to contribute to fulfilment.

If Ada says "It is raining" intending Ben to take an umbrella, she may intend:

  1. Ben to recognize that she is informing or warning him;
  2. Ben to recognize that she wants this recognition to guide him;
  3. that recognition to provide a reason for precaution.

Nested intentions need not be consciously rehearsed. The analysis can specify the structure of an act implemented through practical social competence.

Too many levels risk regress and psychological implausibility. Later Gricean accounts therefore seek the minimal reflexive structure needed for overt communication.

Expression meaning and speaker meaning

Speaker meaning presupposes but can depart from conventional expression meaning.

Literal assertion

The meeting begins at noon.

The speaker means approximately what the sentence conventionally expresses.

Indirect communication

It is nearly noon.

In context, the speaker may mean that everyone should leave for the meeting.

Sarcasm

Excellent timing.

Said after a long delay, the speaker communicates criticism through recognizable departure from the literal evaluation.

Malapropism

A speaker selects the wrong word but their intended content remains recoverable.

These cases require both levels. Without stable expression meaning, there is no departure to recognize; without speaker meaning, literal content underdetermines the message.

Audience design

Speakers tailor utterances to beliefs about an audience:

  • shared knowledge;
  • vocabulary and expertise;
  • attentional state;
  • expected inferences;
  • relationship and politeness;
  • available perceptual context.

The same intention can be realized through different words for different audiences. Conversely, identical words can be designed to produce different interpretations.

Audience design supports an intention-based account but complicates it when audiences are plural. A public utterance may address:

  • a primary addressee;
  • overhearers;
  • future readers;
  • institutional audiences;
  • unintended but foreseeable recipients.

There may be no single response intended for all.

Open and future audiences

Books, laws, signs, recordings, and online posts persist beyond their authors' immediate plans. A safety sign communicates to indefinite future readers. Its function depends on convention and institutional practice more than on a detailed occurrent intention.

Possible responses:

  • posit standing or dispositional intentions;
  • appeal to intentions of designers or institutions;
  • let conventional force operate independently once established;
  • treat interpretation as constrained by publicly available purposes; or
  • distinguish historical speaker meaning from current utterance meaning.

These cases limit reduction of all communication to one individual's psychology.

Failed communication

A speaker can mean something without succeeding.

  • The audience does not hear.
  • A gesture is ambiguous.
  • The audience lacks background knowledge.
  • The speaker chooses the wrong word.
  • The audience recognizes the intention but rejects the request.

Failure distinguishes:

  • speaker meaning: what was overtly intended;
  • uptake: whether the act was recognized;
  • perlocutionary effect: what response occurred;
  • successful reference: whether the intended object was identified.

Communication theory should not define intention by successful outcome, or failed attempts become impossible by definition.

Covert influence and deception

Deception can be communicative or covert.

A lie is overt communication of content the speaker believes false. Success depends on the audience recognizing an assertion while not recognizing its insincerity.

Planting evidence can deceive without speaker meaning if the agent intends the audience to mistake the evidence for an unintended trace.

Manipulation can combine both: a speaker communicates one content while covertly exploiting framing or presupposition to shape another belief.

Gricean openness applies to the communicative intention, not to every ulterior motive.

Non-cooperative communication

Communication does not require shared interests. Opponents threaten, bargain, accuse, and interrogate successfully.

The relevant cooperation is often minimal:

  • enough shared language to recognize the act;
  • mutual expectations about rational interpretation;
  • common attention to a topic;
  • reliance on some conversational regularities.

A threat succeeds communicatively when its force and content are recognized, even though speaker and audience conflict.

This is why the Cooperative Principle should not be read as a moral claim that conversations are benevolent.

Conventional acts

Some acts depend on convention and authority beyond intention:

  • pronouncing a verdict;
  • naming a ship;
  • issuing an official order;
  • making a legal declaration.

A person may intend to marry two people by uttering the customary words but lack legal standing. Intention is insufficient.

Conversely, an authorized official can perform an act through a standardized procedure without a rich Gricean intention toward every affected person.

Speaker meaning explains communicative content; institutional felicity conditions help constitute the act.

Overgeneration

Suppose a speaker intends an audience to recognize an intention through an obviously contrived chain. Not every such plan counts as meaning.

Accounts constrain the analysis through:

  • audience-directedness;
  • recognizable rational connection;
  • absence of deception about the communicative intention;
  • conventional or contextual support;
  • intended uptake rather than arbitrary effect.

The more constraints added, the less purely reductive the analysis becomes. This may reflect the fact that communication is embedded in public practices.

Relation to convention

One-off speaker meaning can occur without established vocabulary, but ordinary communication exploits conventions. Expression meanings provide evidence from which audiences infer intentions.

A plausible division:

conventional content+context+recognizable intention⟹speaker meaning.

This is not a deterministic formula. Background assumptions and interpretive norms shape the inference.

Intention and Convention explains how stable linguistic practices and one-off intentions divide the work.

Assessment

Speaker-meaning theory explains why communication can be indirect, creative, and non-literal. Its strongest insight is that understanding an utterance involves recognizing an overt practical orientation, not merely decoding a string.

Its limits are equally important:

  • conventions cannot be reduced to one occasion's intention;
  • open audiences weaken detailed audience-directed analysis;
  • institutions can constitute acts independently of private intention;
  • failed uptake must remain possible;
  • non-cooperative communication retains only minimal coordination; and
  • psychological implementation need not explicitly represent every nested intention.

Speaker meaning is one layer between sentence content and audience effect, not a replacement for semantics or speech-act theory.
