Relevance and Neo-Gricean Theories

Grice's maxims explain pragmatic inference through rational expectations, but their number, formulation, and psychological role remain disputed. Later theories simplify the architecture or make default inferences more explicit.

Relevance theory proposes one overarching principle: cognition and communication seek worthwhile effects for reasonable processing effort. Neo-Gricean theories use smaller sets of principles governing informativeness, stereotypical interpretation, and marked expression. Both seek a better account of how hearers move from encoded meaning to intended interpretation.

Relevance theory

Relevance theory distinguishes:

  • cognitive effects: new conclusions, strengthened beliefs, contradiction or revision;
  • processing effort: resources required to obtain those effects.

An input is more relevant when it yields greater worthwhile effects for less effort. Communication creates a presumption of optimal relevance: the utterance is worth the audience's attention and is the most relevant compatible with the speaker's abilities and preferences.

Hearers follow a path of least effort, enriching and deriving implications until an adequately relevant interpretation is reached.

Explicature and implicature

Relevance theory uses explicature for an explicitly communicated proposition developed from decoded linguistic form through pragmatic processes.

Processes include:

  • reference resolution;
  • disambiguation;
  • saturation of contextual parameters;
  • enrichment;
  • lexical narrowing or broadening.

An implicature is a further communicated assumption or conclusion.

This makes pragmatics partly constitutive of truth-evaluable content, not merely a post-semantic layer. It therefore supports contextualism.

Loose talk, metaphor, and irony

"The bottle is empty" can be acceptable when negligible liquid remains. Lexical meaning is pragmatically loosened to a contextually adequate concept.

Metaphor involves constructing an occasion-specific concept that yields relevant implications. Irony is often analysed as echoic use: the speaker echoes a thought or expectation while expressing dissociative attitude.

This unified treatment handles continuities among literal approximation, hyperbole, metaphor, and irony. Critics worry that flexible occasion concepts weaken stable semantic constraints.

Horn's principles

Horn compresses Gricean reasoning into two pressures:

  • Q-principle: say as much as you can, given quality and relevance;
  • R-principle: say no more than you must, relying on the hearer to enrich.

Q supports upper-bounding inferences:

Some passed

suggests not all.

R supports stereotypical enrichment:

Ada broke a finger

normally suggests one of Ada's own fingers.

Communication balances speaker economy and hearer inference.

Levinson's heuristics

Levinson proposes generalized pragmatic defaults:

  • Q: what is not said is not the case;
  • I: what is simply described is stereotypically exemplified;
  • M: marked expressions indicate marked situations.

Compare:

Ada caused the car to stop.

The marked causative can suggest an indirect or unusual process, whereas "Ada stopped the car" suggests the normal method.

Defaults explain rapid interpretation without a full derivation on every occasion. They must remain cancellable and context-sensitive.

Default versus inference

Are generalized implicatures precomputed defaults or locally reasoned conclusions?

Default theories predict:

  • fast automatic enrichment;
  • later cancellation;
  • stable cross-context tendencies.

Contextual inference theories predict early sensitivity to QUD, alternatives, and speaker knowledge.

Experimental results are mixed and construction-specific. Processing cost does not map cleanly onto semantic status. A default can be implemented through learned predictive inference rather than a stored enriched meaning.

Alternatives

Scalar reasoning depends on which alternatives are active. For "some," "all" is lexically salient. Other alternatives are supplied by grammar, focus, or context.

Formal accounts use:

  • lexical scales;
  • focus alternatives;
  • grammatically generated replacements;
  • questions under discussion;
  • exhaustification operators.

The source of alternatives determines whether enrichment is semantic, grammatical, or pragmatic.

Assessment

The successor theories sharpen three questions:

  1. Which content is decoded and which inferred?
  2. Which inferences are default and which reconstructed from local purposes?
  3. How do processing constraints relate to normative interpretation?

Relevance is indispensable as an explanatory constraint, but too elastic if any successful interpretation is declared relevant after the fact. Defaults capture robust patterns, but not if exceptions require a new stored rule each time. Theories earn explanatory force by predicting alternatives, effort, cancellation, and contextual variation independently.
