Conversational Implicature

When a speaker says "Some students passed," listeners often infer that not all passed. When asked whether a colleague is a good philosopher, a referee who replies "Her handwriting is excellent" conveys a negative assessment without stating one.

These are conversational implicatures: contents communicated through rational inference from what was said, context, and expectations about conversation. They are not straightforward entailments or conventional meanings.

Grice's theory explains how speakers can deliberately exploit shared expectations. The central tests - calculability, cancellability, non-detachability, and reinforceability - diagnose pragmatic content but are defeasible rather than mechanical.

The Cooperative Principle

Grice proposes:

Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the exchange.

This Cooperative Principle is not a moral command. It is an interpretive presumption that contributions are relevantly connected to a recognizable exchange.

The principle is articulated through maxims.

Quantity

  • Make the contribution as informative as required.
  • Do not make it more informative than required.

Quality

  • Do not say what you believe false.
  • Do not say what lacks adequate evidence.

Relation

  • Be relevant.

Manner

  • Avoid obscurity and unnecessary ambiguity.
  • Be brief and orderly where appropriate.

Real conversations depart from these formulations. Their role is to make pragmatic reasoning explicit, not to supply etiquette.

Observance, violation, and flouting

A speaker can relate to a maxim in different ways.

  • Observe: comply straightforwardly.
  • Violate covertly: mislead while appearing compliant.
  • Opt out: explicitly decline to contribute.
  • Clash: one maxim cannot be satisfied without sacrificing another.
  • Flout: conspicuously fail to comply so the audience searches for an implicated content.

The handwriting response flouts Relation. Assuming the referee remains cooperative, the audience infers that no relevant praise of philosophical ability can be offered.

Implicature depends on recognizable flouting, not mere irrelevance.

Deriving an implicature

A simplified derivation:

  1. The speaker said p.
  2. They could be expected to follow conversational expectations.
  3. Saying p would be puzzling or insufficient unless they intended q.
  4. They have not opted out, and nothing blocks the inference.
  5. Therefore the speaker implicated q.

The content must be calculable in principle from accessible premises. Hearers need not consciously rehearse the derivation.

Background beliefs matter. The same sentence can implicate different things in different activities.

Scalar implicature

Lexical items form informational scales:

⟨all,some⟩,⟨and,or⟩,⟨must,may⟩.

If a speaker chooses a weaker item while a stronger relevant alternative was available, hearers may infer that the stronger claim is not assertible.

From:

Some students passed

infer:

The speaker is not in a position to assert that all passed.

In many contexts this strengthens to:

Not all passed.

The epistemically cautious inference and the strong negative inference should be distinguished. A speaker may lack knowledge whether all passed.

Cancellation

Conversational implicatures are normally cancellable:

Some students passed - indeed, all did.

The continuation is not a logical contradiction, though it may sound marked.

Cancellation can be:

  • explicit: directly denying the implication;
  • contextual: setting a context where it does not arise.

For example, if only some passing is required for a team award, "Some students passed" may be fully informative without suggesting not all.

Failure of smooth cancellation can result from conventionalization, discourse incoherence, or strong expectations; it does not alone prove semantic entailment.

Non-detachability

Many conversational implicatures depend on what is said rather than the exact wording. Replacing an expression with a synonym tends to preserve the implication.

Manner implicatures are exceptions. Saying:

Ada produced a series of sounds corresponding closely to the score

rather than "Ada sang the song" may imply an unusual or defective performance because of the marked wording.

Thus non-detachability is characteristic of content-based implicatures, not universal.

Reinforceability

An implicature can often be stated explicitly without redundancy:

Some, but not all, students passed.

The added phrase reinforces what was otherwise inferred.

Conventional content can also be emphasized, so reinforceability is not decisive. In combination with cancellation and contextual variability, it supports a pragmatic analysis.

Generalized and particularized implicature

A generalized conversational implicature arises across ordinary contexts without special background:

Ada entered a house.

often suggests it was not Ada's house.

A particularized implicature depends on a specific context. At a petrol station, "The garage around the corner is open" can implicate that petrol is available there.

The distinction is gradual. Repeated pragmatic inference can become default-like or conventionalized.

Conventional implicature

Some expressions contribute non-truth-conditional content conventionally. Compare:

Ada is poor but honest.

Ada is poor and honest.

They may have the same truth-conditions, while "but" conventionally signals contrast. The contribution is not calculated from a maxim on each occasion and is difficult to cancel while retaining the same use.

This is often called conventional implicature, though theories differ over whether it belongs to semantics, expressive meaning, or multidimensional content.

The category should not be confused with conversational implicature merely because both can leave at-issue truth conditions unchanged.

Embedded implicatures

Implicatures can appear inside larger constructions:

If some students passed, the instructor will be relieved.

Can "some" be locally strengthened to some but not all inside the antecedent? Embedded readings challenge a purely global post-utterance inference.

Responses include:

  • local pragmatic enrichment;
  • grammatical alternatives computed compositionally;
  • exhaustification operators in syntax;
  • context-sensitive strengthening.

Scalar implicature therefore lies near the semantics-pragmatics boundary. Its cancellability does not prove it is computed only after complete sentence meaning.

Implicature and irony

Irony can be analysed as overt dissociation from literal content:

What wonderful weather.

said during a storm.

The audience recognizes that a sincere literal assertion would violate Quality and infers a contrary or critical attitude.

Not all irony communicates the simple negation. Tone, echo, target, and evaluative stance matter. Relevance-theoretic and echoic accounts often handle these dimensions better than a single maxim violation.

Implicature under conflict

Adversarial speakers can exploit expectations without being cooperative in purpose. A negotiator withholds information, a propagandist frames alternatives, and a liar relies on Quality while violating it.

The Gricean framework requires only enough presumed rational coordination for interpretation. Deception works because audiences expect relevance and sincerity.

When trust collapses entirely, implicatures become unstable because the audience no longer knows which expectations the speaker intends to exploit.

Experimental and cross-linguistic evidence

Scalar inferences vary by:

  • context;
  • task;
  • processing time;
  • age;
  • scale;
  • grammatical position;
  • language.

This supports a pragmatic component while showing that "some means not all" is too simple. Some theories model the inference through grammar plus alternatives; others through general reasoning.

Evidence should distinguish availability, preferred interpretation, and processing cost. A truth-value judgment alone may conflate them.

Assessment

Conversational implicature explains how communication outruns encoded content while remaining systematic. Its core ingredients are:

what is said+alternatives+context+conversational expectations.

The classic diagnostics are clusters of evidence, not definitions. Difficult cases include embedded strengthening, conventionalized implications, irony, and adversarial speech. These do not erase the semantic-pragmatic distinction; they show that pragmatic reasoning can be structured, rapid, and partly grammar-sensitive.
