The Semantics-Pragmatics Boundary

Every theory agrees that words constrain interpretation and context matters. The dispute is where semantic determination ends and pragmatic contribution begins.

Minimalism restricts truth-conditional context sensitivity to grammatically controlled elements. Indexicalism posits more hidden variables. Contextualism allows pragmatic processes to help determine what is said even without a syntactic slot. Truth-conditional pragmatics makes this dependence systematic.

The boundary matters for compositionality, learnability, assertion, disagreement, and the classification of lying versus merely misleading.

Three contents

It helps to distinguish:

  1. sentence meaning: conventional meaning before occasion-specific values;
  2. what is said: truth-evaluable content expressed in context;
  3. what is communicated: asserted content plus implications, presuppositions, force, and other conveyed material.

Minimalists keep (2) close to encoded structure. Contextualists allow pragmatic enrichment inside (2). Everyone allows pragmatics to affect (3).

Semantic minimalism

Minimalism supplies context only to a short list:

  • indexicals;
  • demonstratives;
  • tense;
  • overt ambiguity;
  • perhaps a small number of syntactic variables.

After those values are fixed, the sentence expresses a minimal proposition.

Advantages:

  • stable compositional semantics;
  • clear distinction between saying and implying;
  • shared content across contexts;
  • resistance to unconstrained intuition.

Pressure arises when the minimal proposition seems incomplete or unlike what speakers judge was said.

Indexicalism

Indexicalism preserves semantic determination by positing hidden contextual variables.

It is raining.

may contain a location variable:

Raining(lc​,tc​).

"Everyone is here" contains a restricted domain; "Ada is ready" an activity argument.

The view explains truth-evaluable specificity compositionally. Its burden is evidence: hidden structure cannot be introduced solely because an interpretation needs it.

Contextualism

Contextualism permits free enrichment:

Ada has eaten.

may express that Ada has eaten recently or today, although no lexical item supplies the restriction.

Interpretation is guided by relevance, shared purposes, and world knowledge. The resulting content is what the speaker said, not merely implicated.

The challenge is constraint. If pragmatic enrichment can add arbitrary material, compositional meaning no longer predicts content or disagreement.

Weather reports

"It is raining" is the standard case.

  • Minimalism: expresses that it is raining, without a place.
  • Indexicalism: grammar contains a location parameter.
  • Contextualism: pragmatic processes enrich the content with a relevant location.

Tests include:

  • whether a locationless proposition is evaluable;
  • syntactic evidence for an argument;
  • cross-linguistic marking;
  • embedding and anaphora;
  • intuitions about what was said;
  • cancellation.

No single test is decisive.

Quantifier domains

Everyone arrived.

Ordinarily means everyone in a relevant group, not every person in existence.

Options:

  • quantifiers are semantically restricted by a contextual domain;
  • a covert restrictor occurs in logical form;
  • the unrestricted content is pragmatically understood relative to the QUD;
  • ordinary "everyone" conventionally ranges over salient domains.

Domain restriction is pervasive, but whether it is semantic or pragmatic affects logical form and compositional entailment.

Gradable adjectives

"Ada is tall" depends on a comparison class and standard. Ada can be tall for a child and not tall for a basketball player.

A formal semantics may include:

Height(Ada)≥Standard(c).

Contextualists may instead treat the adjective's content as modulated in use.

Gradability provides evidence for structured contextual parameters, though standards can depend on purpose and precision in ways not reducible to one number.

"Ready" and unarticulated constituents

"Ada is ready" prompts:

Ready for what?

The activity may be semantically required but unspoken, or freely supplied.

Evidence for a hidden argument includes:

  • selectional behaviour;
  • anaphoric retrieval;
  • cross-linguistic overt realization;
  • constraints on possible enrichments.

If any salient purpose can complete the sentence without syntactic trace, pragmatic completion becomes more plausible.

Modulation

Lexical content can narrow or broaden:

  • "The bottle is empty" tolerates residue.
  • "France is hexagonal" uses approximation.
  • "The city is asleep" applies a predicate non-literally to collective activity.

Contextualists treat occasion-specific concepts as part of what is said. Minimalists retain literal content and explain acceptability pragmatically.

The issue is whether literal minimal contents play an independent explanatory role in inference, denial, and embedding.

Binding and embedding tests

Semantic variables can often be bound:

Every room has a corner where it is quiet.

If a contextual element varies systematically under quantification, grammar may represent it.

Embedding also tests whether an enrichment contributes to truth conditions inside:

If Ada has eaten today, she can take the medication.

Local enrichment within antecedents is harder to explain as a global conversational afterthought.

Pragmatic theories can allow local effects, so these tests constrain rather than settle the boundary.

Cancellation

If enriched content can be cancelled without contradiction, it appears pragmatic:

Ada has eaten - not today, but last week.

Yet cancellation can target ambiguity, looseness, or metalinguistic correction. Conversely, some implicatures are awkward to cancel because context makes them highly expected.

Cancellation is one diagnostic among several.

Truth-conditional pragmatics

Truth-conditional pragmatics holds that pragmatic processes help determine the proposition expressed. Recanati distinguishes saturation required by linguistic slots from optional free enrichment.

The view aligns semantic analysis with ordinary judgments about what speakers say. It must explain:

  • constraints on enrichment;
  • cross-speaker agreement;
  • composition;
  • embedded readings;
  • distinction from implicature.

Relevance theory supplies one constraint through optimal relevance, though critics find it too flexible.

Methodological stakes

The dispute cannot be settled by asking speakers whether content "feels semantic." Evidence includes:

  • syntax;
  • cross-linguistic realization;
  • anaphora and binding;
  • projection and embedding;
  • acquisition;
  • processing;
  • cancellation;
  • disagreement and denial;
  • formal economy.

Different phenomena may fall on different sides. A single universal boundary need not classify indexicals, quantifier domains, coercion, and metaphor identically.

A productive question is not merely "semantic or pragmatic?" but:

  1. What linguistic material constrains the interpretation?
  2. Which contextual input is required?
  3. Is the result truth-conditionally embedded?
  4. Can it be denied or cancelled?
  5. Does grammar represent a variable?
  6. What explanatory work is lost if the contribution is moved?

The boundary is theoretically substantive even if it is not one sharp line across all language.
