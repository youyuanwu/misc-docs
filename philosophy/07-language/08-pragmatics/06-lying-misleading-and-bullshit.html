Lying, Misleading, and Bullshit

A speaker can utter a true sentence and intentionally create a false belief. Another can utter a false sentence without lying because they believe it true. A third can speak with indifference to whether anything said is true. Semantic truth and communicative honesty therefore come apart.

Distinguishing lying, misleading, paltering, and bullshit clarifies which content a speaker commits to, what they believe, and how they intend an audience to be affected.

Lying

A traditional analysis requires:

  1. a speaker asserts p;
  2. the speaker believes p false;
  3. the speaker intends an audience to believe p or otherwise be deceived.

Each condition is disputed. Bald-faced lies may not aim to convince; they may threaten, perform loyalty, or place a falsehood on record. Some definitions therefore require only insincere assertion, not successful or intended deception.

Lying concerns asserted content. Sarcasm can use literally false words without asserting the false proposition because the speaker overtly dissociates from it.

Misleading

A speaker misleads when they intentionally encourage a false conclusion without asserting it.

Asked whether all expenses were declared, someone replies:

I submitted receipts for the conference.

The sentence can be true while implicating that the relevant expenses were fully declared.

Misleading exploits:

  • implicature;
  • presupposition;
  • selective relevance;
  • framing;
  • ambiguity;
  • omission.

Cancellation tests what was asserted, but a speaker's later ability to say "I never said that" does not erase communicative responsibility.

Paltering

Paltering uses truthful statements to create a false impression. It is a structured form of misleading, often through selective evidence.

A candidate asked about tax compliance lists taxes they did pay while omitting the unpaid category. Each sentence is true; the intended overall inference is false.

Paltering shows why evaluating utterances one proposition at a time misses discourse design. Relevance, QUD, and selection of alternatives shape the communicated message.

Bullshit

Frankfurt characterizes bullshit by indifference to truth rather than belief in falsity. The bullshitter aims to manage impressions without caring whether claims are true.

The category differs from lying:

  • the liar tracks truth in order to conceal it;
  • the bullshitter is unconcerned with truth as a constraint.

Actual cases vary. A speaker can be reckless, evasive, performative, or strategically ambiguous without complete indifference. The philosophical category identifies an orientation toward evidence and assertion, not a semantic property of sentences.

Assertion and commitment

Whether conduct is a lie depends on what was asserted. Minimalists may assign narrow literal content; contextualists may include enriched content in what is said.

Consider:

I have eaten breakfast.

Said in response to concern about today's meal, does it assert that breakfast was eaten today or merely at some point? Boundary theories produce different classifications.

This is one practical consequence of the semantics-pragmatics debate: moral and legal assessment can depend on where asserted content ends and implication begins.

Presuppositional deception

A loaded question can introduce false background:

When did you stop falsifying reports?

Answering directly risks accommodating that falsification occurred. Speakers can also use factive verbs or definite descriptions to present contested content as settled.

Presuppositional deception differs from straightforward assertion because the content is backgrounded and may survive negation. Audiences can resist through explicit presupposition denial.

Omission and silence

Silence is not generally assertion. It can mislead when conversational role, prior undertaking, or a direct question creates an expectation of disclosure.

Relevant factors include:

  • duty to answer;
  • recognized QUD;
  • whether omission is intentional;
  • audience's reasonable inference;
  • explicit qualifications;
  • institutional norms.

Pragmatics explains how silence becomes communicatively significant without assigning one universal semantic content to every omission.

Testimony under uncertainty

A speaker can assert something false without lying if they reasonably believe it. They can assert something true irresponsibly without knowledge or evidence.

Different norms evaluate:

  • truth;
  • sincerity;
  • knowledge;
  • justification;
  • relevance;
  • completeness.

Honest communication requires more than true output, but no single norm governs every speech act.

Artificial and institutional speakers

Organizations and automated systems produce language without a simple individual belief state. Can they lie?

Possible analyses locate the relevant state in:

  • designers or operators;
  • institutional policies;
  • authorized representatives;
  • system objectives;
  • no one, reserving "lie" for agents with beliefs.

Misleading output and false assertion can be identified independently of whether the system literally believes. The vocabulary of lying may require a stronger theory of agency.
