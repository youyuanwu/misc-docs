Context and Conversation

An utterance is interpreted against a background that changes as conversation proceeds. "She is ready" requires a salient person and activity. "The meeting begins now" depends on a time. A pronoun can retrieve an individual introduced several sentences earlier. A reply counts as relevant only relative to the question currently being addressed.

Context is not one undifferentiated container. Formal semantics uses controlled parameters such as speaker, time, place, and world. Pragmatics uses richer information: common ground, salience, discourse referents, purposes, social relationships, and questions under discussion.

Conversation is dynamic. Each move can add information, shift attention, introduce referents, raise questions, undertake commitments, and change what later expressions mean.

Context as parameter

A formal context can be represented as:

c=⟨speaker,time,place,world⟩.

Characters map such contexts to contents. "I" retrieves the speaker; "now" the time. This constrained context belongs inside a compositional semantics.

Other expressions require more:

  • demonstratives depend on attention or intention;
  • "tall" depends on a comparison class;
  • "everyone" depends on a restricted domain;
  • "the table" depends on salience;
  • "ready" depends on a relevant activity.

Theoretical discipline requires stating which contextual feature is retrieved and why.

Common ground

The common ground is the information interlocutors mutually accept for current conversational purposes. It need not be knowledge or deep agreement. Participants can temporarily accept assumptions to investigate their consequences.

A context set represents worlds compatible with the common ground:

C={w:every accepted proposition is true at w}.

Asserting p proposes:

C′=C∩[[p]].

If accepted, worlds where p is false are removed.

This model captures informational update while abstracting from who is committed, degrees of belief, and unresolved questions.

Conversational score

Lewis's conversational score is broader than common ground. It includes parameters such as:

  • salient objects;
  • standards of precision;
  • comparison classes;
  • presuppositions;
  • permissible alternatives;
  • domains of quantification;
  • standards for knowledge attribution.

Utterances both depend on and alter the score. Calling one person "tall" can establish a comparison class; introducing an object makes later pronouns possible.

Scorekeeping explains accommodation and gradual contextual adjustment without assuming that every parameter is explicitly negotiated.

Salience

Salience ranks candidate referents and interpretations. It can arise from:

  • recent mention;
  • perceptual prominence;
  • grammatical subjecthood;
  • shared goals;
  • gesture;
  • contrast;
  • established topic.

Salience is not reference by itself. The most prominent object may not be what the speaker intends. It is evidence used under conventional and intentional constraints.

A formal model may rank discourse referents, but the ranking is sensitive to cognition and interaction.

Questions under discussion

Conversation is organized by explicit or implicit questions under discussion (QUDs). An answer is relevant relative to a question.

If the QUD is:

Which students passed?

"Some students passed" may be underinformative and trigger a not all implicature.

If the QUD is:

Did enough students pass to form a team?

the same sentence may be fully responsive without that inference.

QUD structure explains focus, relevance, alternatives, and why identical content has different pragmatic effects.

Questions can be nested in a stack as subquestions are raised and resolved. Real conversation also permits topic shifts and competing agendas.

Discourse referents and anaphora

In:

A student entered. She sat down.

the indefinite introduces a discourse referent available to the pronoun. Static sentence-by-sentence truth conditions make this connection awkward because the existential quantifier's scope appears confined to the first sentence.

Dynamic semantics lets interpretation update a discourse state. The indefinite adds a marker; the pronoun retrieves it.

The same machinery helps with hypothetical discourse:

If a student enters, she will receive a badge.

No actual student need exist for the pronoun to be interpreted.

Accommodation

Context can be updated with background information required for interpretation. If a speaker says "My sister is visiting," listeners may accommodate that the speaker has a sister.

Accommodation is guided by:

  • plausibility;
  • consistency;
  • minimal change;
  • relevance;
  • speaker authority;
  • ease of identifying the intended structure.

It can be resisted when the assumption is surprising or controversial. Context update is therefore negotiated, not automatic.

Context repair

Misunderstanding prompts repair:

  • "Who do you mean by she?"
  • "Which bank?"
  • "Do you mean ready to leave?"
  • "I was speaking hypothetically."

Repair provides evidence about which contextual parameters speakers recognize and how they rank alternatives. It also shows that context is partially public: interlocutors can inspect and correct their alignment.

Not all background is transparent. Participants can mistakenly assume common ground.

Formal context versus broad background

The formal context used for indexicals is deliberately small and structured. Broad background includes:

  • cultural knowledge;
  • history;
  • institutional roles;
  • relationships;
  • genre;
  • long-term projects.

Putting all background into a formal tuple risks making context an unbounded substitute for explanation. Leaving it all to pragmatics misses conventional dependencies.

A useful architecture distinguishes:

  1. grammatically controlled parameters;
  2. discourse state and salience;
  3. conversational purposes and QUDs;
  4. general world and social knowledge.

Different mechanisms access different layers.

Context change and commitments

Conversation changes more than information. An assertion can commit a speaker without securing audience acceptance. A question can make an issue salient without adding a proposition. A promise creates a normative status.

Richer models track:

  • speaker commitments;
  • audience commitments;
  • proposed common ground;
  • accepted common ground;
  • open questions;
  • plans and obligations.

This prevents the context set from treating every speech act as intersection with a proposition.

Assessment

Context is best understood as structured and dynamic rather than as a bag of everything not encoded in words. A successful theory states:

  • which features grammar requests;
  • which discourse updates introduce;
  • which purposes guide relevance;
  • which assumptions are mutually accepted;
  • how salience is established;
  • how repair changes alignment.

Formalization is valuable when it constrains these dependencies. It becomes empty when "context" simply names whatever interpretation requires.
