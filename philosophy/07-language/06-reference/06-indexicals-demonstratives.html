Indexicals and Demonstratives

"I," "here," and "now" have stable linguistic meanings but change reference across utterances. "That" can pick out a cup, its colour, its handle, or a person nearby depending on attention and intention. These expressions show that knowing a language does not always determine a referent without an occasion of use.

Kaplan's distinction between character and content provides the standard formal framework. Character is a rule from contexts to contents; content is what the expression contributes after context supplies the relevant value. Indexicals thereby combine conventional meaning with direct reference.

The framework also reveals limits of a context-free picture of propositions. What is said can depend essentially on speaker, time, place, world, demonstration, and perspective, while competent speakers grasp general rules governing that dependence.

Pure indexicals

Expressions such as:

  • I;
  • here;
  • now;
  • today;
  • yesterday; and
  • actual

are indexicals because their reference varies systematically with context.

A simple context can be represented as:

c=⟨ac​,tc​,lc​,wc​⟩,

where ac​ is the agent or speaker, tc​ the time, lc​ the location, and wc​ the world of the context.

Then:

[[I]]c=ac​,[[now]]c=tc​,[[here]]c=lc​.

The rules are stable even though their outputs vary. This is why "I" does not have a different lexical meaning for every speaker.

Character and content

Kaplan calls the stable context-to-content rule an expression's character. Its content is the value produced in a context.

For "I":

Character(I)(c)=speaker(c).

When Ada utters "I am tired," the character of "I" yields Ada. When Ben utters the same sentence, it yields Ben. The utterances share sentence character but express different contents.

Character can be understood as linguistic meaning at a level prior to occasion-specific reference. It is conventional: competent English speakers know that "I" refers to the speaker, not to the listener or the nearest person.

This gives a principled kind of context sensitivity rather than treating every contextual difference as pragmatic enrichment.

Context of utterance and circumstance of evaluation

Kaplan separates two roles often assigned to a possible world.

The context of utterance fixes the referents of indexicals. The circumstance of evaluation is the world and time relative to which the resulting content is evaluated.

Suppose Ada actually says:

I am in London.

"I" refers to Ada because she is the speaker in the actual context. To evaluate:

I might have been in Paris,

we consider counterfactual circumstances involving Ada. We do not let "I" shift to the speaker in each counterfactual world.

This explains why indexicals can be directly referential and rigid after context fixes their content.

Content and proposition

If propositions are context-independent objects of assertion, two utterances of "I am tired" by different speakers express different propositions:

⟨Ada,tired⟩=⟨Ben,tired⟩.

Yet speakers can understand the sentence type without knowing which proposition a future utterance will express. They know its character.

This produces a layered semantics:

charactercontext​contentcircumstance​truth value.

No one layer replaces the others. Character models linguistic competence; content models what is said; evaluation determines truth.

Demonstratives

"That," "this," "she," and some uses of "here" require more than an automatically retrieved contextual coordinate. A demonstrative depends on salience, gesture, attention, or intention.

A speaker pointing into a crowded display and saying:

That is damaged.

may intend:

  • a particular vase;
  • the vase's handle;
  • a visible crack;
  • a photograph of the vase; or
  • a type exemplified by the item.

Physical pointing underdetermines reference. The audience uses perceptual scene, conversation, shared purposes, and recognizable intention.

Demonstration and intention

Early Kaplan associated a demonstrative with a directing intention or demonstration that completes its character. Later discussion asks which factor has priority when gesture, attention, and intention diverge.

Candidate accounts emphasize:

  • speaker intention: the referent is what the speaker intends to demonstrate;
  • objective demonstration: public gesture and perceptual direction constrain reference;
  • audience-directed intention: the relevant intention is one the speaker intends the audience to recognize;
  • salience: context supplies the uniquely prominent candidate; and
  • hybrid constraint: intention succeeds only when appropriately connected to a publicly available object.

Pure private intention is too unconstrained. A speaker cannot point at a cup while secretly intending "that" to refer to a remote star with no recognizable connection. Pure gesture is also insufficient because one gesture intersects many candidate objects and properties.

Reference versus successful communication

A demonstrative use can fail in several ways:

  • no object is present;
  • several candidates are equally salient;
  • the speaker's intention is incoherent;
  • the audience attends to the wrong object;
  • the speaker mistakes one object for another; or
  • the object disappears before the utterance is completed.

The semantic question is what, if anything, the token refers to. The pragmatic question is what the speaker tried to communicate and what the audience recovered.

A theory can therefore say that reference failed while communication partially succeeded, or that reference was fixed despite audience misunderstanding.

Perry's essential indexical

Perry follows a trail of sugar through a supermarket while trying to find the shopper making a mess. He then realizes:

I am making a mess.

He may already have believed, of Perry, that Perry was making a mess. The new first-person belief explains a change in action: he adjusts the torn bag.

The case suggests that indexical modes of presentation are essential to practical reasoning. Replacing "I" with a non-indexical name can preserve reference and truth-condition while losing the action-guiding perspective.

Similar cases involve:

  • "It is happening now";
  • "The meeting is here";
  • "You are the person addressed."

The lesson can be expressed through character, de se content, centered worlds, or special first-person modes of presentation. A purely objectual proposition appears too coarse to explain cognitive and practical significance.

De se attitudes

A de se attitude represents something as true of oneself. It differs from merely having an attitude about the same person under another description.

An amnesiac may believe:

The person in the mirror is in danger

without realizing:

I am in danger.

The referent is the same; the self-locating content differs.

Centered-world semantics represents possibilities as world-individual-time triples, allowing a subject to locate themselves within a world rather than merely identify which uncentered world is actual.

Whether centered content is public semantic content or a structure of thought remains disputed. Indexical sentences provide public vehicles for expressing perspective even if the underlying attitudes require additional mental architecture.

Token-reflexive analyses

A token-reflexive theory analyses indexicals through relations to the utterance token:

  • "I" means the producer of this token;
  • "now" means the time of this token;
  • "here" means the location of this token.

This captures systematic dependence without a rich context tuple. It also treats written and recorded tokens naturally in some cases.

Complications include:

  • delayed or scheduled messages;
  • inscriptions without a clear producer;
  • quotation and mention;
  • fictional utterances;
  • answering-machine messages heard much later; and
  • uses of "here" referring to a wider salient region rather than the token's exact location.

Character-based and token-reflexive approaches may be notational variants for pure indexicals but diverge in nonstandard contexts.

Deferred and descriptive uses

Demonstratives can refer through an attended object to something else. Pointing at a photograph, one can say:

She is a remarkable architect.

and refer to the photographed person rather than the photograph. Pointing at an empty parking space, one can refer to the person who normally parks there.

These deferred uses show that perceptual target and referent can differ according to an established relation and communicative purpose.

Some uses are also descriptive:

He who scores highest will advance.

The pronoun-like expression may vary with whoever satisfies a condition rather than directly designate one actual person.

A complete theory should distinguish genuinely directly referential tokens from anaphoric, bound, descriptive, and deferred uses sharing surface forms.

Indexicals in reported speech

Indexicals normally shift when speech is reported:

Ada said, "I am tired."

becomes:

Ada said that she was tired.

The reporter adjusts person and tense while preserving attributed content. Some languages contain indexical shift, where an embedded counterpart of "I" can refer to the reported speaker rather than the utterance speaker.

Cross-linguistic variation warns against treating English patterns as universal. Formal semantics can parameterize which operator controls which contextual coordinate.

Quotation blocks ordinary evaluation:

Ada said the word "I."

Here the expression is mentioned rather than used to refer to a speaker.

Contextualism beyond indexicals

The success of character semantics tempts theorists to treat every contextual effect as a hidden indexical. "Tall" may contain a variable for comparison class, "ready" for an activity, and quantifiers for a restricted domain.

Indexicalism preserves compositional truth-conditions by positing overt or covert parameters. Contextualism allows pragmatic enrichment without requiring syntactic variables.

Indexicals provide evidence that some context sensitivity is conventional and structured. They do not show that every contextual contribution has the same form. Independent syntactic and semantic evidence is needed for each proposed parameter.

Demonstratives add a further lesson: reference emerges from convention, public context, attention, intention, and audience-directed practice rather than from any single factor in isolation.
