Natural-Kind Terms and Semantic Externalism

"Water," "gold," "tiger," and "electron" appear to classify things independently of what any one speaker believes. A person can competently use "gold" while being unable to distinguish it from pyrite, and a community can discover that many beliefs about a kind were false without concluding that it had always spoken about something else.

Kripke and Putnam use these cases to argue that reference is not fixed solely by descriptions in a speaker's head. Natural-kind terms depend on historical contact with samples, environmental facts, and a division of linguistic labour. This is semantic externalism.

The semantic thesis must be separated from a stronger metaphysical thesis. Externalism can hold even if kinds have complex, interest-sensitive, or cluster-based structures. The ontology of kinds is examined in Natural Kinds; this page asks how the terms refer.

Descriptive identification

Before modern chemistry, speakers identified water through features such as:

  • clear;
  • drinkable;
  • fills lakes and rivers;
  • falls as rain;
  • freezes and boils in familiar ways.

A descriptive theory says "water" refers to whatever satisfies an associated description or cluster. The account explains how speakers recognize and communicate about samples.

But superficial descriptions can be satisfied by a different substance, and many speakers possess incomplete or false criteria. Scientific discovery appears to reveal what water is rather than change the word to a wholly new subject.

This motivates a distinction between:

  • stereotype or mode of presentation: features ordinary speakers associate with the term;
  • reference: the kind to which the practice is historically and environmentally connected.

The stereotype guides identification without necessarily determining extension.

Twin Earth

Putnam imagines a planet matching Earth in ordinary appearances and speaker psychology around 1750. The liquid in its lakes and taps behaves like water but has a different chemical structure, abbreviated XYZ.

Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth are intrinsic duplicates. Each says:

Water is wet.

Putnam's judgment is:

[[waterEarth​]]=H2​O,

[[waterTwin​]]=XYZ.

Their terms differ in extension despite identical internal states and associated descriptions. As Putnam summarizes the result, "'meanings' just ain't in the head" ("The Meaning of 'Meaning'," 1975).

The case directly supports externalism about public linguistic reference. Extending it to mental content requires an additional claim that the content of thought inherits the linguistic or world-involving concept. That debate remains in Internalism and Externalism about Mental Content.

Ostension and samples

A natural-kind term may be introduced roughly as:

Let "water" apply to this liquid and other samples of the same kind.

The phrase same kind is crucial. Ostension alone underdetermines whether the term tracks:

  • the sample;
  • its colour;
  • its chemical composition;
  • a functional category such as drinkable liquid;
  • a phase such as liquid water;
  • a local source; or
  • an arbitrary disjunction.

Reference fixing presupposes classificatory intentions and a surrounding practice of induction and correction. Externalism is not bare causal contact.

Later science can refine what sameness relation best explains the practice. That refinement may be discovery, conceptual revision, or both.

Division of linguistic labour

Ordinary speakers cannot identify elms, aluminium, arthritis, or many chemical substances by expert criteria. They defer to specialists and to the wider linguistic community.

Putnam's division of linguistic labour says that competence can be distributed:

  • ordinary speakers know enough stereotype and syntax to participate;
  • experts possess more reliable identification methods;
  • institutions preserve samples, tests, and taxonomies;
  • historical chains transmit the term; and
  • the community jointly sustains reference.

This explains partial understanding without making every speaker's idiosyncratic beliefs meaning-constitutive.

Deference is not unlimited. Speakers can reject purported experts, communities can contain rival classifications, and technical uses can diverge from ordinary ones. A theory must identify which practice a use participates in rather than appeal vaguely to "the community."

Social externalism

Burge's arthritis case shows externalism without a natural kind in the simple chemical sense. A patient believes arthritis can occur in the thigh. In the actual community, "arthritis" is restricted to joint inflammation, so the belief is false. In a counterfactual community where the same word covers joint and muscle ailments, an intrinsic duplicate would express a different concept.

Content depends partly on communal norms and expert use. Error is possible because a speaker can incompletely understand a term while deferring to a practice.

The mechanism differs from environmental externalism:

  • environmental: reference depends on substances or kinds causally encountered;
  • social: reference depends on a community's division of labour and standards.

Many scientific terms involve both.

Rigidity

Kripke extends rigid designation from names to natural-kind terms. If "water" rigidly designates the kind water, it picks out that kind in every possible world where the kind exists.

In a counterfactual world where lakes contain XYZ, the liquid is not water even if it looks and functions like water. Conversely, water remains H2​O under unusual appearances or environmental conditions.

This differs from a description such as:

the clear drinkable liquid filling local lakes.

That description can select different substances in different worlds. The term and description therefore have different modal profiles.

Rigidity is a semantic claim about designation across counterfactual evaluation. It does not by itself identify the essence of the kind.

Necessary a posteriori identities

If "water" rigidly designates water and "H2​O" rigidly designates the chemical kind H2​O, then the discovered identity:

water=H2​O

is necessary if true. The discovery is a posteriori because empirical investigation was needed to learn that the two terms co-refer.

The argument has two stages:

  1. Semantic: establish that the terms rigidly designate the same kind.
  2. Metaphysical: apply the necessity of identity.

Neither stage is supplied by chemistry alone. Chemistry provides structural facts and explanatory success. The claim that the ordinary term rigidly targets exactly that kind requires semantic and metaphysical premises.

The complexities of water, isotopes, mixtures, and scientific classification are discussed in Natural Kinds and Necessity and Metaphysics.

Externalism without simple essences

Externalism does not require every kind to have one microstructural essence. A term could be externally anchored to:

  • a homeostatic property cluster;
  • an evolutionary lineage;
  • a functional kind;
  • a historically continuous practice;
  • a plural scientific category; or
  • a family of context-sensitive classifications.

"Tiger" may track a biological lineage rather than a list of intrinsic properties. "Disease" may combine causal, functional, and evaluative dimensions. "Jade" divided into jadeite and nephrite when inquiry showed two structures under one ordinary label.

These cases can produce reference refinement or reference splitting rather than the discovery of one hidden essence.

The externalist core is that reference is not exhausted by individual conception. It can survive a more plural metaphysics of kinds.

Qua problem and interest

Any sample instantiates indefinitely many properties. Why does a baptism target molecular composition rather than appearance, origin, function, or an arbitrary disjunction?

Answers appeal to:

  • explicit classificatory intention;
  • explanatory and inductive practice;
  • scientific investigation;
  • naturalness or joint-carving;
  • biological or practical interests; and
  • later communal stabilization.

If naturalness alone selects the referent, metasemantics depends on a substantive metaphysics of privileged properties. If interests alone select it, reference may become too relative. Most accounts combine world structure with the purposes of the classification.

Reference change through science

Scientific inquiry sometimes preserves a term while revising belief:

  • whales cease to count as fish in technical taxonomy;
  • combustion is explained without phlogiston;
  • atoms turn out to have internal structure;
  • genes receive changing molecular and functional analyses.

Was the old term always about the later referent, did reference change, or did the term fail to refer?

Factors include:

  • continuity of samples and explanatory role;
  • degree of preserved inferential practice;
  • explicit decisions by specialists;
  • whether the old theory uniquely targeted anything;
  • success of retrospective interpretation; and
  • practical interests of historical inquiry.

Causal continuity prevents every theory revision from changing the subject, but it does not guarantee reference through radical failure.

Competence and stereotype

Putnam distinguishes knowing a term's full extension from possessing enough competence to use it. Ordinary competence may require:

  • command of grammatical category;
  • a socially recognized stereotype;
  • ability to defer appropriately;
  • participation in relevant correction practices; and
  • intention to use the community's term.

This permits semantic division of labour without saying that any noise copied from an expert is competent use.

Stereotypes remain important to cognition and communication. "Water" evokes ordinary features that help speakers identify and discuss it. Externalism denies only that the individual stereotype fully fixes reference.

Self-knowledge and transparency

A speaker may know:

I am thinking that water is wet.

without knowing that water is H2​O or even whether their environment is Earth or Twin Earth. Externalism distinguishes knowing one's thought from knowing the facts that fix its content.

The distinction preserves ordinary first-person authority but makes semantic knowledge less transparent. A person can be wrong about the extension, history, or expert norms partly constituting their words.

This is unsurprising for public language: speakers routinely use inherited terms whose full criteria they do not know. The sharper mental-content problem is treated in the mind section.

Semantic externalism convincingly shows that individual descriptions do not exhaust reference. Its strongest defensible conclusion is relational: natural-kind terms are fixed through interactions among speakers, communities, historical chains, samples, and structured reality. Which factor dominates varies across terms, and no semantic slogan by itself supplies a universal metaphysics of kinds.
