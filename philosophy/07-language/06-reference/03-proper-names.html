Proper Names

Proper names appear to be the simplest referring expressions. "Ada" picks out Ada; "Venus" picks out Venus. Yet names generate several of the deepest problems in semantics. How can a name refer when speakers know almost nothing about its bearer? Why are identities between names informative? What happens in belief reports? Can a name remain meaningful when its bearer does not exist? How does a naming practice persist or change?

The modern debate moves from Millian direct reference through Fregean and Russellian descriptivism to Kripke's arguments for rigidity and a causal-historical picture. Contemporary views often combine direct truth-conditional contribution with separate resources for cognitive significance.

Millianism

On a Millian view, a proper name contributes only its bearer to the proposition expressed. Names have denotation but no descriptive connotation.

Thus:

Ada runs.

expresses a singular proposition containing Ada herself and the property of running. If "Ada" and "the morning runner" happen to identify the same person, the expressions can still differ semantically because the description contributes quantificational or descriptive structure while the name contributes the object.

Millianism explains the apparent semantic simplicity and rigidity of names. It faces:

  • informative identities between co-referring names;
  • substitution failure in belief reports;
  • differences in understanding;
  • negative existentials; and
  • empty fictional or mistaken names.

The theory's usual strategy is to keep truth-conditional content coarse and explain cognitive differences elsewhere.

Fregean senses

Frege distinguishes a name's reference from its sense, the mode under which the referent is presented. "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" both refer to Venus but express different senses.

This explains why:

Hesperus is Phosphorus

can be informative while:

Hesperus is Hesperus

is trivial. It also explains why a rational speaker may believe one corresponding sentence without believing another.

The account raises identity conditions for senses. Different speakers can associate different information with a name while still communicating. If every cognitive difference is a difference in sense, shared content becomes excessively fine-grained.

The general problem is developed in Sense and Reference.

Russellian descriptivism

Russell treated ordinary names as disguised definite descriptions. A logically proper name would refer directly to an object with which the thinker is acquainted, but most ordinary names abbreviate descriptions.

"Aristotle" might be analysed as:

the pupil of Plato who taught Alexander and wrote the Metaphysics.

The name then refers to whatever uniquely satisfies the description. If nothing does, the sentence containing it can still receive a quantificational analysis.

Descriptivism explains cognitive significance and empty-name intelligibility in one framework. It also turns reference into something an individual could fix through their associated knowledge.

The costs are ignorance, error, variation among speakers, and modal mismatch.

Cluster theories

No single description is known or accepted by every competent speaker. A cluster theory instead associates a weighted family:

  • pupil of Plato;
  • teacher of Alexander;
  • author of the Metaphysics;
  • ancient Greek philosopher; and so on.

The name refers to the object satisfying enough of the important descriptions. This allows some misinformation and individual variation.

The theory must specify:

  • which descriptions belong to the cluster;
  • how they are weighted;
  • how much satisfaction is enough;
  • what happens when different objects satisfy different parts; and
  • how a speaker with almost no identifying information refers.

If the cluster is whatever best preserves ordinary reference judgments, explanatory circularity threatens.

Kripke's modal argument

Names and descriptions behave differently under modality.

Aristotle might not have taught Alexander.

This is true. Aristotle could have pursued a different life.

If "Aristotle" meant the teacher of Alexander, the sentence would become:

The teacher of Alexander might not have taught Alexander.

On the relevant de dicto reading, that is false or contradictory. The description selects whoever satisfies it in each world; the name continues to concern Aristotle.

Kripke calls a term rigid if it designates the same object in every possible world in which that object exists. Proper names are rigid; ordinary identifying descriptions are usually non-rigid.

The argument shows that a name is not synonymous with the description. A description may still fix its initial reference.

The epistemic argument

If "Aristotle" meant the teacher of Alexander, then:

Aristotle taught Alexander

would be knowable a priori by anyone who understood the name. It is an empirical historical claim.

Competence with a name does not generally include knowledge of a uniquely identifying description. Speakers can use "Feynman" while knowing only that Feynman was a famous physicist, a description satisfied by many people.

The argument separates semantic competence from identifying knowledge. A speaker may depend on the linguistic community to preserve reference.

The semantic argument

Suppose the incompleteness theorem was proved by Schmidt and stolen by Gödel. Speakers associate "Gödel" with the prover of incompleteness. Intuitively, their name still refers to Gödel, and their belief about him is false; it does not unknowingly refer to Schmidt.

Reference survives massive descriptive error. A theory making the best-fitting description constitutive predicts the wrong bearer.

Defenders of descriptivism can enrich the cluster with historical or causal descriptions, such as the person called "Gödel" by the community. But this concedes that reference depends on a naming practice not reducible to ordinary descriptive belief.

Rigidity and necessary identity

If two names rigidly designate the same object, then a true identity between them is necessary:

a=b→□(a=b).

Hesperus could not have failed to be Phosphorus, because both are Venus. The empirical discovery concerns which object each name actually designates; once the identity is known, its necessity follows from the necessity of identity.

This yields the necessary a posteriori: a claim necessary in modal status but known through empirical investigation.

The argument depends on names being rigid and on cross-world identity. It does not show that every scientific identity has one unambiguous necessary reading. The broader modal cautions are developed in Necessity and Metaphysics.

Baptism and causal transmission

If descriptions do not determine reference, how is it fixed? Kripke sketches:

  1. A name is introduced through ostension or description.
  2. Later speakers acquire it through communication.
  3. Each intends to use it with the reference it has in the source practice.
  4. A causal-historical chain connects later tokens to the introduction.

A baptism can be ostensive:

Let this child be called "Ada."

or descriptive:

Let "Neptune" name the planet causing these perturbations.

The description can fix reference without becoming synonymous with the name. Once the practice is established, the name directly designates the object.

The qua problem

Ostension does not identify which aspect is named. Pointing at a person could introduce a name for the person, their body, a temporal stage, a role, or even a visible surface. The act occurs under a sortal or identifying intention.

This gives descriptive or conceptual material a role in baptism. Direct reference does not mean reference is fixed without classification. The question is whether that material enters later semantic content or only explains the introduction.

Reference-preserving intention

Causal contact by itself is insufficient. A speaker may hear "Feynman" from another person while deciding to use it as a new name for their dog. The new use is causally connected to physicist-directed uses but does not refer to the physicist.

Transmission requires an intention to use the name as received. That intention cannot be specified merely as "intend to preserve the reference" without some circularity. It may instead involve deference to the source speaker or continuation of a public practice.

Mistakes complicate the picture. Mishearing, conflating two people, or attaching a name to the wrong photograph can produce mixed chains whose reference is not obvious.

Reference change and branching

Historical practices can redirect names. The standard example "Madagascar" may have shifted from a mainland region reported by earlier sources to the island now bearing the name. The later practice is causally linked to the earlier one but has a different referent.

This shows that causal origin is not permanently decisive. Sustained communal intention, use, and interaction can establish a new reference.

Chains can also branch:

  • two people receive the same name;
  • one historical figure is split into two legendary characters;
  • two naming practices merge;
  • an astronomical observation is misidentified and later corrected.

A causal theory must explain when continuity preserves reference and when a new practice begins.

Descriptive names

Some names are introduced by descriptions whose satisfaction seems central:

  • "Jack the Ripper";
  • "Deep Throat";
  • a mathematical object named as the unique solution to a condition;
  • an astronomical body predicted before observation.

If no unique object satisfies the description, does the name fail to refer? If an unexpected object caused the evidence, does reference shift to it?

These cases show that names can have different introduction histories. A uniform direct semantics may coexist with multiple reference-fixing mechanisms.

Belief reports

Substitution of co-referring names can fail:

Lois believes that Superman can fly.

may be true while:

Lois believes that Clark Kent can fly.

is false, although Superman is Clark Kent.

A Millian proposition contains the same person and property in both cases. Responses include:

  • belief relates subjects to propositions under modes of presentation;
  • reports pragmatically convey a way of identifying the object;
  • names contribute guises or mental files in attitude contexts;
  • attitude reports are context-sensitive in their standards of attribution; and
  • the sentences express different fine-grained propositions.

The choice affects both semantics and philosophy of mind. Direct reference keeps ordinary extensional content simple but requires extra structure for attitudes.

Empty names

If a name contributes only its bearer, a bearerless name appears to contribute nothing. Yet speakers understand "Vulcan does not exist" and "Sherlock Holmes is a detective."

Options include:

  • the sentence lacks a complete proposition;
  • the name contributes a descriptive sense;
  • free logic admits empty terms;
  • fiction introduces a pretense operator;
  • a fictional artifact becomes the referent; or
  • the utterance is about a name or representational practice.

No solution follows from direct reference alone. The alternatives are compared in Empty Names and Reference Failure.

Hybrid theories

Contemporary accounts often divide the work:

  • the referent is the name's truth-conditional contribution;
  • causal and social history fix that referent;
  • a mode of presentation explains cognitive significance;
  • speaker intentions guide local use;
  • pragmatic context helps audiences identify the target; and
  • free logic or pretense handles failure.

The result lacks the elegance of one mechanism but matches the diversity of phenomena. The central dispute becomes where each factor belongs: public semantics, metasemantics, thought, or pragmatics.

Proper names teach a general lesson: how an expression contributes to truth-conditions, how its reference is historically fixed, and how a speaker thinks about its bearer are distinct questions. A successful theory must connect them without pretending they are one relation.
