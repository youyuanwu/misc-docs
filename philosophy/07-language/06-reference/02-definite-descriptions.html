Definite Descriptions

Expressions of the form "the F" appear to pick out an individual: "the author of Middlemarch," "the tallest building," "the present king of France." Yet descriptions behave unlike simple names. They encode a condition, interact systematically with quantifiers and negation, and can remain meaningful when nothing uniquely satisfies them.

Russell's theory treats a definite description not as a referring expression but as quantificational structure. Strawson argues that ordinary uses instead presuppose a referent. Donnellan distinguishes attributive from referential uses, showing that a speaker can use a description to direct attention even when it is false of the intended object.

The debate became a model for philosophy of language: apparent grammatical form may hide logical form, but formal analysis must still explain how expressions are actually used in context.

The problem of apparent denotation

If "the present king of France" is a name-like expression, then:

The present king of France is bald.

appears to contain a non-denoting subject. One might conclude that the sentence is meaningless or lacks a truth value. But speakers understand it, can negate it, and can reason about why it fails.

Descriptions create three connected puzzles:

  1. Nonexistence: what happens when nothing satisfies the description?
  2. Nonuniqueness: what happens when several things satisfy it?
  3. Scope: how does the description interact with negation, modality, and other quantifiers?

Russell answers all three by analysing the description away.

Russell's quantificational analysis

"The F is G" has the logical form:

∃x(Fx∧∀y(Fy→y=x)∧Gx).

It says:

  1. at least one F exists;
  2. at most one F exists; and
  3. that object is G.

Existence and uniqueness are part of the asserted content. The description is an incomplete symbol: it has no denotation in isolation but contributes through the quantificational structure of the whole sentence.

For the king example, no object satisfies the first two clauses, so the sentence is false. No mysterious nonexistent monarch is required as a semantic value.

The analysis exemplifies Russell's method of replacing misleading surface grammar with logical form.

Negation and scope

The sentence:

The present king of France is not bald.

has two readings.

On wide scope for the description:

∃x(Kx∧∀y(Ky→y=x)∧¬Bx).

It says a unique king exists and is not bald. This is false.

On wide scope for negation:

¬∃x(Kx∧∀y(Ky→y=x)∧Bx).

It says it is not the case that a unique king is bald. This is true when no king exists.

Russell's theory explains why ordinary negation can feel ambiguous. The difference is not lexical meaning but quantifier scope.

Primary and secondary occurrence

Russell distinguished a description's primary occurrence, where it takes wide scope, from a secondary occurrence, where it remains within the scope of another operator.

Consider:

Ada wants the winner to receive a prize.

On one reading, there is a particular person who is the winner and whom Ada wants to receive a prize. On another, Ada wants whoever turns out uniquely to win to receive a prize, without having any particular person in mind.

The distinction generalizes to belief, modality, and obligation. Quantificational analysis predicts readings by varying scope rather than positing lexical ambiguity.

Descriptions and existence

Russell's analysis solves negative existentials:

The present king of France does not exist.

becomes:

¬∃x(Kx∧∀y(Ky→y=x)).

The statement is true without a name that must denote a nonexistent object. This strategy underlies Russell's and Quine's treatment of apparent ontological commitment.

The success for descriptions does not prove that ordinary proper names are disguised descriptions. Kripke's arguments target that further step. A language may contain both quantificational descriptions and directly referring names.

Strawson and presupposition

Strawson objects that Russell confuses a sentence with uses of it. Someone who seriously utters "The present king of France is bald" in an ordinary contemporary context does not assert a false existential conjunction. The attempt to refer fails, and the question of truth or falsity may not arise.

On a presuppositional treatment:

  • the assertion is that the relevant king is bald;
  • existence and uniqueness are presupposed background conditions;
  • if the presupposition fails, the utterance may be infelicitous or truth-valueless.

Evidence comes from projection. Existence appears to survive negation and questioning:

  • The present king of France is not bald.
  • Is the present king of France bald?

Both continue to suggest that there is a present king. If existence were merely part of asserted content under ordinary negation, this persistence would require further explanation.

Modern semantic accounts may represent the presupposition through partial functions, definedness conditions, or dynamic context update. Pragmatic accounts treat it as a requirement on common ground.

Russell versus Strawson

The disagreement is partly about target.

Russell offers a compositional analysis of a construction's truth-conditional contribution. Strawson emphasizes what speakers presuppose and accomplish in ordinary contexts. A theory can combine:

  • Russellian quantificational content;
  • presuppositional constraints on felicitous use; and
  • pragmatic expectations about relevance and shared knowledge.

The combination is not automatic. If existence is semantically asserted, a separate account must explain projection. If it is only presupposed, the theory must explain cases where speakers appear to deny or accommodate it explicitly.

Donnellan's attributive and referential uses

Suppose a man is on trial for Smith's murder. Seeing the defendant behave strangely, a speaker says:

Smith's murderer is insane.

On an attributive use, the speaker says of whoever uniquely murdered Smith that they are insane. The description is essential to identifying the target.

On a referential use, the speaker uses the description to direct attention to the defendant. If the defendant is innocent but insane, the speaker may have successfully said something about that person despite the description's falsity.

Donnellan argues that Russell captures attributive use but not every referential use. The case motivates the distinction between semantic reference and speaker reference.

Semantic ambiguity or pragmatic use?

Does "the F" have two semantic meanings? A Russellian can deny ambiguity:

  • semantically, the description remains quantificational;
  • pragmatically, the speaker uses it to help an audience identify an object;
  • what the speaker refers to can diverge from what uniquely satisfies the words.

This preserves a unified semantics and treats referential use as a communicative achievement. Critics reply that some truth judgments track the intended object strongly enough to suggest truth-conditional effects.

The dispute illustrates the semantics-pragmatics boundary: data about what a speaker communicated do not alone determine what the expression conventionally contributed.

Incomplete descriptions

Ordinary descriptions are frequently not globally unique:

The table is covered with books.

There are many tables in the world. Russell's uniqueness clause appears false unless the description is enriched to something like the contextually salient table here.

Possible analyses include:

  • implicit domain restriction: quantification ranges over contextually relevant objects;
  • descriptive completion: context supplies additional descriptive material;
  • salience: a ranking selects the most prominent satisfier;
  • situation restriction: uniqueness is evaluated within a relevant situation; and
  • pragmatic identification: semantic content is weaker while context identifies the intended object.

Theories differ on whether the restriction is represented in syntax, supplied to a semantic parameter, or pragmatically inferred.

Incomplete descriptions show that uniqueness must be relativized, but not that Russellian structure disappears.

Plural, possessive, and functional descriptions

Natural language contains constructions beyond singular "the F":

  • "the students";
  • "Ada's teacher";
  • "the temperature of the sample";
  • "the average taxpayer"; and
  • "the first person to arrive."

Some are plural, some relational, some functional, and some appear generic or statistical rather than referential. A complete semantics should not force them all into one singular template merely because English uses "the."

Russell's lasting contribution is the method: analyse quantificational structure and scope explicitly. The exact analysis may vary by construction.

Descriptions under modality

Descriptions are generally non-rigid. "The inventor of bifocals" picks out Benjamin Franklin in actuality, but another person could have invented bifocals.

Compare:

  1. Benjamin Franklin might not have invented bifocals.
  2. The inventor of bifocals might not have invented bifocals.

The first is naturally true. The second has:

  • a de re reading about Franklin, true;
  • a de dicto reading saying someone uniquely invented bifocals without inventing them, false.

Scope distinguishes the readings. Names appear rigid where descriptions vary with the world of evaluation, forming a central argument against descriptivism about names.
