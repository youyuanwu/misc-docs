Empty Names and Reference Failure

"Vulcan does not exist" appears meaningful and true. "Sherlock Holmes is a detective" appears meaningful and true in some sense. "Nineteen is prime" is straightforwardly true despite disagreement about what numbers are. These cases show that understanding a name does not always require encountering an ordinary concrete bearer.

An empty name is a singular term that fails to refer within the relevant domain. Empty names create a dilemma for direct reference: if a name contributes only its bearer, and there is no bearer, what content does it contribute? Yet ordinary speakers reason with empty names, distinguish claims involving them, and discover that some apparently successful names were empty.

This page compares semantic responses. It does not decide whether nonexistent or fictional objects belong in reality's inventory. That ontological dispute remains in Fictional and Intentional Objects and Meinongianism.

Three kinds of apparent emptiness

Cases should be separated before choosing a theory.

Failed theoretical posits

"Vulcan" was introduced for a planet hypothesized to explain Mercury's orbit. No such planet exists. The name arose within serious empirical inquiry, not fiction.

Fictional names

"Sherlock Holmes" belongs to stories whose practice supplies rich, stable claims. The name may fail to refer to a concrete person while participating in fictional and external discourse.

Deliberate or accidental inventions

A fabricated person in a deception, a name introduced through a mistaken report, or a label from a corrupted transmission chain may lack a bearer without belonging to an established fiction.

A theory that handles one class may not handle all. Pretense naturally fits fiction but less naturally a failed scientific hypothesis. Abstract artifacts may fit authored characters but not every accidental empty name.

The negative existential puzzle

Consider:

Vulcan does not exist.

Three plausible claims conflict:

  1. The sentence is meaningful and true.
  2. A meaningful subject-predicate sentence requires its subject to contribute something.
  3. "Vulcan" has no referent.

If the name contributes nothing, composition seems to fail. If it contributes an object, the negative existential appears false. Theories escape by revising the semantic role of the name, the logic of denotation, the ontology, or the analysis of the utterance.

Russellian elimination

Russell treats ordinary names as disguised definite descriptions. "Vulcan" might abbreviate:

the unique planet causing the anomalous precession of Mercury.

Then "Vulcan does not exist" becomes:

¬∃x(Vx∧∀y(Vy→y=x)),

where V expresses the descriptive condition. The sentence is true because nothing uniquely satisfies it. No empty singular term remains in logical form.

Advantages:

  • negative existentials receive ordinary truth-conditions;
  • understanding comes from descriptive content;
  • classical quantification is preserved; and
  • no nonexistent object is required.

Costs:

  • proper names generally are not synonymous with descriptions;
  • speakers may associate different or mistaken descriptions;
  • causal-historical practices can persist after a theory changes; and
  • fictional names do not always have one uniquely identifying descriptive package.

Russellian analysis remains powerful for genuine descriptions even if it fails as a universal theory of names.

Fregean sense without reference

A Fregean can allow a name to express a sense that determines no reference. Speakers understand the mode of presentation even though nothing answers to it.

This distinguishes "Vulcan" from an unrelated empty name: each has a different sense. It also explains cognitive significance and the possibility of rational reasoning.

The remaining problem is sentence value. If a component lacks reference, Frege's principle that the reference of a whole depends on the references of its parts suggests that the sentence lacks a truth value. Negative existentials then risk becoming gappy rather than true.

Possible repairs include:

  • special semantic clauses for existence;
  • treating existence claims as about concepts rather than objects;
  • assigning a conventional dummy referent;
  • partial logics; or
  • letting sense determine structured content even without objectual reference.

Each modifies the simple Fregean architecture.

Free logic

Free logic permits singular terms that denote nothing. It rejects the classical assumption that every name designates an object in the domain.

An explicit existence predicate can be defined:

E!a=df​∃x(x=a).

Then:

¬E!v

expresses that Vulcan does not exist without implying that v denotes.

Free logics differ over atomic sentences containing empty terms:

  • negative: every such atomic sentence is false;
  • positive: some may be true;
  • neutral: they lack a truth value.

The formal choice affects fictional predication and inference. Free logic shows how reasoning with empty terms can be consistent; it does not by itself say what semantic content the empty name contributes.

The logical details and ontological implications are introduced in Existence and the Quantifier.

Gappy propositions

On a direct-reference theory, a sentence containing an empty name may express a gappy proposition: a structured content with an unfilled position.

Schematically:

⟨__,is a planet⟩.

Different empty names may be associated with different representational histories even if the object position is empty. The structure explains why the sentence appears incomplete from the standpoint of singular content.

Objections include:

  • two empty names may generate the same gap despite clear cognitive differences;
  • it is unclear how a gappy proposition is true or false;
  • compositional operations on gaps require special rules; and
  • speakers appear to express determinate fictional claims.

Gappy accounts fit failed reference better than rich fictional discourse unless supplemented by modes of presentation or pretense.

Meinongian and noneist approaches

A Meinongian denies that emptiness entails no object. "Vulcan" or "Pegasus" can denote an object that lacks existence. The negative existential says of that object that it does not exist.

This gives transparent subject-predicate structure and permits true characterization of nonexistent objects. It must distinguish:

  • being from existence;
  • properties an object has in its characterization from properties it exemplifies;
  • incomplete and impossible objects;
  • identity among nonexistent objects; and
  • quantification over what there is from quantification over what exists.

The semantic elegance purchases substantial ontology. Whether that price is acceptable cannot be decided by linguistic data alone.

Pretense theories

On a pretense or make-believe account, fictional discourse operates within a practice of imagining according to prescribed rules. In the Holmes stories:

Holmes is a detective

is fictionally true. The utterance need not refer to a concrete or nonexistent person; it is licensed by the relevant pretense.

External claims require further analysis:

  • Holmes is more famous than any real detective.
  • Holmes was created by Conan Doyle.
  • Some fictional detectives are admired by real readers.

These compare fictional and real discourse or appear to quantify over characters. Responses treat them as claims about representations, roles in pretense, or abstract artifacts.

Pretense explains internal fictional truth and authorial practice well. It is less natural for failed scientific names whose users intended literal reference.

Fictional artifacts

An abstract-artifact theory holds that authors create abstract entities: fictional characters. "Sherlock Holmes" then refers to an abstract artifact created through Doyle's literary activity.

This supports external claims:

  • Holmes was created in the nineteenth century.
  • Holmes appears in four novels.
  • Holmes is more famous than many real detectives.

Internal claims such as "Holmes lives at Baker Street" cannot be literally true of an abstract object. They are analysed under an implicit story operator:

According to the Holmes stories, Holmes lives at Baker Street.

The view gives fictional names genuine referents but does not make those referents the concrete people represented in stories. It also depends on an ontology of created abstracta.

Metalinguistic and representational accounts

"Vulcan does not exist" can be interpreted as:

The name or representational practice "Vulcan" has no bearer.

This avoids an empty object-language name by ascending to talk about words, files, dossiers, or naming practices.

The account fits contexts of correction:

There is no such person as Santa Claus.

But object-level and metalinguistic claims can differ. The name might never have been introduced, yet Vulcan still would not have existed. A purely metalinguistic analysis can therefore have the wrong modal profile.

The best version may treat metalinguistic content as what speakers pragmatically communicate while leaving the literal semantics to another theory.

Truth in fiction

Empty-name theories must explain how fictional claims can be correct or incorrect. Holmes is a detective but not a dentist; both predicates are equally non-instantiated by a concrete Holmes.

Sources of fictional truth include:

  • explicit statements in a work;
  • consequences licensed by genre and background knowledge;
  • authorial prescriptions to imagine;
  • facts about an abstract character's encoded properties; and
  • truths at worlds most similar to the story as described.

Each faces cases of inconsistency, incompleteness, unreliable narrators, and truths not explicitly stated. Fictional truth is therefore not simply ordinary truth with an empty name inserted.

Anaphora and discourse

Empty names participate in cross-sentence anaphora:

A detective named Holmes lives at Baker Street. He plays the violin.

Dynamic semantics can introduce a discourse referent without committing to a concrete object in the ordinary domain. The discourse marker supports later pronouns while its ontological interpretation remains open.

This separates discourse coherence from successful worldly reference. A semantic representation can track an introduced role even when no real individual fills it.

The lesson generalizes to hypothetical and conditional discourse:

If Vulcan existed, it would affect Mercury's orbit.

Reference-like continuity need not imply actual denotation.

Pluralism is plausible because the cases differ. A failed astronomical posit, a deliberate fictional character, and a corrupted report need not receive identical semantics merely because none names an ordinary concrete object.

Division of labour with ontology

Semantic evidence constrains ontology but does not settle it. The fact that speakers quantify over fictional characters does not by itself prove that abstract artifacts exist; paraphrase or pretense may explain the discourse. Conversely, a prior refusal to countenance abstracta should not decide the linguistic analysis without argument.

The language-level question is:

What structure best explains understanding, composition, inference, and use when ordinary reference fails?

The ontological question is:

Which entities, if any, must exist for that explanation to be true?

Keeping them distinct prevents a preferred inventory from being smuggled into semantic premises while allowing successful semantics to provide genuine evidence.
