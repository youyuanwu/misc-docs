Theories of Reference

Language reaches beyond itself. A speaker can use "Venus" to discuss a planet, "water" to classify a substance, "that tree" to direct attention, and "the author of Middlemarch" to identify a person through a condition. The general problem of reference is to explain how expressions connect to objects, kinds, properties, and other semantic values.

Reference is not one uniform relation. Names, descriptions, indexicals, predicates, and natural-kind terms behave differently. Nor is reference the whole of meaning: co-referring expressions can differ in cognitive significance, and complete sentences do more than list their referents. A theory of reference is one component of a theory of language.

This page compares the principal families of account and establishes distinctions used throughout the folder. The following pages examine definite descriptions, proper names, empty names, natural-kind terms, indexicals and demonstratives, and reference indeterminacy.

Semantic reference, speaker reference, and identification

Three relations are easily conflated.

Semantic reference is the referent an expression has according to the language, construction, and context. On a direct-reference account, "Venus" semantically refers to Venus.

Speaker reference is the object a speaker intends to talk about on an occasion. A speaker who points to Ada while mistakenly saying "the person drinking a martini" may intend Ada even if she is drinking water.

Successful identification is an audience's recognition of the intended object. A speaker can refer successfully despite using a false description, or fail to direct an audience despite using a semantically correct name.

The distinctions allow mixed verdicts:

  • the expression has one semantic value;
  • the speaker intends another object;
  • the audience identifies a third;
  • the utterance may nevertheless communicate enough for the practical purpose.

A theory should say which relation it explains rather than use "refers" for all four.

Denotation, extension, and satisfaction

Formal semantics generalizes reference through category-sensitive notions.

  • A singular term may denote an object.
  • A one-place predicate has an extension, the objects satisfying it.
  • An n-place predicate has an extension of ordered n-tuples.
  • A sentence receives a truth value relative to an interpretation and circumstance.

For a model M:

[[Ada]]M=a,

[[runs]]M={x:x runs in M}.

Then "Ada runs" is true just in case:

a∈[[runs]]M.

This explains composition once an interpretation is supplied. It does not explain why the name used by a community denotes a or why the predicate has that extension. That is the metasemantic problem.

Descriptive theories

A descriptive theory holds that reference is fixed by descriptive material associated with an expression. A name might refer to whichever object uniquely satisfies a description or cluster of descriptions competent speakers associate with it.

The theory explains:

  • how a speaker identifies the referent;
  • why co-referring names can differ cognitively;
  • how names can be introduced by description; and
  • why an empty name can remain intelligible.

Its characteristic problems are:

  • speakers can refer despite ignorance or false belief;
  • different speakers associate different descriptions;
  • names and descriptions differ modally;
  • a description may identify the wrong object while the name retains its bearer; and
  • reference transmission often depends on other speakers rather than independent identification.

Russell's quantificational analysis of descriptions need not imply that every genuine name is descriptive. The relation between the two claims is examined in the next two pages.

Causal-historical theories

A causal-historical picture begins with an introduction or baptism and a chain of later uses. Speakers intend to use a name as members of the community from whom they received it use it. Reference can therefore survive widespread ignorance and error.

The picture explains why someone who knows only that Gödel was a mathematician can still refer to Gödel. It also supports social division of linguistic labour: speakers depend on others who can identify the bearer or kind more reliably.

Causal connection alone is too permissive. Many objects causally affect a use without being its referent. A chain must be structured by intentions to preserve or defer to a practice.

Further questions include:

  • What counts as a baptism?
  • Can reference shift gradually?
  • What happens when a chain branches?
  • Can a speaker intentionally break from inherited use?
  • How are reference errors distinguished from new naming?

Kripke presented a picture rather than a complete algorithm, leaving these as central metasemantic problems.

Direct-reference theories

On a direct-reference theory, the semantic contribution of a proper name or demonstrative is its referent, not a descriptive sense. The object itself enters the proposition expressed.

This explains rigidity: a name designates the same object across possible circumstances in which that object exists. "Aristotle might not have taught Alexander" concerns Aristotle in counterfactual worlds, not whoever happens there to satisfy a description.

Direct reference faces the cognitive problems identified by Sense and Reference:

  • informative identities between co-referring names;
  • belief reports in which substitution fails;
  • apparently meaningful empty names; and
  • differences in understanding among speakers using the same name.

Responses distinguish semantic content from modes of presentation, mental files, pragmatic information, or acquaintance relations. The gain is a simple account of truth-conditional contribution; the cost is relocating cognitive significance.

Intention-based accounts

Speaker intentions help determine reference, especially for demonstratives and local uses. A pointing gesture can be ambiguous among an object, its colour, its owner, or a part. What the speaker intends the audience to attend to matters.

Intention cannot be sufficient without constraint. A speaker cannot refer to an arbitrary distant object merely by privately intending it while using an unrelated name. Successful reference depends on recognizable context, conventions, causal relations, and the audience's reasonable interpretation.

The relevant intention is also difficult to specify without circularity:

The speaker intends "that" to refer to the object they intend to refer to.

A substantive account must characterize attention, demonstration, perceptual relation, or communicative plan without presupposing the reference relation being explained.

Use and social practice

Use-based accounts locate reference in public practices of applying, correcting, teaching, and deferring. A term's referent is constrained by how a community interacts with objects and treats applications as correct or mistaken.

This explains:

  • partial competence;
  • reference sustained across individuals;
  • expert correction;
  • historically changing classifications; and
  • the difference between private association and public meaning.

The risk is circularity or relativism. If correct use is defined only by communal use, the community cannot be systematically wrong. If external reality corrects the practice, the theory must specify the world-link in causal, informational, or truth-conditional terms.

Social practice is therefore often combined with causal history and environmental contact rather than offered as a self-contained replacement.

Reference fixing and meaning

An expression can have its reference fixed by a description without that description giving its meaning.

Suppose one metre is introduced as the length of a particular bar at a specified time. The description identifies a reference standard. It does not follow that "one metre" means the length of that bar at that time in every sentence. The bar could have been longer or shorter under different conditions, while one metre remains one metre.

Similarly, "Neptune" was introduced through theoretical description, but later uses can directly refer to the planet even if the original theory contained errors.

This distinction allows descriptive introductions within a non-descriptive semantics. It also separates epistemology from semantic contribution: a way of finding the referent need not be what the term contributes to propositions.

Reference and cognitive significance

A semantic theory must decide how much cognitive difference belongs inside content.

If names contribute only objects, "Hesperus is Phosphorus" and "Hesperus is Hesperus" express the same singular proposition. Their difference must lie in presentation, pragmatics, or the cognitive route by which the proposition is entertained.

If names contribute senses, the propositions differ, but reference becomes mediated by fine-grained entities whose identity is difficult to state.

Two-factor views combine:

mode of presentation+referent.

The mode explains rational perspective; the referent fixes ordinary truth-conditions. The question is whether both factors are semantic or whether one belongs to thought or pragmatics.

Reference failure

What happens when no object meets the conditions for reference?

Options include:

  • the expression lacks a semantic value;
  • the containing sentence lacks a truth value;
  • the term contributes a descriptive condition rather than an object;
  • a free logic permits an empty term;
  • the discourse operates under pretense;
  • the name refers to an abstract fictional artifact; or
  • the utterance is metalinguistically about a name or practice.

Reference failure tests a theory's architecture. A view designed only for successful ordinary naming may assign no content where speakers plainly reason and disagree.

The language-level alternatives are compared in Empty Names and Reference Failure; the ontology of fictional and nonexistent objects remains in Ontology.

Reference change

Reference can shift over time. "Madagascar" appears to have moved from part of the African mainland in an earlier use to the island now bearing the name. Scientific terms are retained while their extensions or associated theories change. Personal names can be transferred intentionally or confused across chains.

A rigid designator is rigid across counterfactual worlds at a time; rigidity does not imply immunity to historical semantic change.

Theories explain change differently:

  • descriptions change until a new object best fits;
  • causal chains are redirected or replaced;
  • communal correction establishes a new practice;
  • speaker intentions initiate a new convention; or
  • interpretation balances continuity against explanatory fit.

Hard cases may have no uniquely precise transition point. That does not show there was never a fact of reference; it may show that social practices change gradually.

Criteria for comparing theories

CriterionQuestion
Ignorance and errorCan speakers refer while knowing little or believing falsely?
Modal stabilityDoes the term track the same object across counterfactual circumstances?
Cognitive significanceCan the theory explain informative identities and belief reports?
Public transmissionHow is reference shared across speakers and generations?
Empty casesWhat content remains when reference fails?
ChangeHow can reference shift without every use becoming equivocal?
CompositionWhat value does the expression contribute to larger constructions?
Non-circular groundingWhat fixes reference without presupposing it?

No single theory dominates every row. Descriptive accounts illuminate identification and cognition; direct reference captures modal and truth-conditional behaviour; causal-historical and social accounts explain transmission; intentions explain local demonstration; formal semantics explains composition. The central question is whether these are rivals at one level or components at different levels.
