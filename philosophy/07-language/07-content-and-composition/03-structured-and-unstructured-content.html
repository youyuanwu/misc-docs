Structured and Unstructured Content

What distinguishes the proposition that Ada admires Ben from the proposition that Ben admires Ada? What distinguishes one necessary mathematical truth from another? A theory of content must be fine-grained enough for these differences while remaining shareable, compositional, and formally tractable.

Unstructured theories identify content with a condition or set of circumstances, most commonly a set of possible worlds. Structured theories let propositions contain constituents arranged in an ordered structure. Russellian structures contain objects and properties; Fregean structures contain modes of presentation.

The choice is not merely ontological. It determines how semantics handles belief, informativeness, modality, impossible contents, and the relationship between sentence syntax and proposition structure.

Unstructured possible-worlds content

A proposition can be represented as its truth-set:

[[p]]={w:p is true at w}.

Two sentences express the same proposition exactly when true at the same worlds.

This representation gives elegant operations:

[[p∧q]]=[[p]]∩[[q]],

[[¬p]]=W∖[[p]].

It is unstructured because the internal route by which the truth-set is specified does not enter the content. Only the division of worlds into truth and falsity matters.

For modality and information states, that abstraction is often a virtue.

Granularity

Possible-worlds content identifies all necessarily equivalent claims. Every necessary truth is true at every world:

[[2+2=4]]=[[Every group of prime order is cyclic]]=W.

Every impossible claim has the empty truth-set.

This is too coarse for:

  • belief and doubt;
  • discovery and proof;
  • explanation;
  • meaning of mathematical statements;
  • subject matter;
  • grounding; and
  • impossible supposition.

A person can know one theorem without knowing another. If belief were simple relation to a truth-set, logical omniscience would follow.

One response is to keep coarse semantic content while adding a separate cognitive representation. Another refines propositions themselves.

Russellian structured propositions

A Russellian proposition contains the objects and properties it concerns:

⟨admires,Ada,Ben⟩.

Constituent order distinguishes:

⟨admires,Ben,Ada⟩.

Names that co-refer contribute the same object, so ordinary singular propositions remain coarse with respect to modes of presentation.

Advantages:

  • direct reference is represented transparently;
  • syntax and semantic structure align;
  • necessarily equivalent sentences can differ in constituents;
  • de re content is straightforward; and
  • composition yields a structured result.

Pressures:

  • informative identities still collapse;
  • belief reports require guises or special attribution;
  • empty names leave missing constituents;
  • propositions involving contingent objects may not exist without them; and
  • unity requires more than an ordered list.

Fregean structured propositions

A Fregean proposition contains senses rather than referents. Co-referring names can contribute different modes of presentation:

⟨Sense(Hesperus),Identity,Sense(Phosphorus)⟩.

This distinguishes informative identity from trivial self-identity and handles opacity in attitude reports.

The cost is fine grain and ontology. What makes two senses identical? Speakers with different knowledge may associate different presentations while still agreeing. Senses must be public enough for shared content but fine enough for rational cognitive difference.

If every possible difference in understanding produces a new proposition, communication becomes fragile.

Neo-Russellian two-factor views

A hybrid keeps a Russellian proposition as public truth-conditional content while adding a mode of presentation at the level of cognition or utterance.

For belief:

Believes(S,p,m),

where p is a coarse proposition and m is the way the subject entertains it.

This explains how Lois can bear different attitudes toward one proposition under "Superman" and "Clark Kent." It preserves direct reference without treating every cognitive difference as a difference in public semantic content.

The challenge is compositional and evidential. How is m determined, and is it part of what words conventionally mean or a fact about the subject's mental file? If modes are needed systematically to predict truth of belief reports, excluding them from semantics may look stipulative.

The unity of the proposition

A proposition is not a mere collection:

{Ada,Ben,admires}

does not distinguish who admires whom or assert anything.

Structure must bind constituents into an assertoric whole. Possible accounts use:

  • ordered tuples;
  • syntactic or logical trees;
  • predication as a primitive;
  • acts of judgment;
  • types that constrain combination; or
  • facts or states of affairs.

Ordered tuples encode difference but may seem to replace rather than explain predication. Adding a relation of instantiation risks a regress: another relation is needed to connect it to the constituents.

Formal semantics can represent unity without settling its metaphysics. A typed function applied to an argument yields a value; whether the proposition literally has that structure is further.

Syntax and proposition structure

Structured theories often mirror syntax:

        admires
       /       \
     Ada       Ben

But syntax and content do not always align one-to-one:

  • active and passive sentences can express the same proposition;
  • pronouns depend on context;
  • quantifier scope can differ from surface order;
  • idioms have non-literal structure;
  • implicit arguments may enter content;
  • synonymous lexical items differ syntactically.

A proposition structure may correspond to logical form, an interpreted structure abstracting from surface grammar.

The danger is circularity: logical form is inferred from desired content, then invoked to explain that content. Independent linguistic evidence should constrain it.

Fine-grained algebraic contents

Structured propositions are not the only fine-grained option. Contents can be modelled as:

  • procedures or algorithms;
  • proofs;
  • equivalence classes under restricted transformations;
  • truthmaker conditions;
  • subject matters;
  • bilateral conditions of assertion and denial;
  • sets of impossible as well as possible worlds; or
  • structured intensions.

Each distinguishes contents that possible-world truth-sets collapse. They differ over which distinctions count.

No maximally fine-grained content is automatically desirable. If every syntactic difference yields a new proposition, obvious paraphrases never share content.

Attitude attribution

Attitudes supply the strongest test.

A theory should explain:

  • substitution failure for co-referring names;
  • belief in one necessary truth without all;
  • rational inconsistency under different presentations;
  • de re and de dicto readings;
  • partial understanding; and
  • communication of shared beliefs.

Fine-grained propositions explain failures of substitution by changing embedded content. Coarse theories explain them through modes of presentation or pragmatic standards for attribution.

Neither strategy is cost-free. Fine grain complicates sameness; coarse grain complicates the attitude relation.

Assertion and disagreement

Public assertion often tolerates differences in cognition. Two speakers can agree that Venus is visible while thinking of Venus under different descriptions.

This supports content coarser than individual mental representation. Yet disagreement can concern wording or conceptualization even when referents and extensions align.

A layered theory may distinguish:

  • public proposition asserted;
  • linguistic mode of presentation;
  • speaker's mental representation;
  • presupposed background; and
  • pragmatic implications.

The layers should be motivated by explanatory differences, not introduced whenever substitution causes trouble.

Content identity

Criteria of proposition identity vary by theory:

Syntactic fine grainsame interpreted logical form
Proof-theoreticsame canonical grounds and consequences
Impossible-worldtrue at the same possible and impossible worlds

Each supports different explanatory purposes. The question "Are these the same content?" is incomplete until the relevant role is specified.

Assessment

Unstructured contents excel at modal algebra and information update. Structured contents excel at compositional articulation and cognitive discrimination.

A plausible division of labour is:

  • use possible-world intensions where truth across possibilities is the target;
  • use structured semantic values for composition;
  • add modes of presentation for cognitive explanation;
  • use hyperintensional resources where necessary equivalence remains too coarse; and
  • avoid inferring ontology directly from formal representation.

The resulting pluralism is acceptable only if translations among levels are explicit. Otherwise "content" changes meaning whenever a counterexample appears.
