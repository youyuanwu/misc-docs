Intensionality and Possible-Worlds Content

The extension of "the tallest person" is whoever is tallest in the actual world. The same expression could pick out someone else had circumstances differed. The sentence "Ada might have won" is not evaluated only against actuality but against relevant possibilities. Belief reports resist substitution even when expressions have the same actual extension.

Intensional semantics represents these dependencies by assigning expressions values that vary across possible worlds, times, or other indices. An intension is, roughly, a function from circumstances of evaluation to extensions.

The apparatus unifies modality, tense, conditionals, and attitude contexts. It does not settle what possible worlds are or show that every distinction in meaning reduces to truth across possibilities.

Extension and intension

An expression's extension is its semantic value at a circumstance:

  • a name: an object;
  • a predicate: a set of objects;
  • a sentence: a truth value.

An intension maps circumstances to extensions. For a one-place predicate:

[[P]]int:w↦{x:x is P in w}.

For a sentence:

[[p]]int:w↦{1,0,​p is true at w,p is false at w.​

The sentence intension corresponds to a set of worlds. Predicate intensions vary their extensions across worlds.

Why extension is insufficient

"Creature with a heart" and "creature with kidneys" may have the same actual extension. Still, it is possible for a creature to have one without the other. Their intensions differ because their extensions diverge at some worlds.

Likewise, two actually true sentences need not have the same meaning. Intensions distinguish them by their modal profiles.

Actual extension is therefore too coarse for counterfactual evaluation and many semantic relations. Intension enriches content without yet introducing internal structure.

Modality

Possible-worlds semantics interprets necessity and possibility:

M,w⊨□piff∀v(wRv→M,v⊨p),

M,w⊨◊piff∃v(wRv∧M,v⊨p).

R is an accessibility relation encoding which worlds count as alternatives from w. Different constraints on R produce different modal logics.

Natural-language modality requires interpretation of the modal base and ordering source. "Must" can express logical, epistemic, legal, or practical necessity depending on context. Possible worlds supply the formal space; context helps determine which worlds are relevant.

Rigid and non-rigid terms

A rigid designator has the same referent at every world where that referent exists. A proper name such as "Ada" is standardly treated as rigid.

A description such as "the tallest person" is non-rigid: its extension can vary from world to world.

This difference explains:

Ada might not have been the tallest person.

The name continues to designate Ada while the description may designate someone else.

In intensional type theory, a rigid term has a constant individual concept; a non-rigid term maps worlds to different individuals.

De re and de dicto modality

Compare:

The tallest person might not have been tallest.

On a de re reading, a particular actual person is said to possibly lack the property. On a de dicto reading, it is possible that whoever is uniquely tallest is not tallest, which is contradictory.

The readings correspond to scope:

∃x(Tx∧◊¬Tx)

versus:

◊∃x(Tx∧¬Tx).

Intensional semantics makes scope interactions explicit. It does not by itself resolve the metaphysics of objects across worlds.

Attitude contexts

Belief reports are intensional:

Leila believes that Hesperus is visible.

Substituting "Phosphorus" can change truth despite co-reference. Treating embedded clauses as ordinary possible-world intensions still predicts equivalence if the names are rigid and co-refer.

Attitudes therefore require more than ordinary intensionality:

  • fine-grained modes of presentation;
  • structured propositions;
  • impossible worlds;
  • centered worlds;
  • context-sensitive report standards; or
  • relations to representations.

Possible worlds explain sensitivity to alternatives but not every kind of opacity.

Context and index

The context of utterance fixes indexical content; the index or circumstance of evaluation determines extension.

For "I might have been elsewhere":

  1. context fixes "I" as the actual speaker;
  2. modal evaluation varies worlds and locations;
  3. the content concerns that speaker across alternatives.

Conflating context with evaluation would let "I" shift to different speakers in each world.

Indices may contain:

⟨w,t,l,a⟩

for world, time, location, and agent. Whether all coordinates belong in one index depends on the phenomenon.

Tense

Temporal semantics can treat sentence extensions as varying across times as well as worlds:

[[p]](w,t).

Past and future operators shift the time of evaluation. Alternatively, tense can be represented through explicit quantification over times.

The formal choice affects iteration, temporal anaphora, and the interaction of tense with modality. It does not decide whether time genuinely passes or which ontology of time is correct. Those questions belong to Philosophy of Space and Time.

Conditionals

Counterfactuals can be analysed through closest antecedent worlds:

If the match had been struck, it would have lit.

The conditional is true when relevant closest worlds where the match is struck are worlds where it lights.

Similarity is context-sensitive and not reducible to crude overlap of facts. Laws, causal history, and conversational purpose affect which worlds count as closest.

Indicative conditionals may require a different treatment through probability, supposition, or information update. Possible worlds provide a common representation without guaranteeing one analysis.

Intensional entities and types

Montague-style semantics assigns higher-order types. If e is the type of individuals and t truth values, intensionalization adds a world type s:

  • proposition: ⟨s,t⟩;
  • property intension: ⟨s,⟨e,t⟩⟩;
  • individual concept: ⟨s,e⟩.

Lambda abstraction and function application compose these values. The formalism can represent quantification into modal contexts, attitudes, and intensional verbs.

Typing is a modelling discipline, not an ontology. A semantic value of type ⟨s,t⟩ need not imply that speakers mentally manipulate set-theoretic functions.

Does semantics require possible worlds?

Possible worlds can play several roles:

  • concrete universes;
  • abstract representations;
  • maximal states of affairs;
  • indices in a model;
  • consistent descriptions;
  • points in an information space.

Formal semantics needs objects with the right structure for evaluation. It need not decide whether they are concrete or metaphysically fundamental.

The ontological options and costs are developed in Possible Worlds.

Using a world variable is compatible with modal realism, ersatzism, fictionalism, or instrumental modelling, provided the interpretation of the formalism is made explicit.

Limits of intensionality

Possible-world intensions remain too coarse for:

  • distinct necessary truths;
  • distinct impossibilities;
  • mathematical cognition;
  • explanatory and grounding contexts;
  • quotation and syntactic form;
  • subject matter; and
  • some attitude reports.

These are hyperintensional phenomena. They require distinctions finer than necessary equivalence.

Intensional semantics should therefore be judged by target. It gives a powerful account of variation across possibilities. It is not a universal identity criterion for every content used in explanation.

Assessment

Intensional semantics earns its central place because it:

  • distinguishes actual extension from modal profile;
  • gives compositional treatments of modality and tense;
  • represents rigidity and scope;
  • supplies formal values for propositions and properties;
  • integrates context with circumstances of evaluation; and
  • provides a common framework for counterfactuals and attitudes.

Its open debts are metasemantic and hyperintensional. The framework assumes interpretations of primitive expressions and a space of indices, and it collapses contents that agree at every possible world. Those are reasons to supplement the framework, not to discard the distinctions it makes precise.
