Semantic Externalism

Two speakers can be internally alike and use the same sounds while their words differ in reference. A speaker can competently use a term while knowing less than experts who help sustain it. A community can discover that its identifying beliefs were mistaken without thereby having spoken about nothing.

Semantic externalism holds that linguistic content depends partly on relations to the environment, causal history, or social practice. Meaning is not wholly fixed by an individual speaker's intrinsic state.

The view comes in different forms. Environmental externalism concerns contact with substances and objects. Social externalism concerns deference and division of linguistic labour. Historical externalism concerns chains through which uses are transmitted. These mechanisms can combine but should not be treated as one vague appeal to "context."

The externalist thesis

Let A and B be internally identical speakers. Internalism requires:

Intrinsic(A)=Intrinsic(B)→Content(A)=Content(B).

Externalism denies this implication. If their environments, communities, or histories differ appropriately, their expressions can differ in content.

The claim is constitutive, not merely causal. Everyone agrees that environments cause language learning. The externalist says some external relations partly make the content what it is.

Environmental externalism

Putnam's Twin Earth case supplies the paradigm. Earth speakers use "water" for H2​O; internally identical Twin Earth speakers use the same sound for XYZ, the superficially identical local liquid.

Before either community knows chemistry:

[[waterE​]]=H2​O,[[waterT​]]=XYZ.

Reference depends on the substance historically sampled, not only on associated descriptions. Internal duplicates can therefore mean different things.

The details of natural-kind reference are developed in Natural-Kind Terms and Semantic Externalism.

Social externalism

Burge's arthritis case shifts dependence from natural environment to linguistic community. A patient mistakenly applies "arthritis" to a thigh ailment. Actual expert usage restricts arthritis to joints, so the speaker's belief is false.

In a counterfactual community where the same sound covers joint and muscle ailments, an intrinsic duplicate expresses a different content.

Social externalism explains:

  • partial understanding;
  • expert correction;
  • use of technical vocabulary by nonexperts;
  • stable disagreement despite different beliefs; and
  • semantic error within competent participation.

The challenge is identifying the relevant community when usage is contested, multilingual, interdisciplinary, or changing.

Historical externalism

Proper-name reference depends on chains of transmission. A speaker using "Gödel" may know almost nothing uniquely identifying, yet intends to use the name as inherited from the community.

Current intrinsic state and present environment may not suffice. Reference depends on:

  • an introduction or baptism;
  • causal transmission;
  • deference to earlier users;
  • intentions to preserve a practice; and
  • later correction or redirection.

Historical externalism permits current duplicates with different histories to refer differently. It also raises slow-switching questions about how quickly content changes after a speaker enters a new environment.

Linguistic content versus mental content

Externalism about public words does not automatically establish externalism about every thought.

A speaker's word "water" can have externally fixed reference while their internal conceptual role remains shared with a Twin Earth duplicate. A two-factor view distinguishes:

narrow cognitive role+external linguistic reference=wide content.

Whether beliefs themselves have wide content is developed in Internalism and Externalism about Mental Content.

The linguistic thesis is independently significant: public terms can outrun what any one speaker knows.

Deference

Speakers routinely defer:

By "elm" I mean the kind experts call elm, even though I cannot distinguish elms from beeches.

Deference is not blind repetition. Competence may require:

  • recognizing the word's general category;
  • intending to participate in the practice;
  • accepting correction from appropriate sources;
  • using enough stereotype to coordinate; and
  • preserving inferential and grammatical roles.

A speaker who copies a word with no idea whether it names a tree, disease, or person may not possess ordinary competence.

Externalism needs a threshold between partial understanding and mere parroting.

Contested expertise

Communities contain disagreement about:

  • disease categories;
  • biological species;
  • legal terms;
  • social classifications;
  • technical standards; and
  • ordinary usage.

There may be no single expert group. Reference can be negotiated among scientific, legal, practical, and everyday practices with different purposes.

Responses include:

  • context selects a subcommunity;
  • one practice has authority for a use;
  • the term is polysemous;
  • reference is partly indeterminate;
  • usage is undergoing change; or
  • external reality constrains all groups despite conceptual difference.

Social externalism should not treat "the community" as a unitary mind.

Communication across environments

Can Earth and Twin Earth speakers disagree when one says "Water is H2​O" and the other denies it? If their terms have different contents, they may talk past one another.

Actual cross-community communication can establish a broader context:

  • speakers learn the difference between water and twater;
  • terms are translated or qualified;
  • demonstrative and descriptive resources identify samples;
  • one term may be borrowed into the other language.

Externalism predicts that apparent verbal agreement or disagreement must be interpreted relative to reference-fixing practices. It does not make communication impossible; it makes translation substantive.

Self-knowledge

A speaker can know:

I mean water by "water"

without knowing chemistry or the full social history of the term. Does externalism undermine first-person authority?

The standard distinction is:

  • knowing what word one is using and what one intends to say;
  • knowing the external facts that make the word have that content.

A speaker can possess the first without the second. Their self-ascription is itself expressed with the externally fixed term.

Still, externalism limits transparency. Speakers can be wrong about extension, community standards, or whether their use continues an inherited practice. Semantic self-knowledge is not complete metasemantic knowledge.

Slow switching

Suppose an Earth speaker moves unknowingly to Twin Earth. When does "water" begin to refer to XYZ?

Possible answers:

  • immediately, through current environment;
  • after sufficient causal interaction;
  • after adopting local deference;
  • gradually;
  • never for retained uses tied to Earth;
  • differently for old memories and new claims.

No one threshold follows from the externalist slogan. Different kinds of term may weight origin, current contact, and social integration differently.

Slow switching shows that content can be historically extended and temporally vague. That is compatible with determinacy in ordinary settled cases.

Semantic change

Externalism allows a community to change reference through sustained practice. Scientific reclassification, borrowing, metaphorical extension, and naming errors can redirect a term.

Causal continuity alone cannot prevent change. The term "Madagascar" can acquire a new geographical bearer despite originating in a different use. Conversely, changing descriptions need not change reference if the community continues to investigate one object.

Reference change depends on a balance of:

  • historical chain;
  • current applications;
  • explicit intentions;
  • expert standards;
  • causal interaction;
  • inferential role; and
  • interpretive continuity.

Externalism is therefore compatible with dynamic language, not only fixed baptisms.

Externalism and compositionality

Compositional semantics assigns values to lexical items and combines them. Externalism concerns how some lexical values are fixed.

The projects fit naturally:

  1. environment and community partly determine [[water]];
  2. syntax and semantic rules combine that value into sentence content;
  3. context determines occasion-specific parameters;
  4. pragmatics explains further communication.

Externalism does not replace formal semantics. It supplies part of its metasemantics.

The arrangement also explains why internally identical speakers can compute similar structures whose resulting truth-conditions differ because their lexical anchors differ.

Narrow content

Some theorists retain narrow content shared by intrinsic duplicates. Candidates include:

  • conceptual role;
  • a function from contexts to wide contents;
  • centered-world content;
  • notional roles in reasoning;
  • computational syntax.

Narrow content explains identical local reasoning and behaviour. Wide content explains reference, truth, and successful interaction.

Critics ask whether a reference-neutral function is genuinely content. Supporters answer that psychological explanation and public truth-conditions need different grains.

The debate mirrors structured and unstructured content: one content may not serve every explanatory role.

Objections

The intuition pump

Twin Earth judgments may reflect theoretical priming rather than ordinary semantics. Speakers can instead treat "water" as a functional category or allow both H2​O and XYZ under a broad term.

Externalism should therefore be tested term by term. The case establishes a possible externalist mechanism more clearly than a universal rule.

Circularity

To specify the relevant environment or community, theorists use semantic descriptions: the experts on arthritis, samples of water, the chain preserving this name. The metasemantics may presuppose reference.

Holistic mutual constraint can be legitimate, but a promised reduction is incomplete.

Causal irrelevance

External differences can change content without changing immediate processing. External content may seem irrelevant to behavioural causation.

This is sharper for mental content. For language, external content explains truth, successful coordination, correction, and long-run interaction even if token production depends on local mechanisms.

Assessment

Semantic externalism establishes that linguistic content can depend on more than an individual's intrinsic psychology. Its mechanisms should remain differentiated:

External factorParadigmMain question
EnvironmentTwin Earthwhich worldly kind anchors the term?
Communityarthritis and expert deferencewhich practice has authority?
Historyproper-name chainswhat preserves or redirects transmission?
Functionscientific and practical roleswhich use is reference-fixing?

The strongest position is relational but not indiscriminate. Speakers, communities, histories, and environments jointly constrain meaning. Externalism should specify the relation for each class of expression rather than treating everything outside the head as one undifferentiated source.
