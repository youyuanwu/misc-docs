Hyperintensionality

Two claims can be true in exactly the same possible worlds and still differ in meaning, belief, explanation, or subject matter. Every necessary truth is true at every possible world, yet understanding one theorem does not amount to understanding all mathematics. Every impossibility is true at no possible world, yet "There is a largest prime" and "Some square is circular" are different claims.

Contexts that distinguish necessarily equivalent contents are hyperintensional. They require semantic structure finer than possible-world intensions.

Hyperintensionality is not one phenomenon with one established solution. Belief, grounding, explanation, quotation, and subject matter may discriminate contents for different reasons. A theory should preserve exactly the distinctions relevant to its target rather than maximize fine grain without limit.

The collapse under necessary equivalence

If propositions are sets of possible worlds:

[[p]]={w:p is true at w},

then:

□(p↔q)→[[p]]=[[q]].

All necessary truths become W, the set of all worlds. All impossible propositions become ∅.

This is acceptable for modal status but inadequate when the route, structure, grounds, or subject of truth matters.

Mathematical belief

A person may know:

2+2=4

without knowing:

Every finite division ring is a field.

Both are necessary if true. Possible-world content predicts identity and therefore logical omniscience for anyone related to the first proposition.

Responses include:

  • structured propositions with different constituents;
  • proof-sensitive content;
  • modes of presentation;
  • impossible worlds representing incomplete theories;
  • syntactic or algorithmic representations; and
  • treating belief as more than relation to coarse propositions.

The case shows that epistemic content tracks how truth is represented or established, not only where it is true.

Impossible contents

Reasoners distinguish and compare impossibilities:

  • If there were a largest prime, it would be odd or equal to 2.
  • A square circle would have conflicting geometrical properties.
  • If 2+2 were 5, familiar arithmetic would change in particular ways.

Counterpossible conditionals with impossible antecedents are not always judged vacuously true. Standard possible-world semantics treats every impossible antecedent as having no worlds and therefore cannot rank alternatives.

Impossible-world semantics adds worlds where logical, mathematical, or conceptual falsehoods hold. Different impossibilities can then have different truth sets.

The cost is specifying impossible worlds without trivializing them. If contradiction entails everything at each world, distinctions collapse again. Non-classical logics or primitive impossible states are often used.

Grounding

Grounding is hyperintensional. Even if:

p↔q

is necessary, the fact that p may ground r while the fact that q does not.

For example, a set's singleton exists because the set exists, not because an unrelated necessary mathematical truth holds. Necessary equivalence does not preserve explanatory dependence.

Truth sets record covariation across worlds; grounding records direction and basis. Adding worlds alone may not capture it unless worlds contain structured facts or grounding relations.

The metaphysics of grounding remains in Grounding. The semantic lesson is that "because" and "in virtue of" create contexts more discriminating than modality.

Explanation

Explanations can differ despite equivalent explananda and explanantia.

The flagpole's height and the sun's angle explain the shadow's length.

The shadow's length and sun's angle may mathematically determine the pole's height, but the reverse derivation is not the same causal explanation.

Explanatory vocabulary tracks direction, relevance, mechanism, and dependence. Replacing a statement by a necessary equivalent can destroy those relations.

A hyperintensional semantics for "because" may use:

  • structured facts;
  • causal models;
  • proof trees;
  • relevance relations;
  • grounding structures; or
  • questions under discussion.

No single truth-set contains all this information.

Subject matter

Two necessary truths can be about different topics. "All bachelors are unmarried" concerns marital status; a theorem of group theory concerns algebra. Their modal profiles alone do not identify subject matter.

Subject-matter theories represent content through:

  • partitions of possibilities by relevant variables;
  • sets of questions or issues;
  • truthmakers and falsemakers;
  • vocabularies and conceptual structures; or
  • dimensions along which information varies.

Topic matters for relevance, aboutness, and conversation. An answer can be true yet irrelevant because it addresses the wrong issue.

Structured propositions

Structured propositions distinguish contents by constituents and arrangement:

⟨Plus,2,2,4⟩=⟨Group,PrimeOrder,Cyclic⟩.

This handles many mathematical and belief cases. It also makes content sensitive to the semantic structure selected by analysis.

Problems include:

  • synonymous constituents can produce distinct structures;
  • one claim can have multiple equivalent formulations;
  • structure may be too syntactic;
  • identity conditions for properties or senses remain difficult; and
  • grounding or explanation may depend on more than constituent order.

Structure is a family of solutions, not one granularity.

Fregean senses and modes of presentation

Fregean senses distinguish necessarily coextensive expressions by how referents are presented. They naturally explain cognitive significance and belief.

If senses are individuated by every possible difference in understanding, content becomes extremely fine-grained. If coarser, substitution problems reappear.

A two-factor approach can retain:

  • coarse truth-conditional content for public assertion;
  • fine-grained modes for cognition and attitudes.

This avoids requiring one proposition to serve incompatible identity criteria, but it must explain how the factors combine.

Proof and procedure

For mathematics and computation, content can be tied to proof conditions or procedures. Two necessarily true formulas may require different proofs, algorithms, or normalization paths.

Proof-theoretic semantics gives logical expressions meaning through introduction and elimination rules. Type theory distinguishes terms inhabiting the same proposition or type.

Procedure-sensitive content explains cognitive effort and constructive information. It may over-distinguish proofs when ordinary speakers regard different derivations as establishing the same claim.

The relevant equality can itself vary:

  • syntactic identity;
  • beta-eta equivalence;
  • proof identity;
  • homotopy or higher equality;
  • interderivability.

Fine grain must be chosen for purpose.

Truthmakers and falsemakers

A proposition may be represented through what makes it true or false rather than the worlds where it is true.

Truthmaker semantics can distinguish claims that are true at the same worlds but in virtue of different portions of reality. It can also model subject matter and relevance.

Challenges include:

  • not every truth has an obvious minimal truthmaker;
  • negative and universal claims are difficult;
  • truthmaker ontology is controversial;
  • multiple independent truthmakers complicate identity; and
  • semantic explanation inherits metaphysical commitments.

Formal utility should be separated from realism about the represented truthmakers.

Impossible worlds

Impossible worlds generalize possible-world semantics:

[[p]]={w∈WP​∪WI​:p is true at w}.

WP​ contains possible worlds and WI​ impossible worlds.

They can model:

  • inconsistent beliefs;
  • counterpossibles;
  • logical ignorance;
  • distinct impossibilities;
  • information states violating laws.

The space is flexible enough to encode arbitrary distinctions. That is both strength and danger. Without constraints on impossible worlds and accessibility, explanatory content can be placed into the model by hand.

Hyperintensional operators

Expressions creating hyperintensional contexts include:

  • believes;
  • knows;
  • explains;
  • because;
  • grounds;
  • is relevant to;
  • is about;
  • proves;
  • means; and
  • according to a theory.

They need not share one semantics. "Believes" concerns cognitive representation, "grounds" metaphysical dependence, and "proves" formal derivation.

The common label states a negative fact: substitution of necessary equivalents can fail. Positive theories should remain operator-sensitive.

Compositionality

Hyperintensional content must still compose. If semantic values become arbitrarily fine-grained objects, combination rules may lose generality.

Options include:

  • typed structured meanings;
  • algebraic operations on proof objects;
  • compositional impossible-world intensions;
  • syntax-sensitive quotation-like values;
  • structured truthmaker conditions; and
  • multi-dimensional semantics with coarse and fine components.

A multi-dimensional value might be:

⟨truth-condition,mode,subject matter⟩.

Different operators select different coordinates. This preserves structure but raises questions about which dimensions are conventional.

How fine is fine enough?

No theory should distinguish contents merely because it can. Criteria include:

  1. Does the distinction affect truth, inference, explanation, or rational action?
  2. Is it stable across paraphrase and translation?
  3. Can speakers manifest sensitivity to it?
  4. Does it compose systematically?
  5. Does it predict substitution judgments?
  6. Is the added structure independently motivated?

Maximal granularity makes every expression express a unique content and destroys generalization. Minimal granularity collapses cognition and explanation. The useful level depends on the explanatory task.

Assessment

Hyperintensionality establishes that possible-world truth-conditions do not exhaust every notion of content. It does not establish one universal replacement.

A disciplined pluralism uses:

  • possible-world intensions for modality;
  • structured content for composition;
  • modes of presentation for cognition;
  • proof objects for constructive reasoning;
  • grounding or causal structures for explanation; and
  • subject-matter representations for relevance.

The remaining philosophical question is whether these are dimensions of one content, several related contents, or tools that model different practices without corresponding to a single semantic entity.
