The Normativity of Meaning

If "red" means red, applying it to a blue object is incorrect. If "and" expresses conjunction, certain inferences are valid and others invalid. Understanding seems to set standards extending beyond what a speaker happens to do.

This motivates the claim that meaning is normative. But several claims hide under that label. Meaning may supply standards of correctness without generating moral or all-things-considered obligations. Norms may govern assertion, belief, or inference rather than constitute meaning itself. Actual communal practice may sustain standards without making every common use correct.

The debate asks both whether semantic facts are normative and whether normative facts can be explained naturalistically.

Four kinds of normativity

Correctness

Meaning distinguishes correct from incorrect application:

"red" applies correctly to xiffx is red.

This is the least controversial sense. Semantic content supplies correctness conditions.

Reasons

If a speaker means conjunction by "and," they have a reason to infer p from p∧q. Meaning may guide reasoning.

Obligation

A stronger thesis says a speaker ought to use expressions according to their meanings. This can conflict with other reasons: someone may have prudential or moral reason to lie, remain silent, or deliberately misuse a word.

Commitment

By asserting a sentence, a speaker undertakes inferential and justificatory commitments. These norms arise from an act performed with meaningful language, not necessarily from lexical meaning alone.

Arguments often move illicitly from correctness to obligation. The distinctions should be preserved.

Correctness conditions

Every representational content appears to permit correctness and error. A map can misrepresent a road, a belief can be false, and a word can be misapplied.

One might say this normativity is merely classificatory:

Given that "red" means red, red applications are correct and non-red applications incorrect.

No categorical command follows. The relation resembles a standard of functional success rather than a moral requirement.

If this thin normativity is all that meaning requires, many naturalistic theories can accommodate it by explaining how content determines satisfaction conditions.

Kripke's normativity argument

Rule-following scepticism pressures dispositional accounts. A speaker may be disposed to make mistakes. If meaning were identical with actual disposition, whatever answer the speaker would give would become correct by definition.

Meaning addition determines that 68+57 should be 125 even if the speaker is disposed to answer 5. Semantic content appears to set a standard beyond behaviour.

The argument shows:

  • actual performance does not exhaust correctness;
  • error must remain possible;
  • idealization needs a non-circular standard; and
  • finite dispositions underdetermine unbounded rules.

It does not by itself establish irreducible obligations. A naturalistic account might explain correctness through function, history, or optimal interpretation rather than raw disposition.

See Meaning Scepticism and Rule-Following.

Guidance

For a rule to guide, the speaker must in some sense be sensitive to it. A purely external relation that never affects reasoning may determine correctness without explaining competent action.

Guidance can involve:

  • explicit consultation of a rule;
  • tacit practical mastery;
  • dispositions shaped by training;
  • sensitivity to correction;
  • inferential integration;
  • representation of goals and standards.

Requiring explicit interpretation creates a regress, because the interpretation itself needs guidance. Practical competence must eventually operate without a further represented rule.

Meaning can guide through acquired capacity rather than conscious instruction.

Norms of assertion

Assertion is governed by proposed norms:

  • assert only what is true;
  • assert only what you know;
  • assert only what you reasonably believe;
  • assert only what is warranted enough for the context.

These norms concern a speech act. They should not be confused with the meaning of the sentence asserted.

A liar can use words with their ordinary meanings while violating a truth or knowledge norm. Semantic competence is compatible with assertoric misconduct.

Conversely, a sentence can be meaningful even if never assertible, as in fiction, supposition, or quotation. Norms of assertion cannot define all linguistic meaning without extension.

Norms of belief and inference

Beliefs are evaluated for truth, rationality, consistency, and evidential support. Inferences are evaluated for validity or strength.

Inferentialism makes some of these relations content-constitutive. To mean conjunction is partly to accept its introduction and elimination rules. Other theories say meaning first determines content, while independent logical norms evaluate transitions among contents.

The explanatory order matters:

  1. Meaning-first: expressions have contents; logic states truth-preserving transitions.
  2. Inference-first: rules of correct transition constitute the contents.

Proof-theoretic and model-theoretic semantics embody the competing priorities while often validating the same logic.

Constitutive norms

A constitutive norm helps make an activity the activity it is. Rules of chess do not merely regulate an independently existing practice; they partly constitute playing chess.

Linguistic analogues might include:

  • using assertions as commitments;
  • preserving inferential roles;
  • treating correction as answerable to shared standards;
  • coordinating reference;
  • distinguishing truth from falsity.

If semantic norms are constitutive, systematic violation can mean failure to participate rather than merely poor participation.

The analogy has limits. Natural languages evolve without a fixed rulebook, tolerate variation, and allow speakers to innovate. Constitutive norms may be multiple, defeasible, and reconstructed from practice.

Community practice

Public correction appears to ground semantic standards. Learners are trained, mistakes are corrected, and competent speakers converge on patterns.

Community accounts explain:

  • publicity;
  • shared standards;
  • learning;
  • norm transmission;
  • correction of individual idiosyncrasy.

They face the community error problem. A whole community can classify badly, reason invalidly, or revise its language. If whatever the group does is correct, systematic error disappears at the social level.

A practice can contain internal standards that participants fail to satisfy. It can also be answerable to environmental success, evidence, and logical relations. Social constitution need not imply communal infallibility.

Convention

Conventions create expectations and conditional reasons to conform. If everyone uses "red" for red, each speaker has reason to do so when coordination matters.

This explains some normative force:

  • deviation impedes communication;
  • shared practice creates reliance;
  • explicit stipulations generate local obligations;
  • participants can criticize unannounced changes.

But conventional reasons are often instrumental. They depend on a desire to coordinate. Semantic correctness appears to remain even when a speaker wants to confuse.

Convention explains why a standard is socially sustained more readily than why it has the specific extension it does or why truth matters.

Biological function

Teleosemantic theories ground representational correctness in biological function. A state can misrepresent when it fails to indicate what its selected function is to indicate.

For language, biological function may explain capacities for communication and learning, but particular lexical meanings are historically and culturally variable. "Electron" has no direct evolutionary function.

Cultural selection and learning history can generalize the model:

  • forms persist because they coordinate;
  • correction stabilizes functions;
  • conventions reproduce across speakers.

Functional history supplies a natural standard beyond current disposition. It may underdescribe inferential and truth-conditional detail.

Rational interpretation

Interpretivist theories assign meanings that best make speakers coherent, responsive to evidence, and largely correct about their environment.

Norms enter the constitutive interpretation:

  • logical consistency;
  • rational action;
  • charity;
  • truthfulness;
  • evidential sensitivity.

This explains why radically irrational assignments are poor interpretations. It also risks idealizing away real irrationality or imposing the interpreter's norms.

A constrained interpretation allows local error against a background of intelligible practice. The relevant norms need not be perfectly obeyed to help constitute content.

Naturalism and the open-question pressure

A reductive naturalist seeks facts about:

  • dispositions;
  • causal covariance;
  • biological function;
  • social convention;
  • training history;
  • optimal interpretation.

The normativist argues that none entails what a speaker ought to do without a normative premise.

This resembles a general is-ought gap. The naturalist can reply that semantic correctness is not categorical obligation. Functional and relational facts can constitute standards in the same way facts about a heart's function distinguish normal from malfunctioning performance.

The debate partly turns on how strong the target normativity is. Thin correctness is easier to naturalize than unconditional reasons.

Meaning without obligation

One deflationary position says meaning is not intrinsically reason-giving:

  • meaning determines truth or satisfaction conditions;
  • agents have independent epistemic and practical reasons to pursue truth or communication;
  • those reasons generate norms for use;
  • no special semantic "ought" is required.

If "red" means red, calling a blue thing red is semantically incorrect. Whether the speaker ought not do so depends on their purposes and circumstances.

This preserves correctness while avoiding mysterious categorical prescriptions. Critics respond that correctness itself remains a normative relation in need of explanation.

Semantic change and innovation

Speakers intentionally violate inherited use to create metaphor, technical terminology, or reform. At what point does incorrect use become a new meaning?

Factors include:

  • recognizability;
  • uptake;
  • persistence;
  • systematic inferential role;
  • explicit stipulation;
  • practical success;
  • integration into communal correction.

Innovation shows that norms are not static. A speaker can violate an old standard while proposing a new one. Metalinguistic negotiation distinguishes:

This is what the word currently means

from:

This is how we should use it.

Semantic description and conceptual recommendation are different normative projects.

The safest conclusion is plural:

  1. Meaning supplies standards of semantic correctness.
  2. Speech acts and beliefs are governed by additional epistemic and social norms.
  3. Correctness does not automatically generate categorical obligation.
  4. Public practice is essential to learning and correction but not infallible.
  5. Naturalistic facts can explain much of norm acquisition and function, while the reduction of every normative relation remains contested.

Calling meaning "normative" should begin rather than end the analysis. The question is which norm, governing what act, with what force, and grounded by which facts.
