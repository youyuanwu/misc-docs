Propositions and Truth-Bearers

Different sentences can say the same thing:

Snow is white.

Schnee ist weiss.

A person can believe what another asserts despite speaking a different language. A claim can remain true after the utterance has ended. These observations motivate propositions: shareable, truth-evaluable contents expressed by sentences and targeted by attitudes.

Propositions are assigned several roles, and no role automatically entails the others. They may be sentence meanings, assertion contents, objects of belief, bearers of truth, and values manipulated by formal semantics. A theory must determine whether one entity can do all this work and at what grain.

This page concerns semantic roles. The question what propositions are - abstract objects, structures, sets of worlds, facts, or dispensable posits - remains in Propositions and Other Abstracta.

Why posit propositions?

Cross-linguistic sameness

Two non-synonymous sentence types in different languages can express the same content. The proposition is intended to be what they share.

Translation is rarely exact, so sameness may be context-sensitive or partial. Still, the practice of translation presupposes comparison at a level beyond inscription and sound.

Attitudes

Belief, hope, doubt, and fear appear relational:

Believes(Ada,p).

Different people can believe p, and one person can entertain it through different sentences. Propositions provide objects for these attitudes.

Assertion and disagreement

Speakers using different words can assert the same thing, agree with it, or contradict it. What is asserted must be distinguishable from the physical utterance and sentence type.

Truth and falsity

An utterance event exists at a time and place, but what it says may be timelessly true or false. Propositions are candidates for the primary truth-bearers.

Quantification

Ordinary discourse appears to quantify over contents:

  • Everything Ada said was true.
  • Ben believes something Chandra denies.
  • There are claims no one has expressed.

Paraphrase may avoid commitment, but the apparent explanatory utility is substantial.

Sentence types and tokens

A sentence token is a concrete inscription or utterance. A sentence type is the repeatable linguistic pattern instantiated by tokens.

Neither straightforwardly serves as the content.

Tokens can be true only derivatively: a sound event is not itself how the world is. Types are context-sensitive. The type "I am tired" can be true as uttered by Ada and false as uttered by Ben at the same time.

An interpreted sentence in context is a stronger candidate:

⟨s,c,L⟩,

where s is a sentence, c a context, and L an interpreted language. This can do much proposition-like work without positing an independent abstract entity.

It may still be too language-bound to explain cross-linguistic sameness and unexpressed contents.

Utterance contents

The proposition expressed by an utterance depends on sentence meaning and context:

Content(u)=Character(s)(c).

An utterance of "I am here now" yields a content involving its speaker, location, and time. Different tokens of the same sentence type express different propositions.

This content should be separated from:

  • implicatures;
  • presupposed background;
  • illocutionary force;
  • emotional or social effects; and
  • every proposition an audience can infer.

Otherwise "the proposition expressed" expands to everything communicated.

Propositions and facts

A proposition can be false; a fact cannot. The proposition that snow is black is a possible object of belief but not a fact.

One common distinction is:

  • a proposition represents a way things are;
  • a fact is a proposition's obtaining or the worldly state making it true.

Truthmaker theories instead relate propositions to entities that make them true. The ontology and explanatory role of facts are treated in Facts and States of Affairs and Truthmakers.

Semantics need not decide that every true proposition corresponds to one fact. It must specify what truth applies to and how that content is evaluated.

Propositions as sets of worlds

In intensional semantics, a proposition is often represented as the set of worlds at which it is true:

[[p]]={w:p is true at w}.

This makes:

  • entailment subset inclusion;
  • conjunction intersection;
  • disjunction union;
  • negation complement;
  • possibility non-emptiness; and
  • necessity truth at every world.

The representation is elegant for modal semantics. It is coarse-grained: necessarily equivalent propositions are identical. Every necessary truth corresponds to the set of all worlds.

That is often unacceptable for belief and explanation. A person can believe one mathematical truth without believing every necessary truth.

Structured propositions

A structured proposition contains constituents arranged in a way paralleling sentence structure.

For "Ada admires Ben":

⟨admires,Ada,Ben⟩

differs from:

⟨admires,Ben,Ada⟩.

Russellian propositions contain objects and properties. Fregean propositions contain senses or modes of presentation.

Structure distinguishes necessarily equivalent and co-referring contents more finely. It faces the unity problem: a proposition is not merely a list. What makes the constituents assertorically combined rather than collected?

The alternatives are compared in Structured and Unstructured Content.

Truth-bearers

Candidates for primary truth-bearers include:

  • sentence tokens;
  • sentence types relative to languages;
  • utterances;
  • statements;
  • judgments;
  • beliefs;
  • propositions; and
  • facts.

Propositions have advantages:

  • one bearer can be expressed many times;
  • truth does not vary with accidental features of tokens;
  • false contents exist as candidates for evaluation;
  • attitudes and assertions can share an object.

Problems include:

  • criteria of proposition identity;
  • ontology and epistemic access;
  • context sensitivity;
  • granularity;
  • unexpressed propositions; and
  • whether all meaningful acts express propositions.

Pluralism is possible. Propositions may be primary bearers while sentences and beliefs are derivatively true when they express or have a true proposition as content.

Objects of belief

Substitution creates pressure on proposition grain:

Lois believes that Superman can fly.

may be true while:

Lois believes that Clark Kent can fly.

is false. If the embedded sentences express the same coarse Russellian proposition, belief cannot be a simple two-place relation between person and proposition.

Responses include:

  • fine-grained Fregean propositions;
  • a three-place relation involving a mode of presentation;
  • mental files or guises;
  • context-sensitive attitude attribution;
  • structured propositions containing different name meanings; and
  • pragmatic explanations of reports.

The theory of belief may need more structure than the semantics of ordinary extensional sentences. It does not follow that one notion of proposition must serve every purpose.

Assertion content

Assertion undertakes commitment to a content. But how much content is asserted?

Suppose someone says:

It is raining.

Possibilities include:

  • a minimal proposition that it is raining at an unspecified place;
  • a proposition containing the contextually relevant location;
  • a richer proposition about present local conditions;
  • no complete proposition until pragmatic enrichment occurs.

The choice bears on contextualism and the semantics-pragmatics boundary. Assertion content should be rich enough to evaluate but not automatically include every implicature.

Presuppositions complicate the picture: they may be commitments without being part of the main asserted proposition.

Non-declaratives

Questions and imperatives challenge proposition-centered semantics.

A question may denote:

  • a set of possible answers;
  • an issue partition over worlds;
  • an inquisitive content; or
  • a function from answers to propositions.

An imperative may express:

  • a desired or commanded proposition;
  • a property of actions;
  • a preference update; or
  • a directive force applied to propositional content.

Propositions remain useful components without exhausting meaning. A complete theory distinguishes content from force and generalizes semantic values by sentence type.

Unexpressed and impossible propositions

Are there propositions no actual or possible language expresses? If propositions are language-independent ways things could be, likely yes. If they are abstractions from linguistic practice, perhaps not.

Impossible contents create a sharper issue. "There is a largest prime" is impossible but meaningful. Sets of possible worlds collapse all impossibilities into the empty set. Structured or impossible-world accounts preserve differences.

The choice depends on the explanatory target:

  • modal evaluation may need only possible-world content;
  • belief and reasoning require finer distinctions;
  • linguistic understanding requires systematic structure;
  • metaphysics may seek language-independent truth-bearers.

Eliminating propositions

A nominalist may replace propositions with:

  • interpreted sentence types;
  • equivalence classes of synonymous sentences;
  • utterance-event types;
  • facts about speakers' dispositions;
  • substitutional quantification over sentences; or
  • a primitive sentential operator, "Ada believes that p," without an object p.

Each avoids abstract proposition objects but must recover sharing across languages, attitude logic, truth attribution, and unexpressed contents.

Equivalence classes are especially circular if synonymy was to be explained through shared proposition.

The indispensability case is therefore comparative: propositions are justified if they unify these roles better than paraphrases, not merely because grammar appears to quantify over them.

One notion or several?

The explanatory roles pull toward different grains:

RoleUseful grain
Modal truth and entailmentsets of possible worlds
Compositional sentence contenttyped or structured values
Cognitive significanceFregean or hyperintensional content
Belief explanationmodes of presentation or mental files
Assertion and disagreementcontextually expressed public content
Metaphysical truth-bearinglanguage-independent proposition

One theory may identify these levels; another may distinguish several notions of content. Multiplicity is not automatically a defect if the relations among levels are explicit.

The minimal conclusion is functional: semantic theory needs stable, shareable, truth-evaluable contents. Whether those contents are propositions as entities, formal representations, or roles in a theory remains an open interface between language and ontology.
