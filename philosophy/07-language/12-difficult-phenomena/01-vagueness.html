Vagueness

"Bald," "heap," "tall," and "red" have clear positive and negative cases but no obvious sharp boundary. Small changes that appear irrelevant can accumulate into an undeniable difference. Vagueness is the challenge of explaining this pattern without making ordinary predicates incoherent.

The central puzzle is the sorites paradox. Competing theories locate the sharpness in an unknowable boundary, allow truth-value gaps or degrees, make truth depend on admissible precisifications, or explain tolerance through shifting context.

This page concerns linguistic and semantic vagueness. Whether objects and identity can be genuinely indeterminate is treated in Vagueness, Gunk, and Boundaries.

Borderline cases

A predicate is vague when it permits cases where competent speakers hesitate between application and non-application without merely lacking facts.

Vagueness differs from:

  • ambiguity: several distinct meanings;
  • generality: one broad but precise extension;
  • ignorance: facts are missing but the criterion is sharp;
  • context sensitivity: context selects among precise or vague standards;
  • open texture: unforeseen cases require extension of a rule.

These phenomena can interact. "Tall" is both vague and context-sensitive.

The sorites

One grain is not a heap. Adding one grain never appears sufficient to turn a non-heap into a heap. By repeated applications:

¬H(1),

∀n(¬H(n)→¬H(n+1)),

therefore:

¬H(1,000,000).

The conclusion is false, so at least one premise or inference must fail.

The paradox combines clear endpoints, tolerance under small change, and classical induction. Each major theory revises one component.

Epistemicism

Epistemicism holds that vague predicates have sharp extensions, but speakers cannot know the boundary. There is a last non-bald hair count, though nothing reveals it.

Strengths:

  • preserves classical logic and bivalence;
  • validates ordinary quantification;
  • treats vagueness as extreme ignorance.

Pressures:

  • the boundary appears arbitrary;
  • ignorance is unlike ordinary hidden facts;
  • higher-order uncertainty needs explanation;
  • use does not seem to determine one exact cutoff.

Epistemicists appeal to complex use facts and margins for error: close cases cannot be known because reliable discrimination requires distance from the boundary.

Supervaluationism

Supervaluationism represents a vague predicate through many admissible precisifications. A sentence is:

  • supertrue if true on all precisifications;
  • superfalse if false on all;
  • otherwise indeterminate.

"One million grains form a heap" can be supertrue even though no exact cutoff is privileged. Classical tautologies remain supertrue.

The theory rejects truth-functionality for compounds at the supervaluation level and must explain admissibility without presupposing vagueness.

Many-valued and fuzzy approaches

A many-valued theory assigns degrees:

Truth(H(n))∈[0,1].

Membership increases gradually rather than switching at one boundary.

This models graded judgments and similarity. Problems include:

  • truth degree is not obviously degree of baldness;
  • precise numerical values can be as arbitrary as cutoffs;
  • logical connectives need justified degree functions;
  • some vague predicates are not naturally scalar.

Degree semantics is especially useful where measurement and comparison structure are independently motivated.

Contextualism

Contextual theories say standards shift during a sorites series. Attention to adjacent cases makes them count alike, but the conversational standard changes gradually.

This explains tolerance and why explicit boundary questions alter judgments. It also risks predicting disagreement away: speakers can hold context fixed and still face borderline cases.

A stable context can remain vague, so contextuality is at most part of the explanation.

Subvaluation and gaps

Gap theories deny that every borderline statement is true or false. This blocks the sorites step or classical reasoning across indeterminate cases.

The challenge is higher-order vagueness. If there is a sharp boundary between determinate and indeterminate cases, vagueness reappears at that boundary. Iterating gaps threatens an endless hierarchy.

Higher-order vagueness

There are borderline borderline cases: uncertainty about whether a case is determinately bald. Any theory drawing a precise semantic line must explain why speakers cannot locate it.

Responses include:

  • iterated precisifications;
  • epistemic limits;
  • degree structures;
  • rejection of sharp meta-level categories;
  • contextual shifts.

Higher-order vagueness is a principal test of whether a theory merely moves the boundary.

Vagueness and composition

Vague expressions can compose:

Ada is very tall.

Ada is not clearly tall.

Everyone in the group is tall.

Modifiers, negation, and quantification interact with borderline status. Formal semantics must specify whether they operate on degrees, precisifications, partial truth values, or contextually selected standards.

Pragmatic uses

Vagueness can be useful:

  • efficient approximation;
  • flexibility under limited information;
  • postponing irrelevant precision;
  • coordinating despite different thresholds.

Speakers can also sharpen terms explicitly:

For this regulation, "tall" means at least 180 cm.

Stipulation removes vagueness for a purpose without discovering the ordinary word's hidden boundary.
