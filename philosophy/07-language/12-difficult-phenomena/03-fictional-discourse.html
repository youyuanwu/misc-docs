Fictional Discourse

"Holmes lives at Baker Street" is true according to Conan Doyle's stories. "Holmes is a fictional character" is true outside them. "Holmes is more famous than many real detectives" compares fiction with reality. These uses place different demands on semantics and ontology.

This page treats the linguistic mechanisms: story operators, pretense, names, internal and external predication, and cross-fiction discourse. The question whether fictional characters exist as abstract artifacts remains in Fictional and Intentional Objects.

Internal and external claims

Internal fictional claims hold within a story:

Holmes is a detective.

External claims concern works or characters from outside:

Holmes was created by Conan Doyle.

Mixed claims compare:

Holmes is more famous than many real detectives.

A uniform subject-predicate semantics struggles because an abstract character does not literally live at Baker Street, while a nonexistent concrete detective cannot be created or famous.

Story operators

Internal claims can be analysed under an implicit operator:

According to story F, p.

"Holmes lives at Baker Street" becomes true when the relevant story represents that content.

The operator handles reference and truth relative to a fiction. It must explain unstated consequences, background import, unreliable narration, and inconsistency.

Pretense

Pretense theories treat fiction as prescribed imagining. Texts function as props in games of make-believe.

Internal utterances are moves licensed by the game rather than assertions about actual objects. Readers coordinate imaginings without believing the content.

The theory handles incompleteness naturally: if the story does not settle Holmes's blood type, no imagining is prescribed.

External and cross-fiction claims require extended pretense or paraphrase.

Fictional names

Options for "Holmes" include:

  • an empty name;
  • a descriptive role;
  • a name inside a pretense;
  • reference to an abstract artifact;
  • a discourse referent introduced by the story.

Empty Names and Reference Failure compares the general alternatives.

Different mechanisms may apply to failed scientific names and deliberately authored characters.

Abstract artifacts

If Holmes is an abstract artifact, external claims receive ordinary reference:

Holmes was created in the nineteenth century.

Internal properties are represented or encoded rather than literally exemplified. The artifact is not a concrete detective.

This dual predication explains the two discourse levels but incurs created abstract objects and identity questions across adaptations.

Truth in fiction

Sources include:

  • explicit text;
  • logical consequences;
  • genre conventions;
  • ordinary background facts;
  • authorial prescriptions;
  • best interpretation.

Importing every actual fact yields unwanted results; importing none makes stories too sparse. The reality principle imports background unless the fiction blocks it, but which background is relevant depends on genre and purpose.

Inconsistency

A fiction can imply contradictions. Classical consequence would make every sentence true in the fiction.

Responses use:

  • paraconsistent consequence;
  • local story fragments;
  • authorial repair;
  • maximal consistent subsets;
  • relevance-sensitive inference.

Fictional truth therefore need not inherit unrestricted classical closure.

Cross-fiction discourse

Holmes is more observant than Poirot.

No single source story settles this. The claim can be:

  • comparative criticism about represented traits;
  • a new pretense combining works;
  • a claim about abstract artifacts and encoded properties;
  • context-sensitive evaluation under selected standards.

Such cases pressure simple "according to the story" operators.

Quantification

Some fictional detectives are violinists.

The surface quantifies over characters. Anti-realists seek paraphrases through works, names, or prescribed imaginings. Realists accept character objects.

Semantic convenience provides evidence but not a conclusive ontological argument. Paraphrase quality and theoretical cost matter.

Assessment

Fictional discourse requires at least:

  1. separation of internal and external predication;
  2. a theory of fictional truth;
  3. treatment of empty or artifact-denoting names;
  4. controlled background import;
  5. non-trivial inconsistency handling;
  6. cross-fiction comparison.

Pretense excels at internal engagement; artifact theories at external discourse; story operators at compositional embedding. Hybrid accounts are common because the discourse itself has multiple functions.
