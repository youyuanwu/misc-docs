Questions, Imperatives, and Exclamatives

Not every meaningful sentence states a proposition. Questions raise issues, imperatives direct action, and exclamatives display evaluation or surprise. Their interpretation requires semantic values and illocutionary force beyond ordinary truth conditions.

Formal semantics can generalize from truth values to answers, properties, preferences, and context-change potentials while preserving compositional structure.

Questions

Question types include:

  • polar: "Did Ada leave?";
  • constituent: "Who left?";
  • alternative: "Did Ada or Ben leave?";
  • embedded: "Ben wonders who left."

A question can denote a set of possible answers:

{ p,¬p }

for a polar question.

Partition semantics divides worlds by complete answers. Inquisitive semantics represents issues and information jointly.

Answerhood

Answers can be:

  • complete or partial;
  • direct or indirect;
  • resolving or merely relevant;
  • true or false.

"Ada left" answers "Who left?" partially if others did. "The lights are off" may answer "Is anyone home?" indirectly.

Semantics characterizes alternatives; pragmatics determines relevance and sufficiency.

Imperatives

Close the door.

does not straightforwardly describe a world. Analyses assign:

  • a property of actions;
  • a proposition to be made true;
  • a preference update;
  • a command operator;
  • a plan-set restriction.

Imperatives interact with negation, conditionals, and conjunction, requiring more than force added to a declarative paraphrase.

Satisfaction and compliance

An imperative can be satisfied when the action occurs, but not true. Compliance also depends on agency and authority.

The content may identify acceptable outcomes while speech-act theory explains who is directed and what obligation, if any, arises.

Conditional imperatives

If you see Ada, call me.

The antecedent restricts circumstances in which the directive applies. Dynamic or modal semantics can represent conditional plans.

Material implication is inadequate because a false antecedent would make every command trivially satisfied in the wrong sense.

Exclamatives

What a beautiful day!

How tall Ada is!

Exclamatives convey degree and speaker attitude, often presupposing the relevant fact while expressing surprise or evaluation.

They may contribute expressive content rather than an at-issue assertion. An audience can challenge the presupposition or evaluation differently.

Expressive meaning

Expressives such as interjections and evaluative particles can affect appropriateness without changing truth conditions.

Multidimensional semantics separates:

⟨at-issue content, expressive content⟩.

This preserves composition while explaining why denial targets dimensions differently.

Mood and force

Declarative, interrogative, and imperative mood conventionally correlate with asserting, asking, and directing, but indirect speech acts break identity.

Mood supplies a default force potential; context and intention determine the performed act.

Embedding

Questions and exclamatives embed:

Ada knows who left.

It is amazing how tall Ada is.

Embedding suggests they have compositional semantic values independent of main-clause force.

Imperatives embed less freely, revealing cross-construction differences.

Assessment

Non-declaratives show that meaning cannot be universally identified with a proposition's truth-condition.

A complete theory needs:

  • alternatives and issues for questions;
  • action or preference content for imperatives;
  • expressive dimensions for exclamatives;
  • mood-to-force conventions;
  • pragmatic interpretation of indirect uses.

Truth-conditional semantics remains central for declaratives and embedded propositional content, but formal semantics must support more than truth values.
