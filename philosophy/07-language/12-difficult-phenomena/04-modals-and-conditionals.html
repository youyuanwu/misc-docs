Modals and Conditionals

"Must," "might," "if," and "would" relate claims to alternatives rather than only to actual facts. Their interpretation depends on which possibilities count, how those possibilities are ordered, and whether the construction concerns knowledge, law, causation, obligation, or practical deliberation.

Formal semantics uses worlds and accessibility; natural language adds contextual modal bases, similarity, probability, and suppositional reasoning.

Flavours of modality

Compare:

  • It must be raining: epistemic.
  • Visitors must sign in: deontic.
  • The bridge can support the load: circumstantial.
  • Ada can swim: ability.
  • The reaction might occur: physical or epistemic.

The modal force and base interact. "Must" need not express metaphysical necessity.

The metaphysical taxonomy remains in Possibility and Necessity.

Modal bases and ordering

A modal base supplies accessible worlds. An ordering source ranks them by normality, law, goals, or rules.

Roughly:

[[must p]]w,c=1

when p holds in all best relevant worlds.

Context supplies facts and standards. This explains why identical modal words vary without unlimited ambiguity.

Material conditionals

Classical logic interprets:

p→q≡¬p∨q.

This validates useful inference but creates paradoxes:

  • a false antecedent makes any conditional true;
  • a true consequent makes any conditional true.

Natural-language indicatives carry relevance, evidential, and probabilistic expectations beyond material truth conditions.

Strict implication

A strict conditional requires necessary implication:

□(p→q).

It is too strong for many counterfactuals and retains variants of irrelevant implication. "If the match had been struck, it would have lit" need not hold at every possible world.

Variably strict conditionals

Stalnaker-Lewis semantics evaluates closest antecedent worlds:

p□→q

is true when the nearest relevant p-worlds are q-worlds.

Similarity preserves laws and history selectively. Context determines which respects matter.

Stalnaker often uses one closest world; Lewis permits a set or ordering without a unique minimum.

Counterfactual dependence

Counterfactuals support causal reasoning:

If the switch had not been flipped, the light would not have turned on.

But backtracking, preemption, and impossible antecedents complicate simple similarity. Counterfactual truth is evidence for causation, not identical with it.

Indicative conditionals

Indicatives:

If Ada is home, the light is on.

are often linked to conditional probability:

P(q∣p).

Suppositional theories say evaluating "if p, q" involves temporarily adding p and assessing q.

Whether indicatives have ordinary truth conditions remains disputed. Their assertibility tracks evidential connection more closely than material implication.

Conditional antecedents and context

Antecedents can restrict operators:

If a student arrives, she must register.

The conditional restricts the modal domain. This "restrictor" view unifies conditionals with quantificational structure.

Dynamic semantics additionally lets antecedents introduce discourse referents and presupposition support.

Impossible antecedents

Standard possible-world accounts make all counterpossibles vacuously true:

If 2+2 were 5, then Paris would be in Germany.

Speakers distinguish sensible from irrelevant consequents. Hyperintensional or impossible-world semantics preserves those judgments.
