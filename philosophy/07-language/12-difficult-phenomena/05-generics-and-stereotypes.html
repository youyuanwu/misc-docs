Generics and Stereotypes

"Birds fly," "Tigers are striped," and "Mosquitoes carry malaria" are generic statements. They do not mean that every member has the property, nor can they be reduced uniformly to "most." Penguins are birds, and few mosquitoes carry malaria, yet the generics can be accepted.

Generics express patterns of normality, characteristic function, prevalence, or strikingness. Their flexibility makes them useful for learning and dangerous when applied to human groups.

Not universal quantification

The universal:

∀x(Bird(x)→Fly(x))

is false because of penguins and injured birds. The generic tolerates exceptions.

Exceptions can be systematic rather than accidental. Female birds may not lay eggs, yet "Birds lay eggs" can be acceptable.

Not simple majority

"Mosquitoes carry malaria" can be acceptable even when a small proportion do. "Books are paperbacks" can be false despite many paperbacks.

Frequency matters but is filtered through:

  • danger or strikingness;
  • biological function;
  • characteristic life cycle;
  • explanatory relevance;
  • comparison class.

Normality

A normality account evaluates ideal or normal members. "Birds fly" says normal birds fly.

The notion can be:

  • statistical;
  • biological-functional;
  • normative;
  • stereotypical;
  • contextually selected.

Without clarification, "normal" simply restates the generic.

Prevalence

Prevalence theories set context-sensitive thresholds. Different properties require different proportions depending on alternatives and characteristic distribution.

This captures frequency effects while allowing "Mosquitoes carry malaria" a low threshold because the property is striking.

The threshold mechanism risks overfitting unless independently constrained.

Strikingness

Dangerous or memorable properties license generics at low prevalence:

Sharks attack swimmers.

Such generics guide precaution. They can distort probability judgments and reinforce stereotypes.

Strikingness belongs partly to cognition and conversational purpose, not only extension.

Characteristic dimensions

Some generics concern functions or dispositions:

  • Knives cut.
  • Bees make honey.
  • Ducks lay eggs.

They describe characteristic capacities or roles even when not currently manifested by every instance.

Context

"Dogs are pets" varies across social and ecological settings. Generic acceptability depends on:

  • domain;
  • contrast class;
  • purpose;
  • time and place;
  • standards of exception.

This contextuality does not make generics arbitrary. World patterns and established classifications constrain them.

Stereotypes

Generics about social groups can:

  • obscure within-group variation;
  • imply explanatory essences;
  • make exceptions seem deviant;
  • transmit expectations despite weak evidence.

These effects can occur even when a generic is interpreted descriptively. A semantic account should explain tolerance and normality without treating stereotypes as natural kind essences.

The section keeps broader political evaluation minimal; the linguistic issue is how a form supports robust generalization without explicit quantifier thresholds.

Acquisition and inference

Children learn categories from generic statements and may generalize properties more strongly than from quantified claims. Generics invite inductive expectations.

Inference is defeasible:

Birds fly; Tweety is a bird; therefore Tweety probably flies.

Exceptions and subkind information can defeat it.

Formal approaches

Candidates include:

  • generic operator GEN;
  • normal-world quantification;
  • probabilistic thresholds;
  • kind predication;
  • default logic;
  • causal/explanatory accounts.

No one analysis covers all biological, artifact, striking, and social generics without contextual parameters.

Assessment

Generics are neither disguised universals nor vague versions of "most." They encode defeasible generalization shaped by prevalence, normality, function, and relevance.

A successful theory must predict:

  • tolerated exceptions;
  • low-prevalence striking generics;
  • contrasts among properties;
  • subkind defeat;
  • context shifts;
  • inferential and stereotype effects.
