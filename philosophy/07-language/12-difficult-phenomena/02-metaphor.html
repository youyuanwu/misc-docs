Metaphor and Non-Literal Meaning

"Juliet is the sun" does not classify Juliet as a star. "The argument collapsed" applies spatial language to reasoning. Metaphor can communicate determinate insights without a literal paraphrase that exhausts them.

Theories treat metaphor as abbreviated comparison, semantic interaction, pragmatic speaker meaning, relevance-guided concept construction, or a cognitive mapping across domains. The cases range from novel literary metaphors to dead expressions whose figurative origins are no longer noticed.

Literal and metaphorical content

A metaphor uses an expression outside its ordinary application to invite a related interpretation. It should be distinguished from:

  • literal polysemy;
  • idiom;
  • hyperbole;
  • irony;
  • metonymy;
  • analogy.

Boundaries shift as uses conventionalize. "The foot of the mountain" may now be an ordinary lexical sense rather than an active metaphor.

Comparison theories

A comparison view analyses:

Juliet is the sun

as roughly:

Juliet is like the sun in relevant respects.

This explains similarities but underdetermines which respects matter. Every pair of objects resembles in indefinitely many ways.

Metaphor can also create rather than report perceived similarity. Calling a theory a "building" organizes attention around foundations, support, and collapse.

Substitution theories

A substitution account replaces the metaphorical expression with a literal one:

The argument collapsed

might mean the argument failed.

This works for conventional metaphors but often loses imagery, evaluation, and inferential possibilities. If a perfect paraphrase exists, the original's distinctive effect remains unexplained.

Interaction theories

Interaction theories hold that the two domains reshape one another. A "wolf" metaphor selects and organizes beliefs about predation, danger, and social relation rather than substituting one property.

The metaphor filters a system of associated commonplaces. This captures creativity but risks making content depend on stereotypes that vary among speakers.

Gricean pragmatics

On a Gricean account, a blatantly false literal assertion overtly violates Quality. Assuming cooperation, the audience searches for a related speaker meaning.

The account preserves literal semantics and explains contextual inference. It faces:

  • metaphors that are literally true;
  • rapid conventional interpretation;
  • difficulty specifying inferred content;
  • metaphor inside embedding.

Not every metaphor is best treated as saying one thing while meaning another.

Relevance theory

Relevance theory treats metaphor as lexical broadening or construction of an occasion-specific concept. "Bulldozer" applied to a person activates implications worth processing in context.

Literal approximation, hyperbole, and metaphor form a continuum. Interpretation stops when sufficient relevant effects are achieved.

The account is flexible but can become post hoc unless relevance and candidate implications are independently constrained.

Conceptual metaphor

Cognitive theories identify systematic mappings:

  • ARGUMENT IS WAR;
  • TIME IS MONEY;
  • THEORIES ARE BUILDINGS;
  • MORE IS UP.

These patterns organize ordinary expressions and reasoning, not just poetry. They suggest metaphor is part of conceptual structure.

Critics question whether linguistic patterns always reveal stable mental mappings and whether bodily correlations explain abstract content fully.

Truth conditions

Can metaphors be true or false? "No person is an island" seems true metaphorically.

Options:

  • only literal content has truth conditions; metaphor conveys implications;
  • pragmatic enrichment produces metaphorical truth conditions;
  • metaphor presents a perspective not reducible to propositions;
  • different interpretations yield partially ordered contents.

Disagreement and denial support some propositional content, while resistance to paraphrase suggests it may not be exhaustive.

Creativity and paraphrase

A novel metaphor can yield open-ended insights. Paraphrases may capture central implications without replacing the metaphor.

This resembles model use: one domain structures reasoning about another while remaining partial and purpose-sensitive.

Open-endedness should not imply absence of constraint. Text, context, associations, and speaker purpose make some interpretations clearly better than others.

Dead metaphor

Repeated metaphorical uses can lexicalize:

  • branch of science;
  • mouth of a river;
  • grasp an idea.

Speakers may process them without accessing the original source domain.

Diachronic origin does not determine current semantics. A theory should distinguish live metaphorical inference from established polysemy.

Assessment

Metaphor tests the semantics-pragmatics boundary because it combines stable lexical meaning, contextual adjustment, inference, cognition, and expressive effect.

No single theory covers every case. Conventional metaphors may involve lexical senses; novel cases require pragmatic and cognitive construction; literary cases can exceed one paraphrasable proposition. The goal is a constrained continuum, not a forced choice between literal decoding and unconstrained association.
