Comparative Traditions on Language

Indian, Buddhist, Chinese, and Islamic traditions developed sophisticated accounts of words, sentences, reference, inference, interpretation, and rhetoric. Their categories should not be forced into modern analytic labels. The comparisons below identify problems shared across traditions while preserving internal disagreement.

Indian debates over sentence meaning

Mimamsa traditions debate whether words first express independent meanings that are then composed or whether sentential connection is primary.

  • Abhihitanvaya: words express meanings, which are subsequently connected.
  • Anvitabhidhana: words express meanings already related within the sentence.

The dispute parallels questions about lexical autonomy, composition, and the context principle, but arises within theories of testimony and Vedic interpretation.

Sphota

Grammarian traditions associated with Bhartrhari use sphota for the unit through which meaning is grasped, contrasted with variable sounds that manifest it.

Sentence understanding can appear holistic and instantaneous despite sequential utterance. This pressures atomistic composition while still requiring an account of parts, learning, and error.

Bhartrhari's broader philosophy connects language and cognition more deeply than a simple abstract sentence type.

Nyaya reference and testimony

Nyaya develops realist semantics tied to objects, universals, relations, and reliable means of knowledge. Testimony is an independent source of knowledge when produced by a competent speaker.

Word meaning and sentence understanding involve convention, syntactic expectancy, semantic compatibility, and proximity. These provide a structured account of composition and interpretation.

Buddhist apoha

The apoha theory explains concept and word meaning through exclusion: "cow" means through exclusion of non-cows rather than direct grasp of a universal cow-nature.

This supports nominalism and accounts for generality without real universals.

Challenges include:

  • how exclusion yields positive content;
  • circularity among contrasts;
  • learning the relevant field of alternatives;
  • explaining similarity and successful reference.

The theory invites comparison with inferential, contrastive, and prototype accounts without being identical to them.

Chinese rectification of names

Confucian discussions of rectifying names connect correct naming with roles and normative order. A ruler, parent, or minister should live up to the standards expressed by the role term.

Meaning here is not detached classification; it carries practical expectations. The project combines semantic clarification and reform.

Mohist distinctions

Mohist texts develop argument about names, kinds, standards, sameness and difference, and disputation. Correct distinctions depend on publicly applicable models or standards and practical discrimination.

This supplies resources for reference and inference without assuming one Western theory of propositions.

Daoist perspective

Zhuangzi emphasizes shifting perspectives, limitations of fixed distinctions, and the practical use of language. Critique of rigid naming does not amount to silence; it uses language to loosen attachment to one scheme.

The challenge resembles contextual and pluralist concerns while embedded in a different ethical and practical project.

Islamic theories of signification

Arabic logic, jurisprudence, theology, grammar, and rhetoric analyze:

  • conventional signification;
  • literal and figurative use;
  • general and particular expressions;
  • implication;
  • speaker intention;
  • contextual evidence;
  • interpretation of authoritative texts.

Theories of dalala distinguish ways expressions indicate meanings, while rhetoric examines metaphor and eloquence. Jurisprudence makes semantic and pragmatic distinctions practically consequential.

Translation and comparison

Comparative work faces several risks:

  • one term translated into a misleading modern category;
  • diverse schools treated as one tradition;
  • doctrines detached from epistemic, ritual, or legal purpose;
  • superficial similarity mistaken for identity.

A responsible comparison states:

  1. the original problem;
  2. the theory's own distinctions;
  3. the argumentative role;
  4. the limited modern parallel;
  5. where the mapping fails.

Assessment

Comparative traditions expand the decision space:

  • sentence-first and word-first composition;
  • holistic manifestation of meaning;
  • exclusion rather than universals;
  • naming as normative practice;
  • public standards of distinction;
  • context-sensitive legal and rhetorical interpretation.

They belong within the history of ideas about language, not as decorative alternatives after a completed analytic theory.
