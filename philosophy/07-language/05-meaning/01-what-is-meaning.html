What Is Meaning?

A stain can indicate a leak, smoke can indicate fire, and a tree's rings can carry information about its age. Words also carry information, but they do more. They can be used correctly or incorrectly, combined into new sentences, understood when their claims are false, and deployed to refer to things that are absent or nonexistent. A theory of linguistic meaning must explain these capacities without treating every correlation or association as semantic.

The first task is therefore not to choose between truth, use, intention, or convention. It is to identify what each is being asked to explain. Some theories assign semantic values to expressions; some explain how those values combine; some explain how speakers communicate; and some explain what makes a particular assignment correct. Calling all four a "theory of meaning" conceals differences that matter.

This page states the explanatory targets and separates meaning from neighbouring relations. The following pages examine sense and reference, truth-conditional semantics, meaning as use, and intention and convention.

Four explanatory projects

At least four projects travel under the name theory of meaning.

Semantic value

A theory may say what contribution an expression makes to larger constructions. A proper name might contribute an object, a predicate a function from objects to truth values, and a sentence a truth-condition or proposition. This is a theory of semantic values.

Different grammatical categories need different kinds of value. Treating every word as naming an object leaves no plausible account of "not," "every," or "quickly." Formal semantics therefore assigns values by type and specifies how they compose.

Compositional interpretation

A theory may explain how the meaning of a complex expression depends on its parts and their arrangement. "Ada admires Ben" and "Ben admires Ada" contain the same names and verb but differ because syntax assigns different roles.

This project addresses productivity: finite speakers understand indefinitely many new sentences because they need not memorize each as an independent unit. They know repeatable expressions and modes of combination.

Communication

A theory may explain how speakers use expressions to make intentions recognizable to audiences. The conventional content of "It is late" does not by itself determine whether an utterance is an observation, a reason to leave, a reproach, or a reminder. Communication depends on common knowledge, context, purposes, and inference.

This project is developed through the distinction between semantics and pragmatics. It cannot simply replace a semantic theory, because speakers exploit relatively stable expression meanings when they communicate indirectly.

Metasemantics

A theory may ask why an expression has one semantic value rather than another. Why does "water" refer to water rather than to a superficially similar liquid? Why does a speaker's "and" express conjunction rather than an unfamiliar function? Answers appeal to causal history, environment, convention, public practice, inference, rational interpretation, or speaker intentions.

This is metasemantics: an account of the facts that ground semantic facts. A model-theoretic interpretation can assign values without answering what makes that assignment the interpretation of English.

The projects interact but should not be collapsed. A truth-conditional theory might give semantic values and composition while relying on convention or interpretation for its metasemantics and on Gricean inference for communication.

What an adequate theory must explain

Understanding

To understand an expression is not merely to react reliably to it. A person who understands "triangle" can recognize new applications, explain some errors, combine it with other expressions, and follow discourse about triangles that do not exist.

Understanding need not be conscious possession of a theory. Most speakers cannot state the grammatical or semantic rules they competently follow. A theory must therefore distinguish tacit competence from explicit knowledge while explaining why the competence is systematic.

Correctness and error

Words can be misapplied and sentences can be false. A child may call every animal a "dog"; an adult may falsely say that Venus is a star. Error is possible only if meaning sets a standard not reducible to whatever the speaker happens to do.

This creates a normative pressure. If actual disposition alone fixes meaning, then a systematic mistake threatens to become correct by definition. If ideal disposition fixes meaning, the theory must explain which idealization is relevant and why.

Public shareability

Speakers with different beliefs, experiences, and abilities can mean the same thing well enough to agree, disagree, teach, and correct one another. Meaning must be stable across some differences among individuals.

Publicity does not require perfect uniformity. Dialects, technical sublanguages, partial competence, and semantic change are real. The explanatory task is to say which differences preserve shared content and which amount to a change of meaning.

Productivity and systematicity

Understanding is productive because speakers can interpret novel sentences, and systematic because capacities come in patterns. Someone who understands "Ada admires Ben" normally understands "Ben admires Ada." A theory that assigns meanings independently to whole sentences misses this structure.

Compositionality is the standard explanation, but its strength is disputed. Idioms, context-sensitive constructions, and lexical modulation show that the contribution of a part may depend on more than an isolated dictionary entry.

World-involvement and misrepresentation

Language is answerable to how things are. A meaningful claim can be true or false, a name can fail to refer, and a classification can be inaccurate. A theory must explain both connection to the world and the possibility of getting it wrong.

Pure covariance is insufficient. "Fire" can be used meaningfully when no fire is present, and smoke can correlate with fire without meaning fire in a language. Semantic representation is detachable from its normal cause.

Cognitive significance

Co-reference does not guarantee sameness of meaning. "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" both refer to Venus, but learning that Hesperus is Phosphorus can increase knowledge. A theory must explain why substituting co-referring expressions can change what a speaker learns or believes.

This is the central motivation for Fregean sense and a central challenge for views on which a name's semantic contribution is only its referent.

Meaning is not reference

Reference is one semantic relation, not a complete theory of meaning.

  • Different expressions can share a referent while differing cognitively.
  • Some meaningful expressions, such as "and" or "perhaps," are not naturally treated as names of objects.
  • Empty names and fictional discourse appear meaningful despite reference failure.
  • Complete sentences can be meaningful regardless of whether they are true.

A referential theory may still explain important categories, especially names, demonstratives, and natural-kind terms. It must be embedded in a broader account of sentence structure, cognitive role, and non-referring vocabulary.

Meaning is not information alone

In one technical sense, a signal carries information about whatever reliably covaries with it. Tree rings carry information about age, and a fuel gauge carries information about a tank. This relation can exist without users, conventions, or understanding.

Linguistic meaning differs in at least three ways:

  1. A sentence can represent a condition that does not obtain.
  2. The same information can be expressed by semantically different sentences.
  3. Accidental correlation does not establish what an expression means.

Information theory can measure alternatives reduced by a signal without supplying its content. Richer informational semantics adds causal or counterfactual conditions, but then must explain error, abstraction, and convention.

Meaning is not association

Words evoke images, emotions, stereotypes, and memories. These associations affect processing and communication, but they vary too freely to constitute all meaning. One speaker associates "dog" with fear and another with affection while both use the word for dogs.

Associations can become conventionally significant, and some expressions carry evaluative or expressive dimensions not captured by reference. The point is not that association is irrelevant. It is that a theory needs criteria for distinguishing semantic contribution from personal or culturally common accompaniment.

Meaning is not use frequency

Use theories make linguistic practice central, but meaning as use is not the claim that meaning equals a frequency distribution. Rare and novel uses can be correct; frequent mistakes can remain mistakes; and two expressions with similar distributions can differ in inferential or referential role.

"Use" in a philosophical theory includes rule-governed possibilities, standards of correction, inferential consequences, and place within a practice. Corpus frequencies are evidence about that practice, not a definition of it.

Meaning is not one occasion's intention

A speaker can intend a familiar word to mean something idiosyncratic, but intention alone does not guarantee success. Secretly intending "bank" to mean oak tree does not make an unprepared audience understand "The bank is tall" accordingly.

One-off speaker meaning depends on a background of shared expression meaning and recognizable communicative purposes. Intention-based theories must explain how stable conventions arise and how speakers can exploit, bend, or violate them.

Meaning is not causal covariance

A thermometer covaries with temperature and a footprint with a foot. Neither relation alone establishes linguistic content. Covariance theories face the disjunction problem: if a token is caused by horses in good conditions and cows in poor conditions, does it mean horse, horse-or-cow, or something else?

Causal history is nevertheless important to reference. The lesson is narrower: causation must be structured by learning, function, convention, or historical chains before it can distinguish content from every reliable indicator.

Three dimensions of evaluation

Theories are easier to compare when three questions are asked separately:

DimensionQuestionTypical candidates
SemanticWhat value does the expression contribute?object, sense, extension, intension, proposition, inferential role
MetasemanticWhat fixes that value?causal history, convention, use, intention, interpretation, environment
Epistemic/psychologicalWhat enables a speaker to understand it?tacit knowledge, recognitional skill, inference, simulation, social learning

A theory may be strong on one dimension and silent on another. Direct-reference theory may assign an object to a name while leaving cognitive significance to a distinct mode of presentation. Formal semantics may derive truth-conditions while leaving their metasemantic grounding to social practice.

A pluralist working hypothesis

The explanatory targets suggest a division of labour rather than one universal relation:

  • reference and truth-conditions connect language to the world;
  • compositional rules explain productivity;
  • sense or cognitive role explains how content is presented;
  • convention and use explain public stability;
  • intention and pragmatic inference explain occasion-specific communication; and
  • causal and social history help fix reference.

This is a hypothesis, not a conclusion. A unified theory would be preferable if it explained the same range without equivocation. The burden on any monistic account is to show that its favoured relation can recover the work apparently done by the others.
