Meaning as Use and Language-Games

A word is not normally understood by pairing a sound with an object and then carrying that pairing into every context. "Game" applies to chess, tag, solitaire, professional football, and improvised play despite the absence of one obvious feature shared by all. "Pain" is learned amid injury, comfort, avowal, and response rather than by privately inspecting an inner object and attaching a label. Logical and mathematical expressions acquire roles through practices of inference and calculation.

The later Wittgenstein's formulation that "the meaning of a word is its use in the language" (Philosophical Investigations, §43) redirects attention from hidden entities to these public practices. Meaning is not an object accompanying a word but the word's place in rule-governed human activity. The proposal illuminates learning, normativity, and the diversity of language, but it must not be reduced to frequency or observable behaviour. It also faces questions about composition, reference, novelty, and the possibility of criticizing established use.

From the picture theory to use

The early Wittgenstein of the Tractatus treated a proposition as a logical picture of a possible state of affairs. A proposition shares logical form with what it represents; understanding it is grasping the condition under which it would be true. This belongs broadly with truth-conditional approaches.

The later Philosophical Investigations resists the assumption that all meaningful expressions work by naming objects or picturing facts. The opening target is an Augustinian picture of language: words name things, and sentences combine names. That picture fits some uses but fails as a universal model.

Consider:

  • orders and responses on a building site;
  • questions, jokes, greetings, and prayers;
  • calculating, describing, pretending, and storytelling;
  • teaching a child a colour word;
  • reporting pain; and
  • inventing rules for a game.

These activities do not all reduce to naming or fact-stating. The mistake is not using a referential model where it works, but demanding that every expression conform to it.

Language-games

A language-game is a simplified or actual practice in which words, actions, rules, and purposes belong together. The term emphasizes that speaking is part of an activity, not the manipulation of an autonomous code.

A builder's call "Slab!" can function as an order to bring a slab. Its role depends on training, recurring circumstances, and coordinated action. Treating it as an elliptical declarative sentence may be possible, but doing so can obscure the practice that gives the call its point.

Language-games serve two methodological roles:

  1. They display the plurality of things done with words.
  2. They locate standards of correct use within practices speakers can learn and share.

The analogy with games should not imply that language is optional entertainment or that every practice has an explicit rulebook. Games themselves range from codified competition to improvised play. What matters is patterned participation with distinctions between going on correctly and incorrectly.

Forms of life

Language-games are embedded in a form of life: the biological capacities, habits, institutions, environments, and practical concerns that make the activity intelligible. A command matters because people act; colour terms matter within perception, classification, and coordination; pain language belongs to patterns of injury, avowal, treatment, and care.

This blocks an infinite regress in which every rule requires a further interpretation. At some point explanation reaches how speakers simply go on as a matter of shared training and practice.

The stopping point is not a brute frequency. It includes standards internal to the practice. A novice can make a mistake even if the mistake is common, because correction and justification belong to the activity.

Family resemblance

Traditional analysis seeks necessary and sufficient conditions for a concept. Wittgenstein's example of games suggests a different structure. Board games, card games, athletic games, children's games, and solitary games overlap in many respects, but no single property appears in every case. Their unity is a network of family resemblances.

Family resemblance explains flexible extension without requiring arbitrary homonymy. A concept can have central cases, overlapping similarities, and open-ended uses.

The idea should not become a refusal to analyse. Some concepts do have sharp definitions, especially within technical practices. Even for open-textured concepts, a theory can investigate prototypes, inferential relations, purposes, and boundaries. The claim is that one uniform essence should not be demanded in advance.

Rule-following

Meaning appears normative: understanding "add 2" distinguishes continuing

2,4,6,8,…

from writing an arbitrary next number. Yet any finite history of performance is compatible with many rules. An interpretation of a rule can itself be interpreted in different ways, threatening a regress.

Wittgenstein's response is not that communal agreement votes mathematical answers into truth. It is that following a rule is a practice manifested in training, correction, agreement in judgments, and the ability to continue without consulting a fresh interpretation at every step. A signpost guides only within a practice of using signposts.

This theme becomes the rule-following scepticism developed in Meaning Scepticism and Rule-Following. The present point is constructive: use is structured by standards that exist through practice rather than through a private mental item attached to every sign.

Private language

Imagine someone who records "S" whenever a private sensation occurs, where the sensation is in principle accessible only to that person. A private ostensive definition is supposed to fix the sign's meaning by inwardly attending to the sensation.

The problem is a standard of correctness. On later occasions, whatever seems to the diarist to match the original sensation counts as matching. There is no distinction between correct remembrance and seeming correct, so the proposed rule lacks an independent application standard.

This does not deny private experiences or first-person authority. It challenges the idea of a language whose signs are grounded solely by objects only one person could in principle identify. Public pain language can concern private experience because its use is learned and corrected within shared life.

The corresponding issues about experience and self-knowledge are developed in Philosophy of Mind.

Use is not frequency

"Meaning is use" is easily caricatured as:

meaning=most frequent observable behaviour.

That cannot preserve error, innovation, or rare but correct applications. Philosophical use includes:

  • circumstances of appropriate application;
  • inferential consequences;
  • contrasts with neighbouring expressions;
  • responses and corrections;
  • purposes served within an activity; and
  • counterfactual uses a competent speaker would recognize.

Frequency data can reveal patterns of use, but no raw count determines which dimensions are meaning-constitutive. Corpus evidence requires interpretation, as the method page explains.

Is use theory behaviourism?

Use is publicly manifest, but a use theory need not identify meaning with bodily movement. Understanding involves abilities, sensitivities, expectations, and participation in norms. These can be displayed behaviourally without being reducible to a finite list of stimulus-response pairs.

Nor does public criteria imply that mental episodes are unreal. It changes the order of explanation. The meaning of "pain" is not secured by privately naming an incorrigibly identified object and then inferred outward. The public practice supplies the concept through which sensations are described.

A remaining challenge is to connect public criteria with novel thought and silent understanding. If a speaker has never used a construction aloud but can interpret it correctly, the relevant notion of use must include competence and counterfactual capacity, not performance alone.

Compositionality

Natural language is productive. A pure catalogue of practices for whole utterances would not explain understanding of new combinations. Use theorists therefore need an account of how uses of parts contribute to uses of wholes.

Several strategies are available:

  • grammatical rules are themselves parts of linguistic practice;
  • inferential roles compose through introduction and elimination patterns;
  • constructions have conventional functions that combine with lexical roles; and
  • sentence understanding is an ability to use the result across variable contexts.

The challenge is to make these claims precise without reverting to exactly the independent meanings the use theory hoped to replace. Formal semantics may provide the compositional structure while use supplies its public grounding. That division of labour is compatible with taking both seriously.

Reference and truth

If meaning is use, how do words reach the world? A community could consistently use "water" under systematic illusion. Actual use patterns alone seem compatible with different referents.

One answer treats reference and truth as internal to practices of inquiry, correction, and interaction with the environment. Successful use is not merely verbal agreement; speakers test, intervene, defer to experts, and revise classifications.

Another answer makes use only one metasemantic factor alongside causal history and environment. This hybrid preserves external constraint while explaining why a causal link becomes linguistic rather than merely informational.

The risk for a pure use theory is semantic idealism: if community practice fixes all correctness, the community appears unable to be systematically wrong. The risk for an externalist correction is that use ceases to be the complete theory of meaning.

Conservatism and critical distance

Grounding meaning in established practice can make existing usage seem authoritative. But speakers criticize inherited categories, expose presuppositions, and deliberately revise concepts. How can a use theory explain justified reform?

The resources are internal:

  • practices contain conflicting aims and standards;
  • new cases reveal tensions among established applications;
  • speakers can distinguish a term's current use from the use they recommend;
  • metalinguistic negotiation is itself a practice; and
  • practical consequences can supply reasons for revision.

Still, an account that says only "meaning is whatever the community does" lacks the normative distance required to evaluate a community. Use theory is strongest as an account of how meanings are sustained, not as a complete ethics of which meanings should be sustained.

What the use approach establishes

The durable lessons are methodological:

  1. Do not assume all words name entities.
  2. Examine expressions within the activities where they function.
  3. Treat understanding as practical mastery, not merely association with an inner item.
  4. Locate standards of correction in shareable practices.
  5. Expect multiple kinds of linguistic function rather than one essence.
  6. Do not infer that every meaningful utterance states a truth-evaluable proposition.

These lessons constrain truth-conditional and referential theories without eliminating them. A formal account may explain composition, a causal account may explain reference, and an intention-based account may explain communication. Meaning as use asks what makes any of those structures part of a living language.
