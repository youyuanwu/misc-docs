Inferentialism

To understand "and" is to know that from p∧q one may infer p, and that from p and q one may infer p∧q. To understand "red" is partly to know that something red is coloured and cannot be wholly green in the same place at the same time. These examples suggest that meaning is constituted not only by what expressions refer to, but by the inferences their use licenses and forbids.

Inferentialism explains content through a role in reasoning. On its strongest versions, an expression means what it does because of the commitments, entitlements, consequences, and incompatibilities governing its use. This makes semantic normativity and rational practice central. It also creates familiar pressures: if total inferential role fixes meaning, differences in belief threaten shared content; if only selected inferences count, the theory needs a principled boundary.

This page concerns inferentialism about public linguistic meaning. The related view that mental representations are individuated by their cognitive role is developed in Functional and Inferential-Role Semantics.

From representation to inference

A referential picture begins with a relation between word and object. A truth-conditional picture begins with the circumstances in which a sentence is true. Inferentialism begins with the place an expression occupies in giving and asking for reasons.

Consider the claim:

The coin is copper.

Competent use connects it to consequences such as:

  • the coin is metallic;
  • the coin conducts electricity under normal conditions;
  • the coin is not made entirely of wood; and
  • some observational and testimonial circumstances count in favour of applying "copper."

Not every associated belief is meaning-constitutive. A speaker might falsely believe that every copper object is ancient. Inferentialism must distinguish the relations that articulate the concept from collateral commitments involving it.

The central proposal can therefore be stated schematically:

Content(e)=Role(grounds,consequences,incompatibilities,practical transitions).

The formula is not yet a theory until the relevant role and its norm are specified.

Sellars and the space of reasons

Sellars distinguishes merely being caused to respond from occupying the logical space of reasons. A thermometer responds reliably to temperature, but it does not justify a claim, retract one, or infer a consequence. A speaker's assertion belongs to a network in which reasons can be requested and supplied.

This does not make causal contact irrelevant. Perceptual reports have language-entry transitions from reliable environmental circumstances; actions have language-exit transitions from practical reasoning; and claims connect to one another through intra-language transitions.

A linguistic token has conceptual content when it participates appropriately in this norm-governed network. The view explains why reliable discrimination alone is not yet the possession of a concept.

Brandom and normative pragmatics

Brandom develops inferentialism through a social practice of discursive scorekeeping. Participants track:

  • commitments a speaker undertakes;
  • entitlements the speaker has to those commitments;
  • consequences that follow from a commitment;
  • incompatibilities among commitments; and
  • changes produced by assertion, challenge, defence, and retraction.

If Ada asserts that the metal is copper, she becomes committed to its being metallic. She may be entitled through observation or testimony. If she is also committed to its being wholly wooden, other participants can challenge the incompatible score.

Content is articulated by these inferential relations. Assertion is not merely emission of a sentence with a truth-condition; it is entry into a normative practice.

Scorekeeping need not be conscious bookkeeping. It models practical sensitivities displayed when speakers draw conclusions, request reasons, detect conflict, and revise claims.

Formal inference and logical vocabulary

Logical connectives provide inferentialism's clearest cases. Natural-deduction rules can be read as specifying use:

p∧qpq​∧Ipp∧q​∧E1​qp∧q​∧E2​.

Introduction rules state how a connective can be asserted; elimination rules state what follows from it. Proof-theoretic semantics proposes that appropriately related rules fix the connective's meaning.

The qualification matters. Prior's invented connective tonk has rules allowing one to infer ptonkq from p and then infer arbitrary q from ptonkq. If any stipulated rules automatically conferred meaning, tonk would trivialize inference.

This motivates constraints such as:

  • harmony: elimination rules extract no more than introduction rules put in;
  • conservativeness: adding the connective does not create new consequences in the old vocabulary without justification;
  • normalization: detours through introduction followed by elimination can be reduced; and
  • stability: introduction and elimination rules determine one another in the relevant sense.

Inferential role is therefore normative structure, not arbitrary transition.

Material inference

Formal validity depends on logical form. Material inferences depend on non-logical content:

It is scarlet; therefore it is red.

The object is west of the tower; therefore the tower is east of the object.

An inferentialist may treat these relations as constitutive of the concepts involved. They need not be made valid by inserting suppressed conditionals learned separately.

Material inference gives the theory reach beyond logical constants. It also intensifies the analytic-synthetic problem. Is "Copper conducts electricity" constitutive of copper, or a revisable empirical discovery? If every accepted generalization enters meaning, false science becomes semantically correct and theory change becomes change of subject.

Local and global role

Global inferentialism lets an expression's content depend on its place in an entire network of commitments. It captures theoretical interdependence but produces holism: because no two speakers have exactly the same beliefs, they rarely mean exactly the same thing.

Local or molecular inferentialism selects a limited set of meaning-constitutive inferences. It protects communication and disagreement but owes a non-circular account of which inferences belong to the molecule.

Possible boundaries include:

  • conceptually basic introduction and elimination rules;
  • inferences speakers treat as criterial;
  • explanations central to learning the expression;
  • counterfactual stability under ordinary belief revision;
  • semantic deference to communal or expert practice; and
  • roles fixed by a best overall interpretation.

Each criterion can vary historically and socially. Molecularism reduces holism rather than automatically eliminating it.

Communication and disagreement

If two scientists disagree about electrons, their inferential commitments differ. Global role theory risks concluding that they attach different meanings to "electron," turning substantive disagreement into verbal difference.

Inferentialists can preserve shared content in several ways:

  1. require similarity rather than identity of total role;
  2. identify a stable core of shared material inferences;
  3. appeal to common causal and historical reference;
  4. let conversational context select the relevant portion of role; or
  5. treat content attribution as an interpretive abstraction serving explanation.

The third response yields a hybrid: world-linking keeps the topic stable while inferential role explains differences in conception. It parallels the division between reference and cognitive significance in Sense and Reference.

Compositionality

A language cannot assign an independent holistic role to every sentence and still explain productivity. The role of a complex expression must be generated from reusable parts and constructions.

For logical vocabulary, proof rules supply a direct compositional architecture. For lexical items, matters are harder. The consequences of "red square" depend on red, square, their mode of combination, and background assumptions about predication.

An inferentialist may distinguish:

  • lexical roles attached to expressions;
  • structural rules governing composition; and
  • pragmatic commitments supplied by context.

This begins to resemble a pluralist semantics rather than an alternative to formal composition. Inferential role can be one kind of semantic value or metasemantic constraint within a typed formal theory.

Reference and world-involvement

A coherent network can be systematically mistaken. Purely internal inferential role seems unable to determine whether "water" concerns water, twin water, or an imagined substance.

Entry transitions connect language to perception, but causal input alone does not fix content without a theory of error. Social deference and historical chains connect speakers to kinds they cannot identify. Truth-conditions distinguish correct from incorrect world-directed applications.

Inferentialists respond in three broad ways:

  • strong inferentialism: reference is derivative from the role a term plays in a sufficiently rich practice;
  • two-factor theory: inferential role supplies cognitive significance while external relations supply reference;
  • interpretivism: reference is part of the assignment that best makes the total practice intelligible.

The strong view is most unified and bears the largest burden. Hybrid views sacrifice reduction in exchange for a clearer world-link.

Normativity and error

Actual transitions cannot alone fix correct inference. A speaker may infer badly. If whatever the speaker does constitutes the rule, error disappears.

Normative inferentialism instead appeals to what a speaker is committed or entitled to infer. The norms may be grounded in:

  • constitutive rules of discursive practice;
  • mutual recognition among participants;
  • objective logical consequence;
  • successful inquiry and environmental interaction;
  • biological or practical function; or
  • rational reconstruction.

Each option faces a version of the rule-following question: what makes these rather than deviant transitions the correct ones? Inferentialism does not escape meaning scepticism; it provides a practice-based candidate answer.

The distinction between commitment and entitlement also preserves error. A claim can commit a speaker to a consequence even when they fail to draw it, and a speaker can be committed without being entitled.

Conservatism

If communal inferential practice constitutes meaning, entrenched prejudice or bad science might seem correct by definition. A community could license invalid or harmful inferences.

The inferentialist should distinguish:

  • what inferences currently articulate a concept;
  • whether participants are entitled to those commitments;
  • whether the practice achieves its own purposes;
  • whether reference and evidence support its applications; and
  • whether conceptual revision is recommended.

The theory describes semantic commitments without endorsing every commitment. Yet the more external standards it invokes, the less inferential role alone explains.

Relation to truth-conditional semantics

Inferential and truth-conditional theories need not be exclusive.

  • Truth-conditions explain how claims answer to possible circumstances.
  • Inferential roles explain what follows from claims and what counts as a reason.
  • In a sound logic, truth preservation and inferential validity align.
  • In non-classical settings, proof-theoretic and model-theoretic semantics can provide distinct but related characterizations.

Conflict arises over explanatory priority. A truth-conditional theorist may define valid inference through truth in all models. An inferentialist may hold that the use of expressions in inference fixes their content and thereby constrains models.

The dispute is developed especially sharply by verificationism and intuitionism, where assertibility or proof competes with verification-transcendent truth as the basis of meaning.

Inferentialism's durable insight is that meaning is not exhausted by a mapping from words to objects. Competent speakers know what follows, what conflicts, what supports, and what practical difference a claim makes. Its open question is whether that role constitutes content by itself or works alongside truth, reference, convention, and causal history.
