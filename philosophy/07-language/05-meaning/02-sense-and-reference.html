Sense and Reference

Two expressions can refer to the same thing without having the same cognitive significance. "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" both name Venus, yet the discovery that Hesperus is Phosphorus was informative. If meaning were exhausted by reference, the identity would say no more than "Venus is Venus."

Frege's distinction between sense (Sinn) and reference (Bedeutung) was designed to explain this difference while preserving compositional semantics. An expression's reference is what it stands for; its sense is the way that referent is presented. Sense determines reference without being reducible to a private image or an associated description.

The distinction set the agenda for modern philosophy of language. It explains identity statements and substitution failure, but it also raises hard questions: what kind of thing is a sense, how fine-grained are senses, and do names contribute senses to truth-conditions at all?

The identity puzzle

Compare:

  1. Hesperus is Hesperus.
  2. Hesperus is Phosphorus.

Both are of the form a=b, and both are true because the names refer to Venus. The first is trivial for a competent speaker; the second was an empirical astronomical discovery. A purely referential semantics appears to assign them the same content.

Frege's solution distinguishes:

  • reference: the object designated, Venus;
  • sense: the mode under which the object is presented.

"Hesperus" presents Venus as the bright body visible in the evening; "Phosphorus" presents it as the bright body visible in the morning. The identity is informative because it connects two senses previously taken to determine different objects.

The familiar descriptions illustrate the difference but need not be definitions of the names. A sense is intended to be an objective, shareable route to a referent, not whatever words one individual happens to associate with it.

Sense is not mental imagery

Speakers can associate different images with the same expression and still understand one another. A mental image is:

  • private to a subject;
  • variable from occasion to occasion;
  • often more detailed or less precise than linguistic content; and
  • unavailable for many expressions, including logical vocabulary.

Frege therefore located senses in neither the external object nor an individual's psychological episode. They are shareable contents available to different thinkers. This anti-psychologism explains communication but creates an ontological question: what are senses, and how do speakers stand in cognitive relations to them?

The ontology of candidate contents is considered in Propositions and Other Abstracta. The semantic issue here is the role a sense would play whether construed as an abstract object, a structured content, or a theoretical relation.

The reference of complete sentences

Frege extended the distinction compositionally. The reference of a complete declarative sentence is its truth value, the True or the False. Its sense is a thought: the truth-evaluable content expressed.

This proposal can initially seem strange. Sentences appear to say that snow is white, not to name the object True. Frege's motivation is substitution. If a component is replaced by another with the same reference, the reference of the whole should remain unchanged. Replacing "Hesperus" by "Phosphorus" preserves the sentence's truth value, even when it changes cognitive significance.

The view separates two jobs:

  • reference supports extensional composition and truth-functional relations;
  • sense carries what a thinker grasps and what an assertion presents as true.

Later theories often retain this division while rejecting truth values as what sentences denote. They may treat sentences as expressing propositions or having truth-conditions directly.

Compositionality and the context principle

Frege is associated with two principles that can pull in different directions.

The principle of compositionality says that the sense of a complex expression is determined by the senses of its parts and their arrangement. It explains how finite speakers understand novel sentences.

The context principle says never to ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a proposition. It blocks the assumption that every meaningful word names an independently identifiable object.

The principles are compatible if sentence context identifies the semantic role of each category while composition explains how those roles combine. The context principle constrains analysis; it need not make whole sentences semantically primitive.

Substitution in ordinary extensional contexts

Leibniz's Law permits substitution of identicals without changing truth:

a=b,F(a)∴F(b).

If Hesperus is Phosphorus, then "Hesperus is visible" and "Phosphorus is visible" cannot differ in truth merely because of the name used. Ordinary extensional contexts therefore appear sensitive to reference rather than sense.

This is one reason direct-reference theorists argue that a name's semantic contribution is its bearer. Frege can agree about the reference of the whole while maintaining that the sentences express different thoughts because their components have different senses.

Whether that difference belongs to semantic content, cognitive significance, or a pragmatic mode of presentation remains disputed.

Belief contexts and indirect reference

Substitution can fail inside reports of thought:

Leila believes that Hesperus appears in the evening.

Leila may not believe:

Leila believes that Phosphorus appears in the evening.

The names co-refer, yet replacing one with the other can turn a true belief report false. Such contexts are intensional or opaque.

Frege's proposal is that in indirect discourse an expression refers to its customary sense. The embedded name contributes its mode of presentation, because that is part of how the subject represents the object. The reference shift blocks substitution.

The solution preserves compositionality but creates an apparent hierarchy. If an expression in ordinary use refers to an object and in indirect use refers to its sense, then reports about reports may require senses of senses. A general semantics must specify these shifts without an uncontrolled proliferation of entities.

Empty names

What is the status of "Odysseus," "Vulcan," or "the present king of France" when nothing answers to the expression? If a name's meaning is only its bearer, reference failure threatens meaninglessness. A Fregean sense can remain available even when it determines no referent.

This explains why speakers can understand and reason with an empty name. It does not by itself determine the truth value of every containing sentence. Frege sometimes allowed truth-value gaps and elsewhere proposed stipulating a conventional referent. Contemporary options include:

  • gappy propositions;
  • free logics without existential commitment from names;
  • descriptive analyses;
  • pretense or fictional operators; and
  • abstract fictional artifacts as referents.

The ontological consequences are developed in Negative Existentials and Empty Names.

The challenge from direct reference

Kripke's modal, epistemic, and semantic arguments challenge the idea that an ordinary description associated with a name gives its meaning.

  • Modal: Aristotle might not have taught Alexander, but the teacher of Alexander could not have failed to teach Alexander. The name and description behave differently across possibilities.
  • Epistemic: if "Gödel" meant the prover of incompleteness, "Gödel proved incompleteness" would appear knowable merely through competence. It is empirical.
  • Semantic: if Schmidt proved the theorem and Gödel stole it, ordinary uses of "Gödel" still refer to Gödel rather than whoever satisfies the description.

Direct-reference theories conclude that a name contributes its bearer, fixed through a causal-historical chain rather than a descriptive sense. Because names are rigid designators, a true identity between names is necessary even when known a posteriori.

These arguments are strongest against descriptive analyses of reference. They do not by themselves eliminate every notion of sense. A theorist may retain modes of presentation to explain cognition while denying that they determine reference or enter the proposition expressed.

Fine-grained senses

How finely should senses be distinguished? If any expressions a rational speaker could fail to identify have different senses, distinctions proliferate. A person may know that 7+5=12 but not recognize a complicated expression as denoting 12. Logically or necessarily equivalent sentences can differ greatly in cognitive value.

Fine grain helps explain belief but creates problems:

  • speakers may never share exactly the same sense;
  • composition becomes difficult to state finitely;
  • identity criteria for senses become obscure;
  • communication may require only partial overlap; and
  • semantic content begins to track every difference in reasoning.

Coarser theories preserve shared content but must explain ignorance and discovery through something other than semantic difference: files, guises, acquaintance relations, mental representations, or pragmatic modes of presentation.

Two-dimensional approaches

Two-dimensional semantics refines the Fregean intuition by associating expressions with two kinds of intension. Roughly:

  • one dimension tracks how reference is fixed under different possible actual circumstances;
  • another tracks the referent across counterfactual circumstances once the actual circumstance is fixed.

The framework aims to represent both epistemic significance and modal rigidity. It can distinguish what a term picks out depending on which world is actual from what happens to its actual referent in counterfactual worlds.

The approach is not a neutral translation of Frege. Different versions disagree about idealization, descriptivism, and whether the first dimension is genuinely semantic. Its importance is methodological: reference and cognitive significance need not be forced into a single undifferentiated value.

What survives Frege

Few contemporary theories accept every part of Frege's system. Several lessons remain central:

  1. Reference alone does not transparently explain cognitive significance.
  2. Compositionality requires category-sensitive semantic values.
  3. Extensional and intensional contexts behave differently.
  4. Public content should not be identified with private imagery.
  5. A theory of reference and a theory of understanding may require different explanatory resources.

The unresolved choice is whether sense belongs to public semantics, to a theory of thought, or to pragmatics and cognitive architecture. Direct reference makes the world-link clearer; Fregean structure makes differences in presentation clearer. A complete theory must account for both without merely naming the missing half.
