Truth-Conditional Semantics

To understand a declarative sentence is, at least in part, to understand what the world would have to be like for the sentence to be true. A speaker may not know whether "There is life beneath Europa's ice" is true, yet can understand the claim by grasping the condition it places on the world. Truth-conditional semantics develops this idea: a theory of meaning should systematically state the truth-conditions of sentences.

The program's attraction is explanatory economy. A finite theory can derive the contents of indefinitely many sentences from axioms for words and rules for composition. Its central challenge is that stating truth-conditions relative to an interpretation does not yet explain why the language has that interpretation, how speakers know it, or whether truth-conditions distinguish contents finely enough.

Truth-conditions are not truth values

A truth value says whether a sentence is true or false. A truth-condition says under what conditions it would be true. "Snow is white" and "Grass is green" may share the truth value True while differing in truth-conditions.

This distinction explains how false sentences remain meaningful. "Snow is black" is false, but its truth-condition is intelligible: it would be true just in case snow were black. Meaning does not require actual truth.

Truth-conditions also support entailment. If every circumstance in which φ is true is one in which ψ is true, then φ semantically entails ψ:

φ⊨ψ.

Formal model theory makes this relation exact. Natural-language semantics must also identify the relevant structure of expressions and the interpretations speakers use.

Tarski's formal achievement

Tarski showed how to define truth recursively for a formal language in a richer metalanguage. Rather than treating truth as a simple list, a theory gives:

  • satisfaction clauses for atomic formulas;
  • recursive clauses for connectives;
  • clauses for quantifiers relative to variable assignments; and
  • a definition of truth for closed sentences.

For conjunction:

M,g⊨φ∧ψiffM,g⊨φ and M,g⊨ψ.

The recursion mirrors syntactic construction. Once the interpretations of primitive expressions are supplied, the theory determines truth for every complex sentence in the formal language.

This is a theory of truth for an already interpreted language, not automatically a theory of natural-language meaning. Tarski's aim was formal adequacy and avoidance of semantic paradox. The philosophical extension is Davidson's.

Davidson's proposal

Davidson proposed that an empirically adequate Tarski-style truth theory could serve as a theory of meaning for a natural language. The theory should entail, for each object-language sentence s, a theorem of the form:

“s” is true iff p.

For example:

“Snow is white” is true iff snow is white.

The left side mentions an object-language sentence; the right side uses the metalanguage to state its condition of truth. Such a result is called a T-sentence.

The proposal does not define meaning as an entity. It gives the work expected of a meaning theory to a finitely axiomatized system that derives interpretive theorems. This fits Davidson's suspicion of meanings conceived as abstract objects while preserving compositional explanation.

Why finite axiomatization matters

A list pairing every sentence with a translation would be extensionally complete but explain nothing about competence. Natural languages permit indefinitely many sentences, so a learnable theory must derive their interpretations from finite resources.

Suppose the theory contains axioms for names and a predicate:

Ref(“Ada”)=Ada,

Sat(x,“runs”)iffx runs.

Together with a compositional clause, it derives:

“Ada runs” is true iff Ada runs.

Real natural languages require treatments of ambiguity, tense, modality, context, attitudes, events, plurals, and non-declaratives. The architectural point remains: semantic competence is modelled by reusable axioms and rules rather than memorized sentence pairs.

Convention T and interpretive adequacy

Tarski's Convention T requires a materially adequate truth definition to entail every instance of the schema:

“p” is true iff p.

For formal languages, translation between object language and metalanguage is assumed. For radical interpretation, that translation is precisely what must be discovered. A theory can derive true biconditionals for the wrong reasons:

"Snow is white" is true iff grass is green.

If both sides happen to be true, the biconditional is true but not interpretive. Mere extensional truth is insufficient.

Davidsonian interpretation therefore constrains a whole theory through patterns of assent, circumstances, rational coherence, and compositional structure. Evidence for many sentences jointly rules out accidental pairings better than any isolated T-sentence.

Radical interpretation

A radical interpreter begins without a dictionary and observes speakers interacting with a shared environment. Meaning and belief must be assigned together. Assent to a sentence may reflect what it means, what the speaker believes, or both.

The interpreter uses a principle of charity: prefer assignments that make speakers broadly coherent and generally responsive to salient shared circumstances. This is not the claim that speakers are always correct. Some agreement is needed to identify stable subjects of disagreement and to treat behaviour as linguistic at all.

Truth theory and interpretation are therefore connected:

  1. observable use constrains the candidate truth theory;
  2. the theory compositionally assigns truth-conditions;
  3. the assignments make speakers' attitudes intelligible;
  4. failures of interpretation lead to revision across the network.

The method page explains why charity is a defeasible condition of interpretation rather than a guarantee of agreement.

Semantic theory versus metasemantics

A truth theory states what sentences are true under. It need not explain what makes its axioms correct. If it assigns "water" to water, a further question remains: why does the community's word have that extension?

Possible answers appeal to causal contact, historical transmission, conventions, expert deference, use, or optimal interpretation. These are metasemantic facts.

The distinction parallels formal model theory:

  • given a structure M, recursive clauses determine satisfaction;
  • the mathematics permits many structures;
  • facts outside the formal recursion must select the interpretation relevant to a language.

This is why Models, Truth, and Metalanguage is a prerequisite but not a complete philosophy of linguistic meaning.

Does a speaker know truth-conditions?

If a theory of meaning represents competence, what relation do speakers have to it? Most cannot state semantic axioms, and no finite person surveys infinitely many T-sentences.

The standard answer invokes tacit knowledge or practical competence. A speaker's interpretive capacities exhibit the structure captured by the theory without requiring conscious rehearsal. This resembles a grammar that models knowledge manifested in production and understanding.

The answer creates two obligations:

  • the theory should be learnable or psychologically realizable in some relevant sense;
  • its derivations should explain systematic patterns in actual interpretation.

An extensionally correct theory with arbitrary axioms may fail both, even if it assigns the right truth-condition to every sentence.

Context sensitivity

A context-independent T-sentence for "I am tired" would be wrong. Its truth-condition depends on who utters it. One response relativizes truth:

“I am tired” is true as uttered in ciffspeaker(c) is tired at time(c).

Indexicals can be handled through stable rules from contexts to contents. Harder cases include demonstratives, gradable adjectives, quantifier domains, and expressions whose interpretation depends on conversational purpose.

The disagreement between minimalism, indexicalism, and contextualism concerns how much contextual contribution is represented in semantic structure. It does not show that truth-conditional modelling is impossible; it changes what the inputs to the truth theory must contain.

Intensional and hyperintensional content

If truth-conditions are represented as sets of possible worlds, necessarily equivalent sentences receive the same content. But:

  • "2+2=4" and Fermat's Last Theorem are true in the same possible worlds;
  • a person may believe one without believing the other;
  • one necessary truth may explain something that another does not.

These are hyperintensional differences: distinctions finer than truth across possible worlds.

Responses include:

  • structured propositions;
  • Fregean senses;
  • impossible worlds;
  • proof or procedure-sensitive content;
  • subject-matter distinctions; and
  • separating semantic content from cognitive mode of presentation.

Truth-conditional semantics need not claim that possible-world intensions exhaust every explanatory notion of content. It must state which notion it models and how the remaining differences are handled.

Non-declarative language

Questions, commands, and exclamations are meaningful without straightforwardly being true or false. A truth-conditional program can respond by:

  • assigning them related propositional structures;
  • modelling questions as sets of possible answers;
  • treating imperatives through satisfaction or preference conditions;
  • adding force as a separate layer; or
  • generalizing meaning from truth conditions to context-change potential.

The need for extension is not a refutation. Declarative truth-conditions may remain one central case within a broader formal semantics. It does show that a complete theory of language cannot identify every kind of meaning with ordinary truth-aptness.

Strengths and limits

Truth-conditional semantics explains:

  • how sentence meaning can be compositionally generated;
  • why false sentences remain meaningful;
  • semantic entailment and contradiction;
  • scope and quantification;
  • the relation between language and possible circumstances; and
  • a rigorous interface with logic.

Its characteristic limits are:

  • it presupposes primitive interpretations or requires a metasemantics;
  • extensionally adequate theories can be interpretively wrong;
  • truth-conditions may be too coarse for cognition and explanation;
  • context must be represented without unconstrained enrichment;
  • speaker meaning and force require pragmatic theory; and
  • non-declaratives require generalization.

The right conclusion is not that truth-conditions are merely formal decoration. They capture a central dimension of declarative meaning. The mistake would be to treat that dimension as explaining by itself how language is learned, fixed, used, and understood.
