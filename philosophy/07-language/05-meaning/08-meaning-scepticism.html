Meaning Scepticism and Rule-Following

Suppose a person has calculated only finitely many additions. Asked for the first time to compute 68+57, they answer 125. What fact about their past makes plus rather than a deviant function the rule they meant? Every finite history of examples agrees with indefinitely many functions on the cases already encountered.

Kripke's influential reconstruction of Wittgenstein sharpens this into a sceptical challenge. Define quus:

xquusy={x+y,5,​x,y<57,otherwise.​

If all past calculations involved smaller numbers, behaviour fits both plus and quus. Which fact determines that the new answer should be 125 rather than 5? The challenge is not merely underdetermination by external evidence. It asks what fact, even from the speaker's own perspective, constitutes meaning addition rather than quaddition.

The sceptical argument

The argument combines finitude with normativity.

  1. A speaker's past applications and instructions are finite.
  2. Indefinitely many candidate rules agree with that finite history.
  3. Understanding a rule determines correct applications beyond the history.
  4. Therefore the constitutive fact cannot be merely the finite list of past applications.

Appealing to an explicit formula does not immediately help. The signs in the formula must themselves be interpreted. "Use ordinary addition" can be read under a deviant interpretation of "ordinary," "addition," or its component symbols. If every sign requires a further interpreted sign, an infinite regress threatens.

The sceptic concludes that no fact about the individual fixes one rule rather than all compatible alternatives. Generalized beyond arithmetic, no fact appears to determine what any expression means.

Why this is not ordinary uncertainty

Ordinary scepticism asks how someone knows which rule another person follows. More evidence might answer it. The rule-following problem is constitutive: complete knowledge of the speaker's finite history, dispositions, and inner episodes is said to leave multiple rules compatible.

Nor is the challenge that speakers occasionally misunderstand. Error presupposes a standard of correct use. The sceptic asks what establishes that standard.

The issue can be stated as a three-way tension:

  • meaning must extend beyond actual applications;
  • meaning must make future applications correct or incorrect;
  • no candidate descriptive fact appears both determinate and normative.

Dispositionalism

A natural answer says that to mean addition is to be disposed to answer addition questions in the relevant way. The rule is not fixed by actual past behaviour but by counterfactual behaviour across possible cases.

Several objections follow.

Finitude

Human beings cannot calculate arbitrarily large sums. For some inputs a speaker has no disposition to answer because they would die, tire, or run out of memory. A mathematical rule has applications beyond the subject's physical capacity.

Error

A person can be disposed to make a carrying mistake. If actual idealized disposition constitutes meaning, the habitual error threatens to become part of what the person means rather than an incorrect application.

Idealization

The theory can appeal to what the speaker would do under ideal conditions. But which idealization? Ideal arithmetic competence yields addition only if the relevant standard of correctness has already been selected. Defining ideality semantically would be circular.

Normativity

A disposition describes what a person would do, not what they ought to do. From the fact that someone is disposed to answer 125, it does not immediately follow that 125 is correct.

Dispositions remain evidence of understanding. The challenge is whether they constitute it without supplementary norms.

Mental instructions and interpretations

Perhaps the speaker grasps an inner formula, image, algorithm, or intention that fixes the rule. Kripke's sceptic applies the same question to that item. A formula is a set of signs; an algorithm contains steps; an image can exemplify only finitely much. Each seems capable of deviant interpretation.

An inner object could fix meaning if it had an intrinsic interpretation. But asserting that interpretation simply relocates the semantic fact the reduction was meant to explain.

This is the force of Wittgenstein's claim that an interpretation by itself does not determine application. What stops the regress is not a final sign accompanied by an unmistakable mental glow.

Primitive meaning facts

Another response accepts irreducible facts of the form:

The speaker means addition by "plus."

Such a fact would be normative and determinate without reduction to behaviour or disposition. The sceptic objects that it can appear metaphysically and epistemically mysterious. How does a finite state determine indefinitely many applications? What evidence reveals the fact, and how does it guide action?

Primitivism is not inconsistent. Every explanation ends somewhere. Its cost is that it forgoes the hoped-for naturalistic or practice-based account of meaning. A satisfactory primitivism should say how meaning facts relate to learning, public correction, and causal explanation rather than merely label them primitive.

The sceptical solution

Kripke distinguishes a straight solution, which identifies a fact satisfying the sceptic's demand, from a sceptical solution, which rejects that demand while vindicating ordinary meaning talk.

On the sceptical solution, assertions that someone means addition are not descriptions of a private fact that determines every future application. They are licensed within a community's practice of:

  • training speakers;
  • checking responses;
  • correcting deviations;
  • projecting shared procedures;
  • attributing understanding; and
  • excluding unresponsive performances.

Meaning claims have assertibility conditions grounded in communal practice rather than truth-conditions requiring a superlative individual fact.

The solution is modelled on Hume's response to induction: sceptical argument defeats a certain philosophical picture, not the practice itself.

Community agreement

A community supplies standards unavailable to a solitary individual. A learner's seeming-right can be checked against how competent participants continue, explain, and correct. Agreement in judgments stabilizes the distinction between following a rule and merely thinking one is following it.

This does not mean that a majority makes any answer mathematically true. The claim concerns what makes a sign part of a rule-governed practice. Participants agree not only in verdicts but in techniques of proof, calculation, correction, and application.

Several objections remain:

  • a whole community could make a systematic mistake;
  • communities change their rules;
  • innovators can be correct against current consensus;
  • agreement appears descriptive rather than normative; and
  • solitary or isolated agents seem capable of following rules.

A practice account must distinguish constituting a standard from infallibly satisfying it. Community is a site of norm-governed activity, not an oracle.

Robinson Crusoe and solitary rule-following

Could an isolated person invent and follow a rule? Denying this outright seems too strong. A solitary mathematician can check one calculation against another, preserve records, notice inconsistency, and distinguish current inclination from an established procedure.

A social account can reply that these capacities were acquired through prior participation in public practices. But a permanently isolated rational agent remains a conceptual possibility.

The deeper point may be publicness in principle rather than current social presence. A rule must support repeatable standards, correction, and distinctions not exhausted by one present impression. Whether those standards require an actual community is further than the regress argument alone establishes.

Wittgenstein and "Kripkenstein"

Kripke presents the sceptical paradox as "Wittgenstein's" and labels his reading an interpretation rather than a straightforward report. The distinction matters.

Wittgenstein's own discussion emphasizes:

  • interpretations do not determine themselves;
  • rule-following is embedded in practices;
  • training and agreement in judgments are conditions of language;
  • there is a way of grasping a rule exhibited in obeying it; and
  • philosophical puzzlement arises when rules are detached from their use.

This need not amount to the global semantic scepticism that no meaning facts exist. Many interpreters read Wittgenstein as dissolving a mistaken demand for an interpretation that rails invisibly determine, not as replacing truth with communal assertibility.

"Kripkenstein" names the specific sceptical argument and solution. Evaluating it should not settle exegesis of Wittgenstein by stipulation.

Normativity: guidance or correctness?

Meaning is called normative in several senses:

  1. Correctness: if "plus" means addition, some applications are correct and others incorrect.
  2. Reasons: meaning may give a speaker reason to apply a term in one way.
  3. Obligation: a speaker may be required to use an expression according to public standards.
  4. Commitment: an assertion may undertake inferential responsibilities.

The sceptical argument is strongest about correctness. It does not automatically show that semantic facts generate all-things-considered obligations. A person can have reason to lie or deliberately violate a convention while still knowing the literal meaning.

Separating these senses prevents the debate from demanding more normativity than meaning requires.

Finite facts and infinite reach

The quus case assumes that a finite base must uniquely determine an extension over indefinitely many cases. Similar problems arise for scientific laws, algorithms, grammars, and inductive projection. Meaning may not be uniquely defective.

Responses appeal to structure rather than enumeration:

  • a finite algorithm can specify unbounded outputs;
  • simplicity can privilege some continuations;
  • biological or social function can select a rule;
  • causal history can constrain interpretation;
  • rational coherence can compare global assignments; and
  • primitive capacities can manifest general competence.

Each response must avoid presupposing the intended interpretation. But the existence of multiple mathematically definable alternatives does not by itself show that all are equally eligible explanations of a speaker embedded in a world and practice.

What the challenge establishes

Rule-following scepticism defeats several simple reductions:

  • meaning is not a finite list of applications;
  • actual disposition cannot by itself preserve error;
  • an uninterpreted inner sign cannot terminate interpretation;
  • intention cannot fix a rule merely by containing another representation; and
  • normativity must be explained rather than read off causal regularity.

It does not by itself establish that meaning is unreal or that majority agreement makes every application correct. Its constructive lesson is that understanding should be explained through a structured combination of capacity, practice, world-involvement, history, and standards of correction.

That lesson supports meaning as use while leaving room for truth-conditions, reference, and convention. The sceptical paradox remains a test for each: what makes one continuation correct, and how can finite speakers be answerable to it?
