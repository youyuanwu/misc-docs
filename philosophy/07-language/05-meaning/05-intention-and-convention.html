Intention and Convention

A speaker can communicate something without encoding it in the sentence uttered. At dinner, "The salt is out of reach" can be a request to pass it. A raised eyebrow can signal doubt. An invented sign can acquire a one-off meaning when its purpose is made recognizable. These cases suggest that communication depends fundamentally on intentions rather than on a fixed code.

Grice's theory of non-natural meaning analyses speaker meaning through an overt intention to produce an effect by means of an audience's recognition of that very intention. It explains occasion-specific communication, implication, and indirect speech. It does not by itself explain how words have stable meanings across occasions. For that, intention-based accounts need a theory of convention.

Lewis models conventions as stable solutions to recurring coordination problems. Together, Grice and Lewis suggest a division of labour: communicative intentions explain what a speaker means on an occasion; conventions explain why familiar expressions provide reliable means for making such intentions recognizable.

Natural and non-natural meaning

Grice begins by distinguishing two uses of "means."

Natural meaning concerns indication:

Those spots mean measles.

If the spots really mean measles in this sense, it follows that measles is present. The relation is factive and does not depend on anyone intending to communicate.

Non-natural meaning concerns communication:

The three rings of the bell mean that the bus is full.

The signal can mean that the bus is full even if the bell was rung in error and the bus is not full. Non-natural meaning permits falsity and depends on agents or conventions. It is the target of Grice's analysis.

The distinction prevents a causal indicator from being treated automatically as a linguistic sign. Smoke naturally indicates fire under suitable conditions; a smoke signal communicates only within an intentional or conventional practice.

Speaker meaning

A first approximation says:

A speaker S means something by an utterance u if S intends u to produce an effect in an audience.

This is too weak. Secretly causing an audience to become nervous, or leaving evidence intended to mislead an investigator, need not be communication. The intended route matters.

Grice's central idea is reflexive intention. Roughly, S means that p by u when:

  1. S intends an audience A to produce a response related to p;
  2. S intends A to recognize that intention; and
  3. S intends A's recognition to be at least part of the reason for the response.

For an assertion, the intended response may be belief; for a request, an action or intention to act. Communication is overt influence: the audience is meant to recognize what the speaker is trying to do.

Why recognition matters

Suppose Ada leaves Ben's umbrella by the door so that he will take it, but she does not want him to notice that she arranged this. Her action is intentional influence, not a request in the Gricean sense. Its success depends on concealing the intention.

By contrast, if Ada points to the umbrella and looks toward the rain, success depends on Ben recognizing her intention that he take it. Recognition is not incidental; it is the communicative mechanism.

The analysis explains why communication can occur without a shared language. Gesture, demonstration, and improvised signs work when the intention is sufficiently recognizable. It also explains how conventional sentences can communicate more than they encode.

Difficulties for intention analysis

Over-complexity

Ordinary speakers, children, and spontaneous conversational partners may not represent nested intentions explicitly. If communication requires conscious reasoning about another person's recognition of an intention, the account is psychologically implausible.

A defender can treat the analysis as specifying the structure of the act rather than a conscious calculation. Speakers can possess practical social competence without formulating its conditions.

Audience response

Different speech acts aim at different responses. Assertions need not aim to cause belief: a teacher may remind students of something they already know, and a witness may put a fact on record for an audience expected to remain sceptical.

The intended effect may therefore be uptake, recognition of commitment, or provision of a reason rather than a specific psychological change. Refining the response makes the theory more flexible but less reductive.

Complex and open audiences

Written works, public signs, broadcasts, and online posts may address indefinite future audiences. Institutional communication can persist beyond its author's intentions. Joint authors may have no single detailed intention.

These cases favour conventions and public norms as partly independent of occurrent speaker psychology.

Accidental and unsuccessful communication

A speaker can reveal information unintentionally, and an audience can understand a meaning the speaker failed competently to express. Conversely, a well-formed communicative intention can fail through ignorance or ambiguity.

The theory must distinguish:

  • what the expression means;
  • what the speaker meant;
  • what the audience reasonably interpreted; and
  • what effect occurred.

No one intention determines all four.

From speaker meaning to expression meaning

Even a successful analysis of one-off speaker meaning leaves a genetic question: how do repeatable expression types acquire stable meanings?

A simple answer appeals to regularity. Speakers repeatedly use expression e to mean m, and audiences repeatedly interpret it that way. But accidental regularity is not yet convention. Everyone may leave a building through the same door merely because it is the only door.

Convention must explain:

  • why conformity is expected;
  • why each person's conformity depends partly on others';
  • how deviation can be criticized;
  • how a practice can begin and change; and
  • why speakers can exploit the convention for indirect or novel communication.

Coordination problems

Lewis analyses convention through recurring coordination problems. Several patterns of action would benefit everyone if participants converged, but no one option is uniquely required independently of what others do.

Driving on the left and driving on the right are standard examples. Either convention can solve the problem; mixing them is dangerous. Language contains analogous cases. Many possible sounds could be used for water. Once a community converges on "water," each speaker has reason to continue because others do.

In simplified form, a regularity R is a convention in population P when:

  • nearly everyone conforms to R in recurring situations;
  • nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform;
  • each prefers to conform on condition that others do;
  • alternative regularities could have served the coordinating role; and
  • these facts are appropriately mutually expected or commonly known.

Different formulations weaken or revise the common-knowledge requirement, but the central idea is conditional preference sustained by expectations.

Signaling systems

Lewis's signaling games provide a simple model. A sender observes a state, chooses a signal, and a receiver chooses an action. Their interests align when the action fits the state.

graph LR
  W[World state] --> S[Sender chooses signal]
  S --> R[Receiver interprets signal]
  R --> A[Receiver chooses action]
  A --> O[Shared outcome]

A stable pairing between states, signals, and responses can emerge as an equilibrium. Nothing about one signal intrinsically requires one meaning; the mapping is sustained by coordinated practice.

The model clarifies arbitrariness and stability, but natural language exceeds simple signaling:

  • speakers' interests can conflict;
  • signals combine compositionally;
  • the same form serves many contexts;
  • speakers discuss absent, abstract, and impossible matters;
  • audiences evaluate reasons rather than merely choose actions; and
  • linguistic norms persist amid partial misunderstanding.

Signaling theory is a model of one foundation for convention, not a complete semantics.

Common knowledge and regress

Coordination appears to require expectations about expectations:

  • I expect you to interpret "water" in the familiar way.
  • I expect you to expect me to use it that way.
  • I expect you to expect me to expect you to interpret it that way.

Common knowledge idealizes this nested structure. Real speakers do not consciously represent an infinite hierarchy. Shared perceptual access, public training, institutional practices, and defaults can provide practical substitutes.

The same issue affects reflexive intention. An analysis can state constitutive relations more complex than the representations agents explicitly token. It must still connect the idealization to capacities actual speakers could acquire and exercise.

Conventional meaning is not speaker meaning

The distinction explains several familiar cases.

Malapropism. A speaker uses the wrong word but their intended message remains recoverable. Speaker meaning diverges from conventional expression meaning.

Sarcasm. The sentence conventionally expresses one proposition while the speaker communicates a contrary evaluation through context and recognizable intention.

Lying. A speaker uses conventional meaning correctly to assert something they believe false. Semantic competence is intact despite deceptive intention.

Code creation. Speakers can stipulate a temporary signal when the intention and mapping are overt. Repetition may turn the local arrangement into a convention.

These cases show why reducing conventional meaning to what one speaker intends on one occasion fails. They also show why convention alone cannot explain everything communicated.

Circularity

A reductive program hopes to derive expression meaning from speaker meaning and speaker meaning from non-semantic intentions. Circularity threatens at both stages.

To intend that an audience believe that p, a speaker already entertains the content that p. The theory may reduce linguistic meaning to mental content, but it has not yet explained content in general. That issue belongs to Intentionality and Aboutness.

At the conventional level, identifying the relevant regularity may require describing participants as using a sign with a meaning. A non-circular account must characterize the practice through actions, expectations, and world-directed purposes without smuggling the semantic relation into its premises.

Reduction of public language to thought can still be informative even if it is not a reduction of all intentionality.

Normativity

Conventions explain why coordination rewards conformity, but semantic mistakes are not always prudential failures. A speaker can misuse a term even when no immediate coordination suffers. Experts can correct an entire population's classification, and minority innovations can eventually become correct.

Possible sources of semantic normativity include:

  • constitutive rules of the practice;
  • commitments undertaken in assertion;
  • standards implicit in correction and teaching;
  • successful reference to shared objects; and
  • rational requirements on interpretation.

Conditional preference is therefore at most part of the explanation. Linguistic conventions are embedded in inquiry, inference, and institutions, not merely repeated solutions to symmetric games.

A division of labour

Intention and convention make their strongest contribution when assigned different roles:

Explanatory targetPrimary resource
What the speaker communicates herereflexive communicative intention
How the audience recovers itrecognition, context, and pragmatic inference
Why an expression is a reusable meansconvention and shared expectations
Why a name or kind term reaches its referentconvention plus causal and social history
How complex sentence content is generatedsyntax and compositional semantics
Why use can be correct or mistakenpublic norms, inquiry, and world-involvement

The table is not a proof that these resources fit together without tension. It states the burden on a comprehensive theory. Grice explains how communication can outrun a code; Lewis explains how a code can be stable without being natural. Neither alone explains every dimension of meaning.
