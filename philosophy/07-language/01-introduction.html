What Is Philosophy of Language?

Language is at once familiar and theoretically puzzling. Children acquire a finite vocabulary and a finite set of constructions, yet speakers understand indefinitely many new sentences. A few sounds can concern a distant galaxy, a possible event, a number, or a person who never existed. The same sentence can be used to assert, joke, warn, insinuate, or deceive. Different expressions can pick out the same object while presenting it differently, and identical words can communicate different things in different contexts.

Philosophy of language asks what makes these achievements possible and what they show about thought, reality, and social practice. It does not begin by assuming that meaning is one kind of entity. Instead it separates the explanatory tasks hidden by ordinary talk of "what a sentence means."

This page introduces five central questions, distinguishes the principal objects of study, and establishes recurring cases. The next pages examine the semantics-pragmatics distinction, compare theories of meaning, and assess the subject's methods and evidence.

Five central questions

What is linguistic meaning?

Competent speakers distinguish meaningful expressions from noise, understand new combinations, recognize some uses as mistakes, and often translate between different forms. A theory of meaning must explain at least:

  • productivity - finite speakers understand unboundedly many sentences;
  • systematicity - understanding related expressions comes in structured families;
  • compositionality - the meanings of complex expressions depend in some way on their parts and arrangement;
  • publicity - meanings can be shared, taught, corrected, and contested;
  • normativity - using an expression correctly is not merely using it frequently;
  • world-involvement - language can be true, false, accurate, or mistaken.

Theories disagree over which feature is fundamental. Truth-conditional approaches begin with conditions under which sentences are true. Use theories begin with rule-governed practice. Intention-based theories begin with communicative intentions. Inferentialism begins with the role an expression plays in reasoning. These need not be exclusive at every level: one theory may describe semantic content while another explains how that content is fixed.

How does reference work?

A name can be used to speak about a person, a demonstrative to pick out a visible object, and a natural-kind term to concern samples whose internal nature most speakers cannot identify. Reference raises several puzzles:

  • Must a speaker know a uniquely identifying description of a referent?
  • Can a name remain meaningful when nothing bears it?
  • Does "water" refer by associated descriptions, causal history, deference to experts, or some combination?
  • What fixes the referent of "I," "here," "that," or "today" on an occasion?
  • Can two people refer to the same thing while thinking about it differently?

Reference is not the whole of meaning. "The morning star" and "the evening star" both refer to Venus, but learning that the morning star is the evening star can be informative. Whatever else a theory says, it must account for this cognitive significance.

How do sentence meanings depend on their parts?

Someone who knows the words in "every student read a book" can understand the sentence without having encountered that exact token. Yet its parts permit more than one scope reading: perhaps one book was read by every student, or perhaps each student read some book. Formal semantics tries to represent such structure precisely.

A simple model assigns semantic values to expressions and rules for combining them. If a name denotes an object s and a predicate denotes a function from objects to truth values, composition yields:

[[Socrates is wise]]=[[is wise]]([[Socrates]]).

The notation makes an explanatory claim visible: the whole is understood through the parts and their mode of combination. Natural language complicates that picture through ambiguity, context sensitivity, idiom, coercion, ellipsis, anaphora, and non-literal use. Those are tests of a compositional theory, not reasons to abandon precision.

How does context affect what is communicated?

"I am here now" changes its truth-conditions with speaker, place, and time. "The meeting starts at noon" may communicate a warning to leave, although no warning is encoded in the sentence. "Sam stopped smoking" normally carries the background assumption that Sam smoked before. A complete account must distinguish:

  • what the linguistic expression conventionally contributes;
  • what context supplies to determine what is said;
  • what the speaker intends the audience to infer;
  • what the utterance presupposes or takes for granted;
  • what act the speaker performs; and
  • what effects the utterance has.

The boundary between semantics and pragmatics is disputed because context can enter interpretation in more than one way. Some dependence is overtly triggered by words such as "I"; other enrichment appears to be supplied by conversational purposes.

What makes an interpretation correct?

Formal semantics can define many interpretation functions. Only some correspond to what actual speakers mean. This raises a metasemantic question: what facts make "water" refer to water, make a conjunction express conjunction, or make one translation better than another?

Candidate answers appeal to:

  • individual intentions;
  • causal relations to objects and environments;
  • conventions sustained by coordination;
  • patterns of use and correction;
  • inferential roles;
  • social deference and historical transmission;
  • rational interpretation under principles of charity; or
  • combinations of these.

This question separates a formal assignment of values from an explanation of why the assignment is linguistically correct. Model theory supplies the former for formal languages. Philosophy of language must also address the latter for languages used by communities.

The distinctions prevent common invalid inferences. A sentence type may be ambiguous although a contextual utterance is not. A speaker can mean something without the words conventionally encoding it. An audience can be affected in a way the speaker did not intend. A theory explaining one row has not automatically explained the others.

Language, thought, and the world

Language relates speakers to both thought and reality, but the direction of dependence is contested.

One picture begins with thought. Speakers possess concepts and intentions, then use public symbols to express them. On this view linguistic content is at least partly derived from mental content. It explains why the same thought can be expressed in different languages, but it owes an account of how thought represents independently. That problem is developed in Intentionality and Aboutness.

A second picture begins with public practice. Possessing a concept requires mastery of rules for using words, drawing inferences, and correcting errors. Thought itself may depend on initiation into linguistic norms. This makes publicity and correction central, but it must accommodate infants, animals, imagery, and other apparently non-linguistic cognition.

A third picture emphasizes direct relations to the environment and community. What a term means may depend on its causal history, the samples from which it was learned, and deference to other speakers. Meaning then is not wholly determined by anything inside an individual mind.

None of these views implies that language mirrors the world without mediation. Grammar, conceptual schemes, practical interests, and conversational context shape what speakers can conveniently say. Nor does mediation imply that reference and truth are merely invented. The question is which contributions come from world, mind, language, and practice.

Recurring test cases

The section repeatedly returns to a small set of cases:

  • "Hesperus is Phosphorus." The names co-refer, yet the identity is informative. The case tests sense, direct reference, and the objects of belief.
  • "The present king of France is bald." Nothing presently fits the description. The case tests reference failure, presupposition, and logical form.
  • "Water is H2O." Ordinary speakers historically lacked chemical knowledge. The case tests natural-kind reference, externalism, rigidity, and necessity.
  • "I am here now." Reference changes systematically with context. The case tests character, content, and the role of an utterance situation.
  • "Every student read a book." Scope affects truth-conditions without changing the words. The case tests syntax-semantics composition.
  • "Sam stopped smoking." The sentence presents prior smoking as background. The case tests presupposition and projection.
  • "Some students passed." In many contexts this suggests that not all passed, although classical semantics makes "some" compatible with "all." The case tests conversational implicature.
  • "Can you open the window?" A question about ability normally functions as a request. The case tests sentence type, speaker meaning, and force.

No single intuition about one case should decide a theory. The point is comparative: which account handles the cases together, with the least arbitrary machinery and the clearest division of explanatory labour?

What the subject can establish

Philosophy of language can clarify the commitments of theories, reveal distinctions hidden by ordinary terminology, construct formal models, and test explanations across cases. It cannot determine meanings by stipulation while pretending to report existing practice, infer metaphysics directly from grammar, or treat one speaker's reaction as the architecture of every language.

Progress therefore requires several kinds of discipline: formal explicitness, attention to attested use, cross-linguistic caution, sensitivity to context, and clear separation of semantic, pragmatic, psychological, and metaphysical claims. The method page develops those standards.
