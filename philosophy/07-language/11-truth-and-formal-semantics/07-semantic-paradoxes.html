Semantic Paradoxes

Semantic vocabulary permits sentences that turn evaluation back on themselves. The Liar sentence:

This sentence is not true.

appears true if false and false if true. Related paradoxes involve definability, denotation, groundedness, and predicates such as "heterological."

Semantic paradoxes test theories of truth and meaning. Solutions restrict languages, revise logic, allow truth-value gaps or gluts, or construct partial fixed points.

The Liar

Let L say of itself:

L↔¬True(┌L┐).

With the T-schema:

True(┌L┐)↔L,

classical reasoning derives contradiction.

The paradox requires sufficient self-reference, a truth predicate, and classical principles connecting them.

Strengthened Liar

If the ordinary Liar is assigned neither true nor false, consider:

This sentence is not true.

It appears true whenever not true, including when gappy. Revenge sentences adapt to the proposed solution:

This sentence is either false or gappy.

A stable theory must express its semantic status without recreating paradox.

Tarskian hierarchy

Tarski separates levels:

  • object language L0​ has no truth predicate for itself;
  • metalanguage L1​ defines truth for L0​;
  • L2​ defines truth for L1​; and so on.

The Liar cannot be formulated at one level with its own truth predicate.

Advantages:

  • classical consistency;
  • precise formal semantics.

Costs:

  • ordinary language appears to use one untyped "true";
  • cross-level generalizations become cumbersome;
  • hierarchy claims require higher levels indefinitely.

Truth-value gaps

A gappy theory says the Liar is neither true nor false. Classical bivalence is rejected while contradiction is avoided.

Kripkean fixed-point theories begin with ungrounded truth values and iteratively assign truth where evaluation is determined. Grounded sentences stabilize; self-referential Liar sentences remain undefined.

The approach explains a distinction between grounded and ungrounded truth talk. Strengthened revenge remains a challenge.

Truth-value gluts

A dialetheist allows some sentences to be both true and false. The Liar is a glut rather than a reason to prohibit self-reference.

To avoid triviality, logic must be paraconsistent: from p and ¬p, not everything follows.

This preserves an unrestricted truth predicate at the cost of rejecting explosion and accepting true contradictions.

Paraconsistent Logic develops the formal systems.

Revision theories

Revision theories treat truth evaluation dynamically. Start with a hypothesis about the truth predicate, revise according to the T-schema, and examine the resulting sequence.

Ordinary sentences stabilize; the Liar oscillates. Semantic status is represented by revision behaviour rather than one fixed truth value.

The view models self-reference without gaps or gluts at each stage, but global statements about stability require care.

Curry's paradox

Curry's sentence says:

If this sentence is true, then absurdity.

With contraction and naive truth rules, absurdity follows without explicit negation. This shows that changing negation alone does not solve every semantic paradox.

Solutions may restrict structural rules, implication, or truth introduction.

Other semantic paradoxes

  • Grelling: Is "heterological" heterological?
  • Berry: "The least number not nameable in fewer than nineteen syllables."
  • Richard: definable real numbers generate a diagonal real.
  • Yablo: an infinite sequence where each sentence says all later sentences are false, avoiding direct self-reference.

The family shows that circularity can arise through definability and dependency, not only explicit self-naming.

Object language and natural language

Formal languages can be stratified. Natural language appears semantically universal: it can discuss its own sentences and truth.

Responses include:

  • natural language is implicitly hierarchical;
  • ordinary truth is context-sensitive;
  • paradoxical sentences fail to express propositions;
  • logic must be revised;
  • inconsistency is locally tolerated.

Usage alone does not choose among these architectures.

Meaning and paradox

A paradoxical sentence can be grammatically meaningful while lacking stable semantic value. This separates:

  • syntactic well-formedness;
  • compositional instructions;
  • successful reference;
  • determinate truth value.

Theories should explain why competent speakers can understand the setup even when no classical evaluation exists.
