Tarski and Truth Theories

Tarski showed how to define truth for a formal language without contradiction by using a richer metalanguage. His recursive method is foundational for logic and formal semantics. Davidson adapted it into a proposal for natural-language meaning.

The formal achievement and philosophical deployment should be separated. Tarski assumes translation and interpretation; Davidson uses empirical interpretation to constrain a truth theory.

Object language and metalanguage

The object language is interpreted. The metalanguage contains names for its expressions and enough resources to state their satisfaction conditions.

Use and mention must be distinguished:

  • Snow is white: uses words to discuss snow.
  • "Snow is white": mentions a sentence.

A truth predicate applying to object-language sentences belongs to the metalanguage.

Convention T

A materially adequate truth definition entails every instance:

“p” is true iff p.

For example:

“Snow is white” is true iff snow is white.

The right side translates the object-language sentence. Convention T tests adequacy relative to that assumed translation.

Recursive satisfaction

Truth for complex formulas is defined through satisfaction:

M,g⊨P(t)

according to interpretations of P and t.

Then:

M,g⊨¬φiffM,g⊨φ,

M,g⊨φ∧ψiffM,g⊨φ and M,g⊨ψ,

M,g⊨∃xφiffsome g′∼x​g satisfies φ.

A sentence is true when satisfied under every assignment.

Compositionality

The recursion mirrors syntax. Finite clauses determine truth for indefinitely many sentences.

This explains why Tarskian architecture attracts theories of meaning:

  • finite axiomatization;
  • compositional derivation;
  • explicit quantifier and connective clauses;
  • formal entailment.

The theory presupposes semantic values for primitives and the correct syntactic parse.

Undefinability

A sufficiently expressive consistent theory cannot define its own unrestricted truth predicate while satisfying all T-biconditionals. Diagonalization produces a liar-like sentence:

This sentence is not true.

Tarski avoids contradiction through a hierarchy: object-language truth is defined in a stronger metalanguage.

The formal details and qualifications remain in Models, Truth, and Metalanguage.

Davidson's adaptation

Davidson proposes that a truth theory can serve as a theory of meaning when constrained by evidence of use and interpretation.

It should derive:

“s” is true iff p

where the right side interprets s. A finite theory explains productive competence.

Material truth alone is insufficient:

"Snow is white" is true iff grass is green.

can be true accidentally. The whole theory must fit speakers' dispositions, context, and compositional structure.

Context sensitivity

Natural language requires relativization:

“I am tired” is true in context ciffspeaker(c) is tired at time(c).

Ambiguity, tense, demonstratives, and pragmatic enrichment complicate the object language. A truth theory must represent the appropriate structure without treating every contextual fact as semantic.

Limits

Tarskian truth does not by itself:

  • select an intended interpretation;
  • explain speaker meaning;
  • handle every non-declarative;
  • distinguish necessary equivalents;
  • ground primitive reference;
  • provide a metaphysics of truth.

It supplies a rigorous compositional framework. The philosophy lies in connecting that framework to speakers and practices.

Assessment

Tarski gives a method for truth in interpreted formal languages. Davidson gives a program for using that method in interpretation. Confusing them makes formal logic appear to solve a metasemantic question it assumes.

The durable division is:

Tarskian recursion+empirical interpretation+metasemantic grounding.

All three are needed for a complete natural-language theory.
