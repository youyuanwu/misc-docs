Deflationism and Minimalism about Truth

Deflationists deny that truth names a deep property requiring correspondence, coherence, or metaphysical reduction. To say that "snow is white" is true is, in a central sense, just to say that snow is white.

Truth remains indispensable because it enables generalization and endorsement. The question for philosophy of language is whether this thin role can support a truth-conditional theory of meaning or whether meaning must be explained independently.

Disquotation

The core schema:

“p” is true iff p.

Removing quotation and "is true" yields the original claim. Truth appears disquotational rather than descriptive of an additional relation.

Instances require care with context, indexicals, and languages. The right side must use an adequate translation.

Expressive utility

Truth allows claims not expressible by listing:

Everything Ada said is true.

Some consequence of the theory is false.

What the theorem states is true.

The predicate performs an expressive or logical function by enabling quantification over propositions or sentences.

Redundancy theory

A redundancy theory says "It is true that p" says no more than p.

Not every occurrence can be syntactically eliminated, especially generalizations. Modern deflationism therefore emphasizes expressive utility rather than literal redundancy in every sentence.

Prosententialism

Prosentential theories treat "That is true" as functioning like a pronoun referring back to a prior assertional content.

Truth vocabulary enables anaphoric endorsement:

Ada said the bridge is closed. That is true.

The view avoids treating truth as a property of propositions while requiring an account of content anaphora.

Minimalism

Minimalism holds that the property of truth is exhausted by instances of the equivalence schema. There may be a property, but it has no underlying nature beyond its role.

This distinguishes minimalism from eliminating truth talk entirely.

Truth-conditional meaning

Can a deflationary truth predicate explain meaning?

A critic argues:

  1. truth-conditional semantics says meaning is explained through truth;
  2. deflationary truth has no explanatory nature;
  3. therefore it cannot ground meaning.

A defender replies that recursive truth conditions explain composition even if truth itself is thin. The explanatory work lies in the axioms connecting expressions to conditions, not in a substantial property.

Thus deflationism may reject truth-first metaphysics while retaining formal semantics.

Metasemantics

The biconditional:

"Snow is white" is true iff snow is white

does not explain why "snow" refers to snow. Deflationism makes this especially clear. Reference and interpretation require causal, social, conventional, or interpretive grounding.

Truth theory can display meaning once fixed without creating it.

Truthmakers

Truthmaker theory asks which entities make truths true. Deflationists may reject the demand if "is true" introduces no substantial property needing a ground.

Truthmaker theorists answer that worldly dependence concerns propositions, not the metaphysical thickness of the truth predicate. Snow's being white still explains the truth that snow is white.

The debate is developed in Truthmakers.

Norms

Truth is often proposed as the norm of assertion or belief. A deflationist can say:

Assert p only if p.

General truth vocabulary expresses the norm compactly without naming a deep property.

The normative role therefore does not by itself refute deflationism.

Assessment

Deflationism captures:

  • disquotation;
  • generalization;
  • endorsement;
  • logical economy.

Its pressure points are:

  • explanatory uses of truth;
  • truthmaker dependence;
  • semantic paradox;
  • cross-language translation;
  • truth as an aim or norm.

The central distinction is between using truth in a compositional semantic theory and using a substantive theory of truth to explain meaning. Deflationism can accept the first while doubting the second.
