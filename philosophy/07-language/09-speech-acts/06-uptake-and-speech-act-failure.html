Uptake and Speech-Act Failure

A speaker can utter meaningful words, intend a recognizable act, and still fail to perform it successfully. The audience may not hear, may misunderstand the force, may lack required standing, or may refuse participation. Different failures occur at different layers.

Uptake is audience recognition of an utterance's content and force. It is distinct from agreement, belief, compliance, and perlocutionary effect. Whether uptake is required for the existence of an act varies by act and theory.

Calling all of these "miscommunication" hides theoretically important differences.

Uptake without compliance

A hearer can understand an order and refuse. The order achieved uptake but not compliance.

Likewise, an audience can recognize an assertion and reject it, or recognize a promise and distrust fulfilment.

Uptake concerns recognition, not success in changing belief or action.

Is uptake constitutive?

For some acts, a publicly available attempt may suffice. A warning left on a sign can remain a warning even if no one reads it.

For conversational acts such as accepting an offer, securing agreement, or answering, audience recognition appears more central. A private acceptance unknown to the offerer may not complete the transaction.

A moderate view makes required uptake act-relative:

  • communicative attempts can exist without actual recognition;
  • some conventional consequences require receipt;
  • some joint acts require reciprocal acknowledgment.

Misunderstanding content

An audience may recognize force while misidentifying content:

Put it there.

They understand a directive but choose the wrong object or location.

Reference resolution, ambiguity, and presupposition can each fail while the act type is clear. Repair should target the content rather than force.

Misunderstanding force

The words may be understood while the act is not:

  • irony taken literally;
  • warning heard as prediction;
  • request heard as optional remark;
  • joke heard as assertion;
  • rehearsal heard as promise.

Force indicators can conflict. Grammar, tone, context, relationship, and convention must be integrated.

Authority failure

A person can be understood perfectly yet lack authority. An unauthorized "You are fired" may be an attempted dismissal without institutional effect.

Authority failure differs from misunderstanding:

  • content and force are recognized;
  • the conventional consequence does not follow;
  • the utterance may still threaten, deceive, or communicate intention.

Speech-act theory should distinguish illocutionary attempt from successful institutional act.

Defective procedure

A procedure can be attempted but incomplete:

  • missing witness;
  • wrong formula;
  • expired deadline;
  • improper sequence;
  • ambiguous scope.

Some defects void the act; others make it contestable or repairable. The practice's rules determine consequences.

Refusal of uptake

An audience can strategically refuse to recognize intended force:

I am not treating that as an apology.

This may mean:

  • the act lacked sincerity;
  • required acknowledgment was absent;
  • the form was insufficient;
  • the audience refuses reconciliation despite recognizing an attempted apology.

The last case shows that refusing consequences is not always failure to understand. Speakers and audiences can disagree about whether felicity conditions were met.

Repair

Repair mechanisms include:

  • repetition;
  • paraphrase;
  • explicit performative;
  • clarification of reference;
  • checking understanding;
  • metalinguistic negotiation;
  • institutional correction.

Repair provides evidence about where failure occurred:

I heard the words, but was that a request?

targets force.

Which report?

targets reference.

Are you authorized to decide?

targets standing.

Overhearing and unintended audiences

An overhearer can recognize a speech act not addressed to them. They achieve understanding without being the act's intended participant.

Public statements can also affect unintended audiences. Uptake by them does not retroactively make them addressees.

Audience roles should distinguish:

  • addressee;
  • intended hearer;
  • ratified participant;
  • overhearer;
  • later interpreter.

Recorded and delayed communication

A recording separates production from uptake. A warning recorded today may be heard tomorrow. A will or contract can take effect later.

This shows that speaker and audience need not be co-present. Conventions can preserve force across media and time, while context must determine which coordinates belong to production and which to reception.

Assessment

Speech-act failure is not binary. A speaker may succeed at locution, reference, and force while failing institutionally or perlocutionarily.

A useful diagnosis asks:

  1. Were meaningful words produced?
  2. What content and referents were available?
  3. What force was intended and publicly indicated?
  4. Did the speaker have standing?
  5. Did the audience recognize the act?
  6. Was acknowledgment or compliance required?
  7. Which conventional consequence followed?

Only after locating the layer can correction be appropriate. More explicit words fix some failures; authority, procedure, or reciprocal participation fix others.
