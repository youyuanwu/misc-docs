Promising, Ordering, and Questioning

Promises, orders, and questions illustrate three ways speech directs action and inquiry. A promise commits the speaker; an order attempts to place a requirement on an addressee; a question requests or organizes information. Their success depends on content, force, authority, intention, convention, and uptake.

These acts cannot be analysed as assertions with unusual propositions. They have different directions of fit and create different normative statuses.

Promising

In promising to do A, a speaker undertakes a commitment to perform A.

Typical felicity conditions include:

  • A is a future act of the speaker;
  • the hearer has an interest in A or the promise is otherwise relevant;
  • A would not obviously occur anyway;
  • the speaker is able to undertake the commitment;
  • the utterance counts as such an undertaking.

Sincerity requires intention to perform, but an insincere promise can still create an obligation. Failure to fulfil is breach, not proof that no promise was made.

Promise and prediction

Compare:

I will return tomorrow.

It can be:

  • a prediction;
  • a promise;
  • a threat;
  • a statement of schedule.

Propositional content is similar; force differs. A promise has world-to-word direction of fit: the speaker commits to making the world conform.

Context, relationship, expectation, and explicit performatives distinguish readings.

Authority to promise

A speaker can normally bind themselves but not another person:

I promise that Ben will attend.

This may promise to ensure attendance or merely misuse the form. Normative power is limited by control and standing.

Collective and institutional promises raise questions about representation. An authorized agent can bind an organization; an unauthorized member cannot.

Ordering

An order directs an addressee to act. Its felicity depends on:

  • identifiable action;
  • relevant authority or standing;
  • ability of the addressee;
  • appropriate circumstances;
  • recognition of directive force.

Without authority, identical words may be a request, demand, threat, or attempted order.

Authority can be legal, institutional, role-based, or locally granted. Force is not created by imperative mood alone.

Orders, requests, and advice

All have world-to-word direction of fit but differ in normative relation.

  • Order: claims authority and purports to require.
  • Request: presents an action as desired while leaving recognized choice.
  • Advice: recommends for the addressee's interests.
  • Suggestion: weakly proposes an option.

Politeness can disguise force, and social context can make an apparent request practically coercive. Linguistic classification and ethical legitimacy remain separate.

Consent and permission

Permission removes or suspends a prohibition; consent authorizes an act within a normative relationship.

Successful consent may require:

  • competence;
  • adequate information;
  • voluntariness;
  • appropriate scope;
  • recognizable expression;
  • continuing validity.

Words alone are not sufficient when these conditions fail. Nor must every consent be verbal; conduct can communicate under established conventions.

The philosophical point here is structural: speech acts can alter normative positions only where background practices grant that power.

Questions

A question raises an issue and requests or invites an answer.

Types include:

  • polar: "Did Ada leave?";
  • constituent: "Who left?";
  • alternative: "Did Ada or Ben leave?";
  • rhetorical;
  • examination;
  • deliberative.

Formal semantics may represent a question as a set of possible answers or a partition of possibilities. Speech-act theory adds force: asking makes the issue conversationally salient and can create an expectation of response.

Answerhood

An answer can be:

  • complete or partial;
  • direct or indirect;
  • true or false;
  • relevant or evasive.

To "Who attended?", "Ada did" is a direct partial answer; "The room was crowded" may be relevant without resolving the issue.

Question-under-discussion frameworks explain how questions structure alternatives and relevance.

Questioning and authority

Questions can request information without authority, but institutional settings create obligations to answer. Courts, examinations, interviews, and forms assign roles.

A question can also presuppose controversial content:

When did you stop falsifying records?

The addressee can reject the presupposition rather than choose an answer.

Direction of fit clarifies differences but does not fully individuate acts. Promise and threat share direction while differing in practical relation.

Multiple acts

One utterance can perform several acts:

If you submit today, I will review it tomorrow.

This can assert a conditional, promise review, advise prompt action, and indirectly request submission.

Speech-act decomposition should not assume one force per sentence. Some acts are primary, others indirect or consequential.

Assessment

Promises, orders, and questions show why language is norm-governed action:

  • content specifies what is undertaken or requested;
  • force determines the act;
  • authority and convention make normative change possible;
  • sincerity affects quality but not always existence of the act;
  • uptake distinguishes recognition from compliance;
  • institutions extend individual capacities.

Formal semantics can model contents and alternatives; it does not by itself explain who becomes committed, entitled, or obligated through speaking.
