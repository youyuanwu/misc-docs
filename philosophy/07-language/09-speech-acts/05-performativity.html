Performativity and Social Constitution

Some utterances do not merely represent an existing institutional fact; under suitable conditions they help create one. Naming, marrying, sentencing, resigning, voting, and contracting can be performed through words because established practices assign normative consequences to recognized forms.

Performativity concerns this capacity of signs and acts to constitute statuses. It depends on repeatable conventions, authority, context, and uptake. Speaker intention matters, but private intention alone cannot create an institutional fact.

Explicit performatives

An explicit performative contains a first-person present-tense act verb:

I promise to pay.

I apologize.

I resign.

Under ordinary conditions, saying the words performs the named act. The utterance can also describe itself, but its central function is practical.

The formula is neither necessary nor sufficient. A promise can be made without "I promise," and an actor on stage can utter the formula without making a real promise.

Institutional facts

Institutional acts depend on constitutive rules of the form:

X counts as Y in context C.

A marked ballot counts as a vote; an authorized pronouncement counts as a verdict. Language helps represent and execute the rule.

The physical event alone does not create the status. The same sound outside the practice lacks its institutional effect.

Authority

Declarations require standing. An unauthorized spectator cannot adjourn a court or name someone else's child by using the correct words.

Authority can derive from:

  • office;
  • delegated role;
  • ownership;
  • consent;
  • procedure;
  • recognized participation.

The conditions can be contested, but the linguistic point is that force depends on a normative background not encoded in the sentence alone.

Iterability and citation

A performative formula must be repeatable across occasions. Its conventional force depends on recognizable citation of prior forms.

The same repeatability permits:

  • theatrical quotation;
  • parody;
  • rehearsal;
  • forgery;
  • failed or unauthorized use.

Context determines whether a token participates in the institution or merely cites its language. Repeatability therefore enables both successful performance and detachment from ordinary force.

Intention and convention

Intention is often necessary:

  • a promise requires undertaking;
  • an apology normally requires presenting regret;
  • a vote requires intentional participation.

But convention constrains what intention can achieve. Privately intending "I object" to create a marriage does not make it a marriage formula.

Conversely, some highly formal acts can succeed despite reluctance or hidden insincerity if procedure and outward intention are adequate.

Performed identity and stance

Repeated linguistic practices can present and sustain roles, relationships, and stances. Address forms, titles, registers, and self-descriptions position speakers within social interaction.

This is not the claim that words create every underlying fact. A linguistic act can constitute an institutional status or public stance while depending on bodies, histories, and practices it does not create.

The roadmap keeps broader political and social theory outside this section; the relevant point is the mechanism by which repeatable signs acquire constitutive force.

Misfire and abuse

Performatives fail in different ways:

  • no accepted procedure;
  • wrong participants;
  • defective execution;
  • missing authority;
  • insincerity;
  • later breach.

An unauthorized declaration may be void. An insincere promise may be valid but abused. A breached promise was still made.

These distinctions prevent all defects from being treated as absence of the act.

Retrospective contest

Participants can dispute whether a performative succeeded:

  • Was consent informed?
  • Was the official authorized?
  • Was the formula complete?
  • Was the act coerced?
  • Did all required parties receive it?

Such disputes concern facts and norms constituting the procedure. They show that institutional reality can be determinate, vague, or contested depending on the rule.

Assessment

Performativity requires a layered explanation:

  1. meaningful form;
  2. conventional procedure;
  3. appropriate context;
  4. authority or standing;
  5. recognizable intention;
  6. execution and uptake;
  7. institutional consequence.

Language is constitutive where practices make saying count as doing. This is neither magic nor mere description: repeatable signs operate within normative structures that participants maintain.
