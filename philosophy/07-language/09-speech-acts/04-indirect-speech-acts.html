Indirect Speech Acts

"Can you open the window?" is grammatically a question about ability but ordinarily a request. "It is cold in here" can request that a window be closed. Indirect speech acts occur when one act is performed by way of another.

Their interpretation depends on sentence meaning, conventional associations, context, speaker intention, politeness, and practical reasoning. They show why force cannot be read directly from grammatical mood.

Primary and secondary acts

In:

Can you pass the salt?

the speaker performs:

  • a literal question about ability;
  • a primary request to pass the salt.

The audience recognizes the request because the ability question is normally obvious and relevant as a preparatory condition for requesting.

Indirectness does not require the literal act to be insincere. The hearer can answer "Yes" while passing the salt, acknowledging both.

Gricean derivation

A simplified inference:

  1. The speaker asks whether the hearer can do A.
  2. Ability is relevant to requesting A.
  3. Mere curiosity about ability is unlikely in context.
  4. The speaker intends the hearer to recognize a request.
  5. Therefore the question indirectly performs the request.

The derivation uses conventional content and conversational rationality. It need not be consciously computed.

Conventionalized indirectness

Some forms are highly conventional:

  • Can you A?
  • Would you mind A?
  • I would like you to A.
  • Why don't you A?

They are interpreted rapidly as requests while retaining traces of literal structure.

This suggests a continuum:

  • novel contextual hint;
  • productive indirect pattern;
  • conventionalized force marker;
  • idiomatic formula.

The boundary between pragmatics and construction meaning can shift historically.

Politeness

Indirectness can protect social options:

  • it avoids presuming compliance;
  • acknowledges ability or willingness;
  • permits refusal without explicit confrontation;
  • reduces direct imposition.

Politeness is not merely ornamental. It shapes why one form is selected and how force is calibrated.

Yet a formally indirect utterance can function as a strong command where authority and context make refusal costly. Grammatical softness does not determine practical force.

Hints

The rubbish bin is full.

can indirectly request that someone empty it. Unlike conventional request questions, the inference depends heavily on household roles, current goals, and shared knowledge.

Hints create deniability:

I was only mentioning it.

This can facilitate tact or manipulation. Communicative success still depends on recognizable intention, though the speaker may intentionally leave force less explicit.

Indirect refusals and answers

Asked:

Are you coming to dinner?

a reply:

I have an early meeting.

can refuse without saying "no." The inference uses relevance and background knowledge.

Indirect answers can be complete for conversational purposes while failing to answer the literal question semantically. QUD structure explains why they count as responsive.

Cross-linguistic and cultural variation

Languages and communities differ in:

  • conventional request forms;
  • honorifics;
  • acceptable directness;
  • role of intonation;
  • assumptions about entitlement;
  • interpretation of silence.

One community's polite request can sound evasive or imperative in another. A theory should distinguish universal intention-recognition capacities from local conventions.

Multiple force

Indirect acts need not replace literal acts. An utterance can simultaneously question, request, warn, and display politeness.

This supports multidimensional representations:

⟨content, literal force, primary force, social stance⟩.

Not every dimension belongs to semantics, but all can matter to interpretation.

Assessment

Indirect speech acts arise through interaction of:

  1. grammatical mood and literal content;
  2. conventional force associations;
  3. preparatory conditions;
  4. context and shared goals;
  5. recognizable intention;
  6. politeness and social relation.

No single inference template covers every case. Conventionalized requests and novel hints lie at different points between code and reasoning.
