Doing Things with Words

Uttering a sentence can change the world without describing it. A speaker can promise, order, apologize, name, warn, ask, consent, or pronounce a verdict. These acts depend on meaningful words, but their success also depends on authority, convention, circumstance, intention, and audience uptake.

Austin's theory of speech acts begins by challenging the contrast between factual statements and meaningless or merely expressive utterances. It develops distinctions among locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts. Searle systematizes illocutionary force through rules, direction of fit, and classifications.

Constatives and performatives

A constative appears to describe and be evaluated as true or false:

The meeting has begun.

An explicit performative appears to perform the act it names:

I promise to return.

I apologize.

I name this ship Endeavour.

The performative is not merely a report that the act occurs. Under suitable conditions, uttering it is part of performing the act.

Austin eventually rejects a sharp division. Assertions too are acts, and performatives can misfire or be assessed for truth-related commitments. The important contrast is among dimensions of an utterance, not two classes of sentence.

Locution, illocution, and perlocution

The locutionary act is producing meaningful words with a certain sense and reference.

The illocutionary act is what is done in saying them: asserting, promising, warning, ordering, asking.

The perlocutionary act is what is achieved by saying them: persuading, frightening, amusing, causing someone to leave.

One locution can carry different forces:

The dog is outside.

It can assert, warn, remind, or reassure. One illocution can have different effects: a warning may frighten one listener and reassure another.

Force and effect must therefore be separated.

Felicity conditions

Speech acts can misfire when conditions are absent.

For a conventional procedure:

  • an accepted procedure must exist;
  • persons and circumstances must be appropriate;
  • the procedure must be executed correctly and completely;
  • relevant participants must have required intentions or commitments;
  • subsequent conduct may need to conform.

A person lacking authority cannot pronounce a legal verdict merely by using the judge's words. A promise about an action outside the speaker's control may be defective. An insincere promise can be successfully made yet abused.

Austin distinguishes failure to perform the act from successful performance followed by insincerity or breach.

Force indicators

Illocutionary force can be indicated by:

  • grammatical mood;
  • performative verbs;
  • word order;
  • intonation;
  • particles;
  • institutional setting;
  • gesture;
  • conversational position.

No indicator works alone. An interrogative can function as a request; a declarative as an order; an explicit "I promise" as theatrical quotation rather than a real promise.

Force is conventionally and contextually determined.

Searle's rules

Searle analyses acts through several kinds of condition.

For promising:

  • propositional-content condition: the content concerns a future act by the speaker;
  • preparatory conditions: the hearer wants the act, and it is not obvious it would occur anyway;
  • sincerity condition: the speaker intends to perform it;
  • essential condition: the utterance counts as undertaking an obligation.

The essential condition identifies the act's conventional point. Sincerity is not required for successful but insincere performance.

This framework generalizes to requests, assertions, questions, and declarations.

Direction of fit

Speech acts differ in how words and world are supposed to align.

  • Word-to-world: assertions aim to fit words to how things are.
  • World-to-word: orders and promises aim to make the world fit the words.
  • Double direction: declarations alter institutional reality by representing it as altered.
  • Null direction: expressives such as thanks or apologies primarily express an attitude toward a presupposed situation.

Direction of fit clarifies why falsity is central to assertion but non-fulfilment is central to promises.

Searle's classification

Five broad families:

  • assertives: commit the speaker to truth;
  • directives: attempt to get the hearer to act;
  • commissives: commit the speaker to future action;
  • expressives: express an attitude;
  • declarations: bring about an institutional state through authorized utterance.

The taxonomy is useful but not exhaustive. An utterance can perform several acts, and categories overlap. A warning asserts danger and directs precaution; an offer creates a conditional commitment.

Convention and intention

Some speech acts appear highly conventional: verdicts, baptisms, resignations. Others, such as hints and warnings, can be performed without a fixed formula.

Intention-based theories emphasize recognizable communicative purposes. Conventional theories emphasize rules making an utterance count as an act.

A hybrid distinguishes:

  • communicative acts whose force is inferred from intention;
  • conventional acts whose success depends on established procedure;
  • institutional acts requiring authority;
  • indirect acts exploiting both sentence form and inference.

No one mechanism explains every case.

Uptake

Uptake is an audience's recognition of force and content. If a warning is heard as a joke, has the speaker successfully warned?

For some acts, intended and publicly available force may suffice despite misunderstanding. For others, uptake is partly constitutive: consent, agreement, and conversational proposals depend on recognition.

Uptake should be distinguished from compliance. A hearer can recognize an order and refuse it.

The varieties of failure are developed in Uptake and Speech-Act Failure.

Speech acts and truth-conditions

Knowing the proposition expressed does not determine force:

⟨Force,p⟩.

The same p can be asserted, questioned, supposed, or commanded in suitable form.

Force does not eliminate truth-conditional content. Assertions, warnings, and promises often contain propositions whose truth or satisfaction matters. Formal semantics and speech-act theory model different dimensions.

Assessment

Speech-act theory establishes that linguistic meaning participates in action. A full account must identify:

  1. locutionary content;
  2. illocutionary force;
  3. felicity conditions;
  4. speaker intention;
  5. convention and authority;
  6. audience uptake;
  7. perlocutionary effect.

Collapsing these dimensions obscures both success and failure. Words can be meaningful without performing the intended act, an act can be performed insincerely, and a successful act can fail to produce its intended effect.
