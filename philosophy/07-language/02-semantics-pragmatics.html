Semantics and Pragmatics

An utterance normally communicates more than a dictionary and grammar appear to put into its sentence. If someone says "Some of the guests arrived," listeners often infer that not all arrived. If a person at a dinner table asks "Can you pass the salt?", the utterance normally functions as a request rather than an inquiry into muscular capacity. If someone says "She is ready," the content remains incomplete until the conversation identifies both the person and what she is ready for.

These cases motivate a distinction between semantics, the relatively stable contribution made by linguistic expressions and their arrangement, and pragmatics, the contribution of context, communicative intentions, shared assumptions, and inference. The distinction is indispensable, but its boundary is contested. Some context dependence is conventionally encoded; some is freely inferred; and disputed cases lie between.

This page supplies a working map rather than a dogmatic partition. The following page compares broader theories of meaning. Dedicated treatments develop speaker meaning, conversational implicature, presupposition, context and conversation, and the disputed boundary.

From expression to communication

At least four notions of meaning should be separated.

Expression meaning is the conventional contribution associated with a word or construction in a language. The English word "and" differs from "or" even before either appears in a particular utterance.

Sentence meaning is the structured result of combining expression meanings according to syntax. An ambiguous sentence may have more than one such result.

Utterance content is what a sentence says on an occasion after context resolves relevant parameters. An utterance of "I am tired" concerns its speaker, not every possible user of "I."

Speaker meaning is what a speaker overtly intends an audience to recognize. It may include, but can exceed or depart from, utterance content. Sarcasm, hints, indirect requests, and deliberate ambiguity rely on this gap.

A fifth category, communicative effect, concerns what an utterance actually causes. A warning may fail to warn, an accidental remark may offend, and a misunderstood instruction may still alter behaviour. Effect is neither semantic content nor intention.

A provisional distinction

A useful starting point is:

Semantics studies conventional and compositionally determined content; pragmatics studies how interpreters use context and communicative reasoning to recover or enrich what a speaker conveys.

This formulation is provisional for three reasons.

First, semantics itself can be context-sensitive. Words such as "I," "here," and "today" conventionally instruct an interpreter to retrieve a speaker, place, or time from context. Contextual input does not automatically make a phenomenon pragmatic.

Second, not every stable contribution affects truth-conditions. A conventional marker might indicate tone, evidential status, or discourse structure. Semantics cannot be identified without remainder with truth-conditional content.

Third, pragmatic processes can appear necessary to obtain any complete proposition. "It is raining" is normally understood as raining at a contextually relevant place, although English contains no overt place expression. Whether the place is an unpronounced semantic variable or a pragmatic enrichment is a central dispute.

The boundary should therefore be drawn by explanatory role, not by the slogan that semantics is context-free and pragmatics is contextual.

Encoded context sensitivity

Indexicals provide the clearest case. A simple framework distinguishes character from content:

  • the character of "I" is a rule that maps a context to its speaker;
  • the content of "I" in a particular context is that speaker.

The expression has a stable linguistic rule even though its referent varies. Similar rules apply, with complications, to "here," "now," "today," demonstratives such as "that," and tense.

The distinction also separates the context of utterance from the circumstance of evaluation. "I am in Paris" as uttered by Ada in London concerns Ada because the utterance context fixes the speaker. It can then be evaluated relative to different possible circumstances when embedded under modality: Ada might have been in Paris. Changing the possible world of evaluation does not change who "I" referred to in the original utterance.

Not all context-sensitive expressions behave as mechanically as "I." A demonstrative may depend on attention, gesture, salience, or speaker intention. Gradable adjectives such as "tall" require a comparison class and standard. Quantifiers often range over a contextually restricted domain. These cases ask how much contextual structure grammar encodes.

Conversational implicature

Suppose a teacher says:

Some students passed.

Listeners commonly infer:

Not all students passed.

Yet the original claim is logically compatible with every student passing. The extra message is a standard example of conversational implicature. On a Gricean account, hearers assume that the speaker is cooperating and has made an appropriately informative contribution. If the speaker knew all passed, saying only "some" would be unexpected; hearers infer that the stronger claim is not available.

Several diagnostics support a pragmatic treatment:

  • cancellability: "Some students passed - in fact, all did" is not contradictory;
  • reinforceability: "Some, but not all, passed" can state explicitly what was otherwise implied;
  • context dependence: where only some students were expected to pass, the same words may not carry the same inference;
  • calculability: the inference can be reconstructed from sentence content, alternatives, context, and conversational expectations.

These are tendencies rather than infallible tests. Conventional implications can sometimes be denied metalinguistically, and familiar pragmatic inferences can become regular enough to look encoded. Evidence must come from patterns across constructions and contexts. The tests and scalar case are developed in Conversational Implicature.

Presupposition

Consider:

Sam stopped smoking.

The sentence normally presents Sam's prior smoking as background. That information tends to survive under several operators:

  • Sam did not stop smoking.
  • Did Sam stop smoking?
  • Perhaps Sam stopped smoking.

This projection distinguishes the background assumption from the main asserted content. If listeners did not already accept that Sam smoked, they may accommodate it by adding it to the conversational background.

Presupposition straddles the semantics-pragmatics boundary. A semantic theory may give a trigger special definedness conditions or a partial truth value. A pragmatic theory may explain the effect through expectations about common ground and speaker commitment. Dynamic theories model sentence meanings as updates to an information state, allowing semantic composition and conversational change to interact.

No one diagnostic settles the classification. Projection can be filtered:

If Sam used to smoke, then he has stopped smoking.

Here the antecedent supplies the background information, so the whole conditional need not presuppose it. Any adequate account must explain both persistence and filtering; see Presupposition.

Speech acts and force

Sentences do not merely describe. Speakers assert, ask, command, promise, apologize, name, warn, and consent. Illocutionary force is the kind of act performed in uttering meaningful words.

Grammatical mood and force often align:

  • declarative form with assertion;
  • interrogative form with questioning;
  • imperative form with ordering or requesting.

But the alignment is not identity. "Can you open the window?" is grammatically interrogative and literally concerns ability, yet convention and context normally make it an indirect request. "You will submit the form today" can function as a prediction, command, or threat. Identifying truth-conditional content does not by itself identify the act.

Speech acts also depend on standing and circumstances. A promise requires an undertaking; a verdict requires institutional authority; a warning requires an appropriate danger and communicative orientation. Uptake may matter to whether an act succeeds. These are pragmatic and normative conditions, although they can become conventional features of constructions. The full framework begins with Doing Things with Words, followed by Assertion and Its Norms and Uptake and Speech-Act Failure.

Three positions on the boundary

Semantic minimalism holds that a sentence, after values are supplied for a relatively short list of grammatically controlled context-sensitive elements, expresses a minimal proposition. Further enrichment belongs to communication rather than semantics. The view protects a stable semantic core but may produce contents far removed from what speakers intuitively say.

Indexicalism allows more hidden or overt variables in logical form. Apparent free enrichment is semantic because context supplies values to grammatically represented slots. The challenge is to provide evidence for the proposed variables rather than adding one whenever content appears incomplete.

Contextualism allows pragmatic processes to help determine truth-conditional content even without a syntactically represented variable. The view tracks ordinary judgments about what was said but risks weakening compositional constraints and making semantic content difficult to distinguish from everything conveyed.

The dispute is not merely terminological. It concerns what speakers know when they know a language, which aspects of interpretation grammar constrains, how communication succeeds, and which regularities a formal theory should encode. The competing views are assessed in The Semantics-Pragmatics Boundary.

A layered working model

Until individual phenomena force revision, the section uses the following sequence:

graph LR
  E[Expression meanings] --> C[Compositional sentence meaning]
  C --> X[Contextually determined content]
  X --> F[Illocutionary force]
  X --> I[Pragmatically inferred content]
  F --> U[Audience uptake and effects]
  I --> U

The arrows do not imply a strictly serial psychological process. They mark explanatory dependencies. Conventional meanings constrain composition; context helps determine what is said; force and inference shape what is communicated; uptake and effects depend on an audience.

The model also leaves room for feedback. Expectations about a speech act can guide disambiguation, and discourse context changes after each contribution. Later pages will refine these interactions. For now, its main value is negative: it prevents truth-conditions, speaker intentions, force, implications, and effects from collapsing into one undifferentiated notion of meaning.
