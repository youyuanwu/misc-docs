Radical Interpretation

How could an interpreter identify another speaker's meanings and beliefs without first possessing a dictionary? Davidson's radical interpretation begins with observable patterns of assent, circumstances, action, and shared environment. Meaning and belief must be assigned together.

A compositional truth theory supplies interpretations for indefinitely many sentences. Principles of charity and triangulation constrain the theory so that it explains speakers as largely coherent and responsive to common causes.

The interdependence of belief and meaning

Assent to "Gavagai is nearby" can reflect both what the sentence means and what the speaker believes. If the sentence is translated as "A rabbit is nearby," dissent might show different belief; if translated differently, it may show semantic difference.

Neither factor can be fixed first without using the other. Interpretation seeks the best joint assignment:

⟨meanings,beliefs⟩↦intelligible pattern of speech and action.

This holism distinguishes radical interpretation from decoding an already known code.

Truth theory

Davidson proposes a finitely axiomatized Tarskian theory that entails interpretive T-sentences:

“Snow is white” is true iff snow is white.

The recursive theory models compositional competence. Isolated materially true biconditionals are insufficient; the whole theory must fit use and derive correct interpretations systematically.

Truth-Conditional Semantics develops the formal program.

Charity

The interpreter favours assignments making speakers:

  • broadly consistent;
  • mostly correct about salient surroundings;
  • responsive to evidence;
  • capable of rational action;
  • in agreement on enough background to locate disagreement.

Charity is not politeness or a claim of infallibility. Without substantial shared truth, there is too little basis for identifying the speaker's words as beliefs about a common world.

Principle of humanity

A more permissive principle interprets others as reasoning as we might reasonably expect given their circumstances, history, and values. It allows systematic differences without classifying them immediately as irrational.

Charity emphasizes truth and coherence; humanity emphasizes intelligibility from the agent's point of view. Interpretation may balance both.

Triangulation

Two creatures responding similarly are not yet using concepts. Davidson's triangulation involves:

  1. one speaker;
  2. another interpreter or speaker;
  3. a shared worldly cause.

Each correlates the other's responses with common objects. Public correction and joint attention locate the source of error and establish objective reference.

Triangulation connects language, social interaction, and world without reducing meaning to bare causal covariance.

Error and disagreement

If charity makes speakers mostly correct, can it accommodate radical error? Local error is identified against a background of agreement. Systematic scientific or cultural differences can be interpreted where enough practical and observational structure is shared.

The controversial claim is that total error about everything is unintelligible. A belief system must be mostly connected to its causes and norms to count as a belief system.

Critics argue this defines deep disagreement away or makes truth too dependent on interpretability.

Circularity

Interpretation uses semantic notions to explain semantic notions:

  • truth theories require translation in the metalanguage;
  • charity presupposes judgments of truth;
  • identifying shared causes presupposes object individuation;
  • rationality standards require interpretation.

Davidson's response is holistic rather than reductive. Meaning, belief, truth, and action are mutually constrained; none is reduced independently to non-intentional facts.

This is informative if the total pattern sharply restricts acceptable theories. It is empty if any behaviour can be rationalized after the fact.

Indeterminacy

More than one truth theory may fit all evidence. Davidson can treat differences without explanatory consequences as not genuine differences in meaning.

This response is less sceptical than Quine's. Charity, shared causes, and rationality provide constraints beyond stimulus dispositions. Whether they yield unique reference is considered in Reference Indeterminacy.

Assessment

Radical interpretation establishes that assigning meaning is inseparable from treating speakers as rational agents in a shared world. Its strengths are compositionality, public evidence, and integration of belief with action.

Its pressures are:

  • idealizing rationality;
  • culturally local standards;
  • circularity;
  • multiple fitting theories;
  • difficulty with systematic error.

The method is strongest as a constitutive constraint on interpretation, not an algorithm guaranteed to produce one translation manual.
