Translation and Conceptual Schemes

Translation seeks expressions in one language that preserve content, force, and use from another. Exact equivalence is often unavailable: words divide semantic space differently, grammar requires distinctions another language leaves optional, and texts depend on historical associations.

Difficulty does not immediately imply untranslatability or radically different conceptual schemes. Translation can preserve some dimensions while explaining losses in others.

What should translation preserve?

Candidates include:

  • reference;
  • truth-conditions;
  • inferential role;
  • cognitive significance;
  • presupposition;
  • tone and register;
  • illocutionary force;
  • literary form;
  • practical effect.

No translation maximizes all simultaneously. Technical translation prioritizes reference and inference; poetry may prioritize rhythm and association.

Translation quality is purpose-relative without being arbitrary.

Equivalence

Two expressions can be equivalent at different levels:

  • extensionally co-referential;
  • truth-conditionally equivalent;
  • synonymous;
  • pragmatically interchangeable;
  • functionally equivalent in a genre.

Replacing one by another may preserve truth while losing mode of presentation or social meaning. A translator should state the intended equivalence.

Loss and compensation

Languages grammaticalize different information: evidential source, gender, aspect, honorific relation, definiteness, or social stance.

A translation can:

  • make implicit information explicit;
  • omit a distinction;
  • use paraphrase;
  • add a note;
  • compensate elsewhere;
  • preserve ambiguity rather than resolve it.

Loss in one dimension does not show that the source is unintelligible.

Incommensurability

Scientific paradigms or cultures may organize concepts differently enough that sentence-by-sentence translation distorts their roles. Incommensurability can mean:

  • no exact synonymy;
  • different standards of evidence;
  • different taxonomies;
  • no neutral comparison vocabulary;
  • partial failure of reference continuity.

These claims vary in strength. Lack of perfect translation is ordinary; impossibility of rational comparison is much stronger.

Conceptual schemes

A conceptual scheme is supposed to organize otherwise neutral experiential content. Different schemes may classify one world in incompatible ways.

Davidson challenges the scheme-content dualism. To identify a scheme as a language, we must interpret it; interpretation requires substantial shared truth and rationality. A wholly untranslatable language cannot be recognized as expressing a radically different scheme.

The conclusion targets total untranslatability, not local conceptual difference.

Davidson's argument

If two schemes are largely translatable, their difference is local and describable. If not translatable at all, there is no basis for identifying the noises as assertions with contents radically unlike ours.

Thus the metaphor of schemes organizing an uninterpreted content becomes unstable:

  • interpreted content is already conceptually articulated;
  • uninterpreted content cannot serve as a common comparison object.

Critics reply that partial interaction, practice, and causal coordination can establish languagehood before full translation.

Reference across theory change

Historical translation asks whether old scientific terms refer to current entities. "Mass," "planet," and "gene" changed theoretical roles.

Continuity can depend on:

  • causal contact;
  • preserved samples;
  • explanatory role;
  • partial inferential overlap;
  • retrospective reconstruction.

Translation may be neither identity nor failure. A term can preserve reference while changing sense, or split into several later concepts.

Hermeneutic translation

Translation across historical horizons requires reconstructing questions and practices, not substituting words mechanically. The interpreter moves between local passages and the wider form of life.

This can expose presuppositions in both languages. It should not authorize unconstrained appropriation; textual and historical evidence remain constraints.

Machine translation

Statistical and neural systems can produce fluent cross-language text through learned patterns. Performance demonstrates substantial structural regularity without by itself settling whether the system possesses human-like understanding.

Evaluation should separate:

  • adequacy of output;
  • preservation of reference and force;
  • robustness to context;
  • calibration under ambiguity;
  • internal representation;
  • agency and communicative intention.

Translation quality is measurable even when metaphysical questions about understanding remain open.

Assessment

Translation is best treated as constrained plural optimization rather than perfect substitution. It can preserve truth while losing tone, preserve function while changing form, or explain a source distinction through paraphrase.

Radical conceptual difference remains possible locally. The stronger claim of a wholly untranslatable language is difficult to state, because evidence that something is a language already supports some interpretation. The practical task is to map both equivalences and irreducible differences without treating either as total.
