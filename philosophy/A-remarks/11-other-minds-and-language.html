Other Minds and Language

The problem of other minds is often presented as an inference across an unbridgeable gap. I encounter my own mind directly, encounter only the bodies of others, and must infer an invisible mental life from visible behaviour. Since the behaviour might occur without the mental life, the inference appears permanently insecure.

The private-language argument challenges the semantic picture presupposed by this construction. If psychological concepts acquired their meanings from objects available only to one subject, it would be unclear how those concepts could have standards of correct application, including standards for reidentifying the subject's own states. Language about pain, thought, and consciousness is instead learned and exercised within practices that already include first-person expression, third-person attribution, correction, concealment, testimony, and response.

This does not prove that every apparent person has a mind. It changes the philosophical burden. Ordinary uncertainty about particular people remains, as do difficult questions about animals, impaired patients, and artificial systems. What is threatened is the global sceptical demand for a proof of all other minds from premises that contain no psychological criteria. Under a Wittgensteinian analysis, that demand is not answered by a stronger inference; it is dissolved by rejecting the conception of meaning and privacy that made the inference seem necessary.

The scope of this dissolution must be stated carefully. A semantic argument can expose an unstable formulation without deciding every metaphysical question about consciousness. In particular, it does not establish behaviourism, eliminate experience, or by itself refute every truth-conditional account on which an undetectable mindless duplicate is intelligible.

1. Four distinctions

The word private can mark several different claims:

ClaimContentConsequence
Experiential privacyOnly the subject undergoes this subject's pain in the first-person wayMy knowing that you hurt is not my having your hurt
Epistemic asymmetrySelf-ascription and other-ascription normally have different grounds"I am in pain" is not ordinarily inferred from my behaviour
Semantic privacyThe meaning and correctness of a sign are fixed solely by what only one speaker can identifyNo one else could in principle understand or correct the sign
Practical secrecyA public language or code is concealed from an audienceThe language remains learnable and rule-governed in principle

The private-language argument targets semantic privacy. It does not infer from the failure of semantically private rules that experiential privacy is unreal. A secret code, an idiolect, or the record of a solitary speaker is not private in the relevant sense if its signs remain governed by repeatable procedures.

The contrast between public and private is also normative rather than merely sociological. Public does not mean that another person is currently watching. It means that correctness is not exhausted by whatever presently seems right to the user. A solitary calculator can follow an established algorithm because intermediate results, records, and later checks can count against present inclination. A sign whose only standard is the user's current impression has no corresponding distinction between correctness and apparent correctness.

Two further distinctions structure the other-minds problem:

  1. The conceptual question asks what it means to attribute a mental state to another subject.
  2. The epistemological question asks what justifies a particular attribution.

A third, metaphysical question asks what mental states are and whether a physical or functional duplicate without experience is possible. An account of meaning can constrain that question without settling it.

2. The private-language argument

The proposed language

Wittgenstein asks whether there could be a language whose words refer to immediate private sensations and can be understood only by the person who has them. This is stronger than saying that sensations are privately undergone. The proposal makes a private item perform the foundational semantic work: the speaker identifies it inwardly, associates a sign with it, and later uses that item as the standard for correct application.

The canonical diarist resolves to write "S" whenever a certain sensation occurs. On the first occasion, the diarist concentrates attention on the sensation and inwardly associates the sign with it. On a later occasion, the diarist must decide whether the same sensation has returned.

The difficulty can be reconstructed as follows:

  1. A rule-governed sign must permit a distinction between applying the sign correctly and merely taking oneself to have applied it correctly.
  2. In the proposed case, the only criterion for whether the present sensation is S is that it seems to the diarist to match the remembered sample.
  3. The remembered sample cannot independently adjudicate the judgment, because what counts as remembering it correctly is itself fixed by the same present seeming.
  4. Therefore the proposal has not supplied a standard by which correct applications of "S" differ from apparent applications.

This is not the crude claim that memory is always unreliable or that a second observer must certify every use. Public language also relies on memory, and communities can be mistaken. The problem is the proposed identity of correctness with an impression of correctness. Fallible checking is possible only where something can count as checking rather than as another unchallengeable impression.

Why inner ostension is insufficient

Pointing does not determine its own interpretation. When someone points to a red square and says "red," the gesture alone does not determine whether the word names the colour, shape, material, location, or an individual object. Public ostension succeeds against a background of learned attention, classification, correction, and expected continuation.

An inward act of attention does not remove this underdetermination. Concentrating on a sensation while writing "S" does not establish:

  • which feature of the occasion the sign records;
  • what degree of similarity is sufficient for recurrence;
  • whether duration, intensity, location, or qualitative character is relevant;
  • how a later apparent memory is to be assessed.

The private object therefore cannot, merely by being privately attended to, make its own interpretation determinate.

The beetle

Wittgenstein's beetle comparison imagines each person possessing a box whose contents only that person can inspect. If the public use of beetle continues without comparison among the boxes, whatever is inside cannot determine the word's shared role. The boxes need not be empty. The point is that an inaccessible object cannot perform the semantic function assigned to it.

Applied to sensation-language, this yields a limited conclusion:

private sample+inner ostension⇒self-sufficient standard of meaning.

It does not yield:

public standards of meaning⇒no privately undergone experience.

The first implication concerns the conditions for rule-governed representation. The second would be an unsupported ontological conclusion.

3. The reflexivity of the analysis

The expressions experience, private, and privately undergone are themselves parts of public language. The analysis cannot step outside every linguistic practice, inspect an unconceptualized private object, and compare that object with the public concept of experience. If "the private experience" were introduced as something independently identifiable only beyond all public criteria, the analysis would reproduce the model that the private-language argument contests.

A more consistent account treats experiential privacy as an internal distinction within public psychological discourse. The practice distinguishes:

  • undergoing pain from observing pain behaviour;
  • avowing one's pain from attributing pain to another;
  • feeling another person's pain from knowing that the person is in pain;
  • expressing a state from concealing or pretending it;
  • knowing the presence of pain from knowing its exact qualitative character.

These distinctions are public: speakers can learn, contest, and apply them. What they classify is asymmetric: only the subject occupies the first-person role for that subject's episode. There is no contradiction in a public practice containing subject-relative positions.

Consequently, the safest formulation is not:

A public concept represents a separately identified private object.

It is:

Public psychological language contains an irreducible grammatical and epistemic asymmetry between first-person expression and third-person attribution.

This formulation neither reduces an experience to its linguistic role nor pretends to describe its nature from outside every possible mode of description. It states how experience enters discourse and marks the limit of what semantic analysis alone can establish.

4. How the traditional problem is constructed

The strongest sceptical problem combines a private-object semantics with an observational model of knowledge:

  1. In my own case, I identify a private inner item and call it pain or consciousness.
  2. Other people present only publicly observable bodies, utterances, and movements.
  3. Those public facts are contingently correlated with the private item in my case.
  4. I project the item into other cases by analogy.
  5. The correlation might fail everywhere outside my case.
  6. Therefore I cannot know whether any other mind exists.

The argument contains both a semantic and an epistemic gap. Semantically, it assumes that the psychological concept is complete before it is applied to another person. Epistemically, it treats another person's expression as neutral movement from which an invisible cause must be inferred.

The two gaps reinforce one another. The more completely the concept is grounded in my inaccessible item, the less clear it becomes how "the same state in another" is determinate. The more another person's body is stripped of psychological significance, the more attribution looks like a hazardous projection.

The resulting scepticism is global. It is not the ordinary possibility that one person is pretending, that a patient is unresponsive despite being conscious, or that a machine produces misleading reports. It asks whether every apparent case other than one's own could lack mentality while all publicly available facts remain fixed.

5. Applying the private-language argument

The concept is not completed in isolation

Pain-language is learned amid injury, crying, withdrawal, protection, comfort, testimony, treatment, concealment, and recovery. The practice includes both "I am in pain" and "she is in pain." First- and third-person applications are not two independently constructed languages later joined by an analogical hypothesis.

This point reverses the traditional order of explanation:

private sample→self-concept→analogical projection

is replaced by:

shared practice→{first-person avowal,third-person attribution.​

The two branches remain asymmetric. A person normally avows pain by undergoing and expressing it, not by observing that person's own behaviour. Another person relies on expression, circumstances, testimony, history, and sometimes physiology. Different routes of application do not by themselves create two meanings of pain.

Criteria are not symptoms or definitions

Wittgensteinian accounts commonly distinguish criteria from merely inductive symptoms. Smoke may be a symptom of fire because the two are contingently correlated. By contrast, crying after injury, protecting a wound, accepting relief, and reporting pain belong to the circumstances through which the concept of pain is learned and applied.

Calling these features criteria does not entail:

pain=overt pain behaviour.

Pretence, stoicism, paralysis, confusion, and deception are intelligible within the practice. No isolated movement is necessary or sufficient in every case. Criteria are defeasible standards embedded in a structured pattern, not a mechanical checklist. Their defeasibility permits error; their conceptual role prevents every application from being an inference from semantically neutral motion.

The difference matters. If a person writhes after an injury, we do not normally first identify bodily geometry and then infer an occult object behind it. We see suffering in the expression. Reflection, unusual cases, and conflicting evidence can turn this recognition into an explicit inference, but inferential reconstruction is not the foundation of every ordinary attribution.

Knowing is not undergoing

The sceptical demand also equivocates between two standards:

  1. knowing that another person is in pain;
  2. undergoing that person's pain as that person does.

The second is not the ideal form of the first. It is a different relation. Requiring first-person acquaintance for third-person knowledge would make other-mind knowledge impossible by definition, not show it to be unsupported.

Likewise, knowing that someone is in pain need not settle:

  • exactly how intense the pain feels;
  • whether its qualitative character matches one's own;
  • whether every self-description is accurate;
  • which physical or functional property constitutes it.

The presence, type, and exact qualitative character of an experience impose different evidential demands. Failure to establish the strongest claim does not erase warrant for the weaker ones.

6. What dissolution amounts to

A philosophical problem is solved when its question is accepted and an answer is established. It is dissolved when analysis shows that the question depends on a misapplication of its concepts or on requirements that cannot coherently be imposed. The Wittgensteinian treatment aims at the latter.

The global demand loses its foundation

The traditional sceptic demands a proof that moves from facts described without any psychological significance to the existence of wholly private mental objects. But the concepts required to state the conclusion were not acquired from such a neutral base. They already operate through expressive and interpersonal practices.

The demand is therefore unstable:

  • If expression, testimony, context, and responsiveness count as psychological criteria, other-mind attribution is not a leap from a completely mindless description.
  • If no possible public fact is allowed to count, the sceptic has detached the alleged contrast from the standards through which mind and consciousness are applied.
  • If first-person access is required for knowledge, the impossibility of knowing others has been stipulated rather than discovered.

Universal doubt also borrows contrasts from local doubt. We understand pretending because most expression is not treated as pretence; concealment because there is something to conceal; misattribution because attribution is ordinarily answerable to criteria. Extending a possibility of error to every case does not automatically preserve the conditions that made the error-description intelligible.

The conclusion is not:

It is certain that every apparent person is conscious.

It is:

The absence of first-person access to another is not a general defeater of other-mind knowledge, because first-person access was never the criterion governing third-person attribution.

The proposition "only I am conscious"

"Only I am conscious" has at least two readings.

On an empirical reading, conscious retains its ordinary interpersonal criteria, and the speaker claims that every other apparent case is misleading. The claim is meaningful, but it owes an account of why all convergent criteria should be rejected. Merely repeating that the speaker lacks first-person access to others supplies no such account.

On a private-definitional reading, conscious means whatever is available in the speaker's uniquely immediate way. Then the word applies only to the speaker by stipulation. "Only I am conscious" no longer reports a discovery; it restates the definition, and the ordinary contrast expressed by only has been lost.

The dilemma does not deductively refute solipsism. It distinguishes an evidentially unsupported hypothesis from a verbal construction that cannot perform the metaphysical work assigned to it.

The body is not a screen

On the rejected picture, the body is an external screen behind which a mind is either present or absent. In ordinary psychological understanding, a living body is also the medium in which attention, hesitation, pleasure, grief, and pain are expressed.

This is not the claim that every appearance is veridical. It is the claim that appearance and expression cannot first be deprived of all psychological content and then required to reconstruct that content by inference. Other people enter the language-game as minded participants, not as physical objects to which mentality is added only after a philosophical proof.

7. What remains after dissolution

The dissolution is neither total nor theory-neutral.

Local epistemology

Particular attributions remain fallible. Someone may pretend, suppress expression, misreport, confabulate, or be unable to respond. Evidence can conflict. These cases require ordinary epistemic methods:

  • testimony and its credibility;
  • expressive behaviour in context;
  • biological and causal similarity;
  • longitudinal patterns of action and learning;
  • intervention and physiological evidence;
  • inference to the best explanation.

The general problem of other minds therefore becomes most substantial at the boundaries of established practice rather than as a universal doubt about familiar persons.

Unfamiliar minds

Animals, severely impaired patients, unfamiliar forms of life, and artificial systems can separate criteria that coincide in ordinary adult humans. Fluent report may occur without shared biology; biological similarity may occur without report; responsiveness may be blocked despite preserved awareness.

The private-language argument does not decide these cases. It requires that proposed extensions or revisions of conscious have standards of application, but it does not dictate which biological, functional, behavioural, or organizational standards are correct. A new case may require conceptual development rather than mechanical application of an old checklist.

Qualitative sameness

Evidence that another person is in pain can be strong while evidence that the pain feels exactly like one's own remains weak. The following questions must not be collapsed:

  1. Is any experience present?
  2. What general kind of experience is it?
  3. Is its precise qualitative character the same as mine?

An inverted-spectrum hypothesis concerns the third question and does not by itself undermine the first. Public criteria may underdetermine exact qualitative identity without making all attribution of experience unintelligible.

Metaphysical zombies

A realist truth-conditional theory may hold that "she is conscious" has a determinate truth value even if no available evidence decides it. On such an account, a physical or functional duplicate without experience may remain semantically describable. Whether it is metaphysically possible depends on further arguments about consciousness, modality, and reference.

Meaning-as-use places pressure on a contrast that can make no difference to any practice or judgment. It does not follow without additional premises that use exhausts the property referred to. The semantic diagnosis therefore blocks an easy route to the zombie hypothesis; it does not by itself prove physicalism or logical behaviourism.

Acknowledgment

The demand for certainty can also obscure the practical relation involved. Responding to another person's suffering is not merely accepting a theoretical proposition about an interior object. It is acknowledging a claim made upon us by another person. Scepticism can persist as a refusal of acknowledgment even after its general epistemological argument has been diagnosed.

This point prevents dissolution from becoming indifference. The absence of a deductive proof does not cancel responsibility, and semantic clarification does not replace the ethical task of responding to another.

8. Objections and limits

"Public criteria are only evidence for a private reality"

A critic may accept public criteria as evidence while denying that they contribute to meaning. The mental fact, on this view, is wholly distinct from every criterion and can vary while all criteria remain fixed.

The private-language argument asks how the critic identifies the relevant property and its recurrence if every public and inferential role is excluded. But this challenge is not automatically decisive. Causal or externalist theories may allow reference to outrun a speaker's identifying descriptions. The dispute then concerns the theory of reference, not merely the existence of experience.

"The account is behaviourism"

It is not, unless criteria are equated with necessary and sufficient behavioural conditions. A criterial account can distinguish the state from its expression and allow concealment, pretence, incapacity, and error. Its thesis is that expression and circumstances belong to the grammar of attribution, not that an experience is a set of movements.

"The argument presupposes other minds"

Public language is learned among apparent speakers, so using that fact as a deductive proof of their consciousness would beg the question against a radical sceptic. The Wittgensteinian response should not be presented as such a proof. Its claim is diagnostic: the sceptic's psychological vocabulary derives its standards from the very interpersonal practices that the sceptical picture redescribes as semantically neutral.

A sceptic can continue to say that all apparent teachers were mindless automata. The question is then what contrast this hypothesis preserves, what evidence could bear on it, and what explanatory role it plays. The burden shifts; it is not eliminated by fiat.

"All talk of privacy is public and therefore contradictory"

The fact that private experience is a public expression does not make its application contradictory. Public rules can encode subject-relative roles. The contradiction arises only if the expression is required both to participate in public standards and to derive its entire meaning from something excluded in principle from every such standard.

The analysis therefore does not compare public language with an independently described, wholly extra-linguistic object. It clarifies distinctions internal to the practice and refuses to convert those distinctions into an unsupported two-realm ontology.

9. Verdict

ProblemWittgensteinian result
How can a sign obtain meaning solely from a private sample?The proposed sample does not supply an independent standard of correct use
How can public language concern first-person experience?One public practice contains asymmetric first- and third-person applications
Is pain identical with pain behaviour?No; criteria for attribution are not an ontological reduction
Can another person's state be known without being undergone?Yes; undergoing and knowing are different relations
Can a particular attribution be mistaken?Yes; criteria are defeasible and evidence can conflict
Can all other people be doubted at once?The unrestricted doubt destabilizes the criteria and contrasts it borrows
Has the existence of other minds been proved?No; the demand for a proof has been diagnosed rather than satisfied
Are zombies or inverted qualia thereby impossible?Not by semantic analysis alone

The problem of other minds therefore divides. Its local epistemological form survives: difficult cases require evidence, theory, and judgment. Its global sceptical form is substantially dissolved: it depends on treating mental concepts as names privately fixed in one case and treating embodied expression as external evidence for a hidden realm. Once psychological language is understood as an interpersonal, rule-governed practice with asymmetric positions, the absence of another person's first-person access is no longer a missing proof.

The final lesson is methodological. Privacy of undergoing does not entail privacy of meaning; publicity of meaning does not entail publicity of undergoing; and neither semantic thesis alone settles the metaphysics of consciousness. The dissolution works by keeping those claims apart.
